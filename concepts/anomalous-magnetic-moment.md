# Anomalous Magnetic Moment

The anomalous magnetic moment is defined as the difference between a particle's experimentally measured magnetic moment and the value predicted by the [[concepts/diracs-equation|Dirac equation]] for relativistic spin-1/2 particles. While the Dirac equation predicts a [[concepts/g-factor|g-factor]] of exactly 2 for particles such as electrons and muons, high-[[concepts/accuracy|precision]] [[concepts/experimental-evidence|experimental data]] consistently reveals slight deviations from this theoretical baseline. This discrepancy indicates that the simple Dirac model is incomplete, as it does not account for quantum field effects.

In quantum electrodynamics (QED), these deviations arise from the particle's interaction with virtual particles in the vacuum. The particle constantly emits and reabsorbs virtual photons, and may briefly fluctuate into virtual particle-antiparticle pairs. These quantum fluctuations modify the effective magnetic moment, resulting in a g-factor slightly greater than 2. The magnitude of this anomaly is calculated using perturbation theory, with higher-order loop diagrams contributing increasingly smaller corrections.

Precision measurements of the anomalous magnetic moment serve as stringent tests of the Standard Model of particle physics. Any significant deviation between theoretical predictions and experimental results could indicate the existence of new, undiscovered particles or forces. The [[concepts/muon|muon]], being approximately 200 times heavier than the electron, is particularly sensitive to contributions from heavy virtual particles, making it an ideal candidate for probing physics beyond the Standard Model.

Recent experimental efforts have focused on the [[concepts/muon-g-2-experiment|Muon g-2 experiment]] at Fermilab to reduce uncertainties in these measurements. By storing muons in a highly uniform magnetic field and precisely measuring their precession frequency, researchers aim to determine the muon's anomalous magnetic moment with unprecedented accuracy. Comparing these results with theoretical calculations involving hadronic vacuum polarization remains a central challenge in confirming or refuting potential new physics.
