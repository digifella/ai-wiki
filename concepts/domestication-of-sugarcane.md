# Domestication Of Sugarcane

Sugarcane (*Saccharum officinarum*) originated in the humid tropical regions of New Guinea and the adjacent Pacific islands, where wild varieties thrived in natural ecosystems. Indigenous populations in this region initiated the domestication process approximately 8000 BCE, engaging in early agricultural practices that involved the selective [[concepts/agriculture|cultivation]] of the plant. Through generations of careful selection, these communities gradually modified the crop to produce larger stalks and significantly higher [[concepts/sugar|sugar]] concentrations, transforming it from a wild forage into a reliable agricultural staple.

The spread of domesticated sugarcane from its Pacific origins was facilitated by ancient maritime trade routes and human migration. It moved westward into Southeast Asia and [[entities/india|India]], where it was further refined for juice extraction and [[concepts/crystal-formation|crystallization]]. By the early centuries CE, sugarcane had become a major economic commodity in the Indian Ocean trade network, with techniques for processing sugar into solid forms developing in India before spreading to the Middle East and North Africa.

The global expansion of sugarcane cultivation accelerated during the Age of Exploration, as European powers established plantations in the Caribbean, Brazil, and other tropical colonies. This era marked a profound shift in the crop’s socio-economic role, [[concepts/linking|linking]] its production directly to the [[concepts/transatlantic-slave-trade|transatlantic slave trade]]. The demand for sugar as a luxury good turned into a mass-market commodity, driving the forced migration of millions of enslaved Africans to labor on plantations, thereby embedding sugarcane production within the structures of [[concepts/spanish-conquests|colonial exploitation]] and global capitalism.
