# Naval Blockades

A naval blockade is a military operation in which a naval force restricts or prevents maritime traffic to and from a geographic area, typically a port, coastline, or entire nation. By controlling sea lanes and strategic chokepoints, blockading forces deny an adversary access to seaborne trade, military supplies, and economic resources. Naval blockades are recognized under international law as acts of war and have been employed throughout modern military history as a means of economic coercion and strategic pressure.

## Historical Development

Naval blockades emerged as a significant strategic tool during the Age of Sail, where controlling trade routes was essential for national survival. The practice was formalized in international law during the 19th and early 20th centuries, particularly through the Declaration of Paris (1856) and the Hague Conventions, which sought to regulate the rights and duties of neutral powers during wartime. Notable historical applications include the British blockade of Germany during World War I, which aimed to starve the Central Powers of essential materials, and the Allied blockade of Axis powers in World War II, which severely impacted their industrial capacity.

## Modern Applications and Legal Context

In contemporary military strategy, blockades are often integrated with anti-access and area denial (A2/AD) capabilities to project power and constrain adversary movement. Recent geopolitical tensions, such as those involving Iran, have highlighted the use of naval blockades or blockade-like operations in strategic chokepoints like the Strait of Hormuz. These scenarios demonstrate how modern navies utilize surveillance, mine warfare, and surface combatants to enforce restrictions without necessarily engaging in direct fleet-on-fleet combat. The legal status of such operations remains complex, with international bodies often debating the proportionality and humanitarian impact of strict maritime embargoes.
