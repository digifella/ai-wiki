# Naval Blockades

A naval blockade is a military operation in which a naval force restricts or prevents maritime traffic to and from a geographic area, typically a port, coastline, or entire nation. By controlling sea lanes and strategic chokepoints, blockading forces deny an adversary access to seaborne trade, military supplies, and economic resources. This strategy serves as a primary mechanism for economic coercion and strategic pressure, aiming to weaken an opponent's war-fighting capability without immediate large-scale amphibious invasion.

The legal framework surrounding blockades is defined by international law, particularly the 1909 London Declaration concerning the Laws of Naval War, which requires a blockade to be declared and maintained by a force sufficient to really prevent access to the coast of an enemy. Under these regulations, a blockade is only considered effective if it is physically enforced; mere declaration without actual enforcement does not constitute a legal blockade. Neutral vessels attempting to breach an effective blockade may be captured, and the blockade must be applied impartially to all nations to remain lawful.

In modern military doctrine, naval blockades are often integrated with anti-access and area denial (A2/AD) strategies to project power and constrain adversary mobility. Contemporary applications, such as those observed in conflict scenarios involving Iran, utilize a combination of surface combatants, submarines, and aerial surveillance to monitor and interdict maritime traffic. These operations aim to isolate target regions economically and militarily, leveraging control over critical maritime chokepoints to exert strategic leverage without escalating to full-scale naval warfare.
