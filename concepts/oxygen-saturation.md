# Oxygen Saturation

During the Carboniferous and Permian periods, spanning approximately 360 to 250 million years ago, Earth's atmospheric composition differed markedly from modern standards. Atmospheric oxygen concentrations peaked at roughly 35% during the Carboniferous, a significant increase from the contemporary baseline of 21%. This hyperoxic environment was primarily driven by the proliferation of vast tropical swamps and dense forests. These ecosystems facilitated high rates of photosynthesis, which released oxygen into the atmosphere while simultaneously burying organic material that would eventually form fossil fuel deposits.

The formation of these extensive peat bogs prevented the complete decomposition of plant matter, effectively sequestering carbon and allowing oxygen levels to rise. As the Permian period progressed, tectonic shifts and climatic changes altered these swampy environments, leading to a gradual decline in oxygen saturation. The burial of organic carbon during this era not only shaped the atmospheric balance but also established the geological foundations for the world's major coal reserves, linking ancient biological processes directly to the planet's long-term geochemical history.
