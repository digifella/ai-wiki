# Extraterrestrial Intelligence

Extraterrestrial intelligence (ETI) refers to the hypothetical existence of conscious, intelligent life forms beyond Earth. The scientific study of this concept encompasses astrobiology, the search for biosignatures in exoplanet atmospheres, and the statistical evaluation of conditions necessary for life elsewhere in the universe. While no confirmed detection of extraterrestrial intelligence has occurred, the sheer scale of the [[concepts/observable-universe|observable universe]]—containing billions of galaxies with billions of [[concepts/stellar-objects|stars]] each—has prompted serious [[concepts/scientific-method|scientific inquiry]] into the probability of life arising independently on other worlds.

## Communication Challenges and Universal Language

A central problem in ETI research is determining how humans might communicate with [[concepts/extraterrestrial-life|alien life]] should [[entities/contact|contact]] occur. Traditional spoken or written language would be meaningless without shared context. Proposed solutions rely on universal principles presumed to transcend biological and cultural differences: [[concepts/mathematics|mathematics]], [[concepts/physics|physics]], and chemical composition. These frameworks have informed actual attempts [[concepts/assistive-technology|at]] interstellar messaging, such as the Arecibo Message of 1974, which encoded mathematical and biological information in radio waves. The underlying assumption is that any technological civilization capable of receiving such messages would recognize mathematical and physical principles as fundamentally comprehensible.

## Scientific Methods and Frameworks

The [[concepts/seti|Search for Extraterrestrial Intelligence]] (SETI) employs radio telescopes to scan for technological signals from space, while complementary approaches examine exoplanet atmospheres for chemical signatures inconsistent with purely geological processes. The Drake Equation, formulated in 1961, attempts to estimate the number of communicative civilizations in the galaxy by multiplying factors such as star formation rates and the fraction of planets harboring intelligent life. Such frameworks remain speculative due to the vast uncertainties involved, particularly regarding the probability of abiogenesis and the longevity of technological civilizations.
