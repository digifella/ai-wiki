# Feynman's Three Step Scientific Method

[[concepts/feynman|Feynman]]'s three-step [[concepts/scientific-method|scientific method]] is a simplified framework for the empirical investigation of natural phenomena. The approach was articulated by physicist Richard Feynman and distills the scientific process into three essential stages: making an initial guess, computing predictions from that guess, and comparing the results against observations from [[entities/nature|nature]].

## The Three Steps

The first step involves formulating an educated guess or hypothesis about how a system works. This guess need not be correct; it serves as a starting point for investigation. The second step requires deriving mathematical or logical predictions that follow from the proposed guess. These predictions must be specific enough to be testable. The third step involves comparing these predictions against empirical observations and experimental data from the natural world. If predictions match observations, the hypothesis gains support; if they diverge significantly, the hypothesis must be refined or rejected.

## Application and Significance

This method emphasizes the importance of both theoretical [[concepts/reasoning|reasoning]] and [[concepts/experimental-validation|experimental validation]] in science. By structuring the scientific process around concrete comparison with nature, Feynman's framework highlights that no hypothesis, regardless of its logical elegance or mathematical sophistication, can be considered reliable unless it accurately predicts real-world behavior. The iterative nature of the process—refining guesses based on observed discrepancies—forms the foundation of scientific progress.
