String theory is a theoretical framework in physics that proposes the fundamental constituents of reality are not point-like particles, but rather one-dimensional objects called strings. These strings are extraordinarily small, with a length estimated at roughly $10^{-35}$ meters, and they vibrate in various patterns through space and time. The different vibrational modes of these strings correspond to the different particles and forces observed in nature, such as electrons, photons, and quarks.

The theory emerged in the late 1960s as an attempt to describe the strong nuclear force, but it was later recognized as a potential candidate for a theory of quantum gravity. It requires the existence of extra spatial dimensions beyond the familiar three, typically totaling ten or eleven dimensions depending on the specific variant of the theory. These additional dimensions are compactified or curled up at such small scales that they are not directly observable in current experiments.

There are five consistent superstring theories in ten dimensions: Type I, Type IIA, Type IIB, Heterotic-O, and Heterotic-E. In the mid-1990s, Edward Witten proposed M-theory, which unifies these five theories and a previously known eleven-dimensional supergravity theory. M-theory suggests that strings are actually one-dimensional slices of higher-dimensional objects known as branes, providing a broader context for understanding the relationships between different string formulations.

Despite its mathematical elegance and potential to unify all fundamental forces, string theory remains largely unverified experimentally. The energy scales required to probe the string scale are far beyond the reach of current particle accelerators. Consequently, the theory is primarily studied for its internal consistency and its ability to resolve theoretical conflicts between quantum mechanics and general relativity, rather than for direct empirical confirmation.
