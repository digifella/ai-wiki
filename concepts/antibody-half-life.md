# Antibody Half Life

Antibody half-life refers to the time required for the concentration of antibodies in the bloodstream to decrease to half its original level. This measurement is fundamental to immunology and clinical medicine, as it determines how long antibodies remain effective at neutralizing pathogens or providing immune [[concepts/secure|protection]]. The decline in antibody concentration follows first-order kinetics, meaning the rate of decrease is proportional to the current concentration, resulting in an exponential decay pattern rather than linear reduction.

## Variation Among Antibody Classes

The half-life of antibodies varies significantly depending on the immunoglobulin class. Immunoglobulin G (IgG) typically exhibits the longest half-life, ranging from approximately 21 to 28 days in humans, which allows for sustained [[concepts/long-term-immunity|long-term immunity]]. In [[concepts/contrast|contrast]], other classes such as Immunoglobulin M (IgM) and Immunoglobulin A (IgA) have substantially shorter half-lives, often measured in days, reflecting their roles in immediate or mucosal responses rather than prolonged systemic protection.

## Biological Determinants

Several biological factors influence antibody [[concepts/data-persistence|persistence]], including the specific subclass of the antibody, the route of administration, and individual host physiology. The neonatal Fc receptor (FcRn) plays a critical role in recycling IgG, thereby extending its circulation time. Additionally, the affinity of the antibody for its target antigen can affect its stability; high-affinity binding may shield the antibody from degradation, effectively prolonging its functional half-life within the body.
