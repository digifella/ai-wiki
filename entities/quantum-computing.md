# Quantum Computing

[[concepts/quantum-computing|Quantum computing]] represents a fundamentally different approach to computation compared to [[concepts/classical-computers|classical computers]]. Rather than using [[concepts/classical-bits|bits]] that exist as either 0 or 1, quantum computers use [[concepts/qubits|quantum bits]] (qubits) that can exist in superposition, allowing them to process multiple states simultaneously. This capability enables quantum computers to solve certain classes of problems exponentially faster than classical computers, particularly in areas like optimization, drug discovery, and materials [[concepts/science|science]].

## Key Principles

Quantum computers operate on the principles of superposition and entanglement. Superposition allows qubits to exist in multiple states at once until measured, while entanglement creates correlations between qubits that enable complex computational [[concepts/relationships|relationships]]. These properties allow quantum [[concepts/algorithms|algorithms]] to explore [[concepts/solution|solution]] spaces more efficiently than classical approaches for specific problem types.

## Current State and Limitations

Quantum computers remain largely in the experimental and early development [[concepts/phase|phase]]. Current systems are limited by factors including qubit decoherence, error rates, and the small number of qubits available in operational machines. Most practical quantum computers today have between dozens and a few hundred qubits, well below the thousands or millions that would be needed for many theoretical applications.

## Post-Quantum Security Concerns

The potential power of quantum computers poses a threat to current cryptographic systems, as quantum algorithms could [[entities/theoretically-media|theoretically]] break widely-used [[concepts/encryption-methods|encryption methods]] like RSA. This has prompted development of [[concepts/post-quantum-cryptography|post-quantum cryptography]] standards, including [[concepts/encryption-algorithms|lattice-based cryptography]], which is designed to resist attacks from both classical and quantum computers. Organizations are beginning to assess and plan transitions to quantum-resistant encryption methods.
