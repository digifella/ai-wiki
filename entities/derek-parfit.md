# Derek Parfit

Derek Parfit (1942–2017) was a British philosopher widely regarded as one of the most significant moral philosophers of the late twentieth century. He spent the majority of his academic career at Oxford University, where he developed a rigorous analytical approach that profoundly influenced contemporary debates in ethics, metaphysics, and personal identity. His work is characterized by novel arguments that challenged traditional views on the nature of the self and moral obligation.

Parfit’s contributions to ethical theory centered on consequentialism and the concept of wellbeing. He argued for a form of impersonal consequentialism, suggesting that the rightness of an action depends on its outcomes rather than the identity of the agents involved. This perspective led him to explore the implications of population ethics and the non-identity problem, questioning how we can make moral judgments about future generations whose existence depends on our current choices.

In the realm of personal identity, Parfit famously argued that the continuity of the self is less important than psychological connectedness and continuity. He proposed that personal identity is not what matters in survival, but rather the relations typically associated with survival, such as memory and intention. This view challenged the traditional notion of a persistent, indivisible self and had far-reaching implications for how individuals understand their own existence and moral responsibilities.

His work on wellbeing has been referenced in various contemporary discussions, including Kate Laffan's TEDxLSE talk on defining wellbeing. Parfit’s analysis of what makes life go well for a person helped shape modern understandings of welfare, distinguishing between hedonic, desire-fulfillment, and objective list theories of wellbeing. His legacy continues to influence philosophical inquiry into the nature of value, identity, and the good life.
