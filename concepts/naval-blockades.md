# Naval Blockades

A naval blockade is a military operation in which a naval force restricts or prevents maritime traffic to and from a geographic area, typically a port, coastline, or entire nation. By controlling sea lanes and strategic chokepoints, blockading forces deny an adversary access to seaborne trade, military supplies, and economic resources. Naval blockades are recognized under international law as acts of war and have been employed throughout modern military history as a means of economic coercion and strategic pressure.

## Historical Development

Naval blockades emerged as a significant military strategy during the age of sail, when control of maritime routes became crucial to national power. The British blockade of Napoleonic France (1803–1815) demonstrated the effectiveness of sustained naval pressure in weakening an opponent's economy and military capacity. During the American Civil War, the Union's Anaconda Plan employed blockades of Confederate ports to strangle the Southern economy. These historical examples established blockades as a recognized tool of statecraft, though their application has evolved with modern naval technology and international law.

## Contemporary Applications

Modern naval blockades often employ anti-access and area denial (A2/AD) strategies, which use a combination of maritime forces, surveillance systems, and weapons to prevent an adversary from operating in contested waters. The Iran conflict scenario illustrates contemporary blockade dynamics, where control of vital shipping routes—particularly through the Strait of Hormuz—carries significant geopolitical weight. Modern blockades face legal and diplomatic constraints under international maritime law, particularly regarding distinctions between blockades targeting military assets versus those affecting civilian populations.
