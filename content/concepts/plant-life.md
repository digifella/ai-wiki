# Plant Life

During the [[concepts/geologic-epochs|Carboniferous and Permian periods]] (approximately 360 to 250 million years ago), vast swamps and forests covered much of Earth's landmass. The decomposition and burial of this plant material, along with marine organisms, formed the organic deposits that would eventually become fossil fuels such as coal, oil, and natural gas. This process fundamentally altered Earth's atmospheric composition.

## Atmospheric Changes

As plant material accumulated and was buried under sediment, [[concepts/oxygen-saturation|atmospheric oxygen levels]] rose significantly during the Carboniferous period, reaching concentrations higher than the present day. This increase reflected the net effect of photosynthesis exceeding respiration and decomposition. Conversely, [[concepts/carbon-dioxide-levels|carbon dioxide levels]] declined as carbon was sequestered in the growing biomass and subsequently locked away in geological strata. These shifts in atmospheric chemistry had cascading effects on climate, ocean chemistry, and the evolution of [[concepts/terrestrial-life|terrestrial life]].

The eventual decline of these vast forests and the transition into the Permian period saw a reversal of some trends, with changing climate conditions and the rise of different plant and animal groups reshaping atmospheric dynamics. The fossil fuel deposits formed during this era represent a long-term carbon sequestration event that shaped modern atmospheric conditions.
