# Lookback Time

**Lookback time** is the time elapsed between the emission of [[concepts/light|light]] from a distant object and its reception by an observer. It represents how far back in time we are looking when observing distant celestial bodies. Due to the finite [[concepts/speed|speed]] of light ($c$), observing distant objects is equivalent to observing the past.

## Key Concepts

- **Relation to Distance**: In a static universe, lookback time would directly correspond to distance ($d = ct$). However, because the universe is expanding, the relationship is more complex. The distance light has traveled (light-travel distance) is less than the current [[concepts/proper-distance|proper distance]] to the object.
- **[[concepts/galaxy-redshift|Cosmological Redshift]]**: As light travels through expanding space, its wavelength stretches, resulting in redshift ($z$). Higher redshift generally correlates with greater lookback time.
- **[[concepts/observable-universe|Observable Universe]] Limit**: The maximum lookback time is approximately 13.8 billion years, corresponding to the [[concepts/cosmic-microwave-background]] (CMB), the oldest light we can observe.

## Integration with Universe Expansion

The concept of lookback time is critical for resolving apparent paradoxes regarding the size and [[concepts/universe-age|age of the universe]].

- **Age vs. Size Paradox**: While the universe is ~13.8 billion years old, the observable universe has a current radius of ~46.5 billion light-years (diameter ~93 billion light-years). This discrepancy arises because space itself has expanded during the time the light was traveling.
- **Current Understanding**: Recent analyses clarify that while light has traveled for 13.8 billion years (lookback time), the source of that light has moved much farther away due to metric expansion.
