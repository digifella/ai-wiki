# Infinite Monkey Theorem

The [[concepts/crested-macaques|Infinite Monkey Theorem]] is a thought experiment in probability [[concepts/theory|theory]] that asks: if a monkey were given a typewriter and infinite time, would it eventually produce the complete works of Shakespeare by random key presses? Mathematically, the answer is yes—given truly infinite time and random selection, any finite text has a non-zero probability of being generated, and infinite attempts guarantee eventual success. The theorem demonstrates a counterintuitive principle: sufficiently large or infinite possibilities can make the improbable become inevitable.

## Probability and Practical Reality

While theoretically sound, the practical timescales involved are staggering. For a monkey to randomly type even a short Shakespeare sonnet (roughly 2,000 characters) would take an expected timeframe vastly longer than the current age of the universe. The calculation reveals why random generation is useless as a method for producing meaningful text—probability guarantees eventual success only in the limit, not in any practically relevant timeframe. This distinction between theoretical possibility and practical feasibility is central to understanding the theorem's real significance.

## Mathematical and Cryptographic Relevance

The Infinite Monkey Theorem relates to broader principles in information theory and [[concepts/cryptography|cryptography]]. It illustrates why exhaustive key searches, while theoretically guaranteed to succeed, are computationally infeasible against modern encryption with sufficiently large key spaces. The theorem underpins arguments about entropy, randomness, and the astronomical improbability required to break well-designed cryptographic systems through brute force. It remains a useful pedagogical tool for discussing the relationship between mathematical certainty and practical security.
