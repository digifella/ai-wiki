# Seasoning Timing

Salt is a fundamental seasoning tool for home cooks, and its effective use depends on understanding different types and their [[concepts/material-properties|physical properties]]. The most common culinary salts—table salt, kosher salt, and sea salt—differ in crystal size and density, which affects how they measure and dissolve. Table salt has fine, compact crystals and is denser, meaning a teaspoon contains more salt by weight than the same volume of kosher salt. Sea salt and kosher salt have larger, irregular crystals with more air space between them, requiring different measurement conversions when substituting between types.

## Application and Dissolution

The timing and method of [[concepts/seasoning-techniques|salt application]] significantly impact both flavor development and [[concepts/texture|texture]]. Adding salt early in cooking allows it to dissolve fully and distribute evenly throughout a dish, enhancing flavors [[concepts/assistive-technology|at]] a chemical level. Salt dissolves more readily in liquids and at higher temperatures, making it ideal to add during the cooking process for soups, stews, and sauces. For finishing dishes, coarser salts applied just before serving provide distinct bursts of flavor and textural [[concepts/contrast|contrast]] that fine salt cannot replicate.

## Practical Technique

Home cooks benefit from adjusting their salt application based on cooking stage and desired outcome. During initial cooking, dissolved salt seasons food from within and aids in protein breakdown and texture development. Intermediate tasting and adjustment prevents over-salting, particularly important since salt concentration intensifies as liquids reduce. A final pinch of finishing salt—often a fleur de [[concepts/social-emotional-learning|sel]] or similar premium variety—adds complexity and textural interest to completed dishes without requiring dissolution.
