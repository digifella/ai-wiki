# Desalination Plants

[[concepts/mega-water-fabs|Desalination plants]] are industrial facilities that extract fresh water from salt water or brackish water through various separation technologies. These plants address [[concepts/water-scarcity|water scarcity]] in arid and semi-arid regions by converting seawater or inland saline sources into potable or irrigation water suitable for human consumption and industrial use. The process removes dissolved salts, minerals, and other impurities that [[entities/make|make]] source water unsuitable for most [[concepts/software|applications]].

## Technologies and Methods

The two dominant [[concepts/desalination|desalination]] technologies are [[concepts/desalination-technology|reverse osmosis]] (RO) and multi-effect distillation ([[concepts/multi-stage-flash-msf-technology|MSF]]). Reverse osmosis forces seawater through semi-permeable membranes under high pressure, separating fresh water from concentrated brine. Multi-effect distillation heats seawater to produce steam that condenses into fresh water across multiple stages, recovering waste heat to improve efficiency. Smaller-scale facilities may employ electrodialysis or other specialized methods depending on local conditions and water quality requirements.

## Energy and Economic Considerations

Desalination is energy-intensive, particularly [[concepts/msf-technology|MSF technology]], which requires substantial thermal input. This [[concepts/energy-demand|energy demand]] creates operational costs and environmental impacts that vary significantly based on local electricity sources. Reverse osmosis is generally more efficient but requires high-pressure infrastructure and membrane replacement. The economic viability of desalination depends on electricity prices, feedwater salinity, and proximity to markets for fresh water. Regions like the Arabian Peninsula rely heavily on desalination to meet freshwater demands in water-scarce environments.
