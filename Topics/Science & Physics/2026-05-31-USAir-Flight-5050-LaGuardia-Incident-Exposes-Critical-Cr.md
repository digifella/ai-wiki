## USAir Flight 5050: LaGuardia Incident Exposes Critical Crew and CRM Failures
**Clip title:** NOBODY Was In CONTROL!! | USAir Flight 5050
**Author / channel:** Mentour Pilot
**URL:** https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=pxGiKrdBiIo

### Summary
On the evening of September 20, 1989, [[entities/usair-flight-5050|USAir Flight 5050]], a [[entities/boeing-737-400|Boeing 737-400]], was preparing for departure from [[entities/laguardia-airport|LaGuardia Airport]] (KLGA) in New York. The airport was under significant strain due to adverse weather conditions, including low clouds and intermittent rain, combined with heavy traffic congestion. LaGuardia, an airport with limited expansion capabilities, amplified these pressures, leading to stacking delays throughout the day. Despite the aircraft itself being nearly new and in excellent mechanical condition, a series of overlooked human factors would tragically escalate what initially appeared to be minor issues into a full-blown disaster.

The crew consisted of a 36-year-old Captain and a 29-year-old First Officer. The Captain, recently upgraded two months prior, had limited command [[concepts/experience|experience]] on the 737, and his training records were described as "average," focusing on catastrophic events rather than compounding issues. The First Officer was making his first flight on a jet aircraft after completing his simulator training, having only 8.2 hours on the 737. Both pilots lacked formal [[concepts/crew-resource-management-crm|Crew Resource Management (CRM)]] training, which emphasizes [[concepts/communication|communication]] and crew [[concepts/coordination|coordination]]. Furthermore, the airline was undergoing a large merger between Piedmont Airlines and USAir, leading to potential discrepancies in operational procedures and company culture. Repeated flight changes and delays had visibly irritated the Captain, leading him to frequently leave the cockpit, leaving the inexperienced First Officer, who was designated as Pilot Flying for this leg, to manage boarding and pre-departure checks alone. Unbeknownst to the crew, the rudder trim switch was inadvertently pushed to a nearly full left position during boarding, which went unnoticed despite checklist procedures, as pilots typically associated "trim" with stabilizer trim. Adding another critical layer of risk, the Captain decided not to arm the auto-brake system for takeoff, based on common but incorrect assumptions about its function.

During the takeoff roll, as the First Officer advanced the throttles, he accidentally pressed the autothrottle disconnect switch instead of the Takeoff/Go-Around (TOGA) [[concepts/buttons|buttons]], resulting in insufficient and uneven [[concepts/engine-thrust|thrust]]. The Captain then manually advanced the throttles, but the left engine produced slightly less [[concepts/engine-thrust|thrust]] than the right, exacerbating a subtle left-pulling tendency already caused by the mis-set rudder trim. Further complicating the situation, both pilots were making conflicting steering inputs, with the First Officer applying right rudder and the Captain using the tiller, neither communicating their actions. The combined [[concepts/stress|stress]] on the nose wheel caused a tire to burst [[concepts/assistive-technology|at]] around 62 knots, introducing a loud bang and rumbling vibrations. As the aircraft accelerated past V1 (decision [[concepts/speed|speed]]), the Captain belatedly called out, "Let's take it back then," instead of the standard "Reject." This ambiguous call, coupled with the lack of explicit [[concepts/speed|speed]] callouts, led to critical seconds of confusion where both pilots partially relinquished control responsibility.

The aircraft ultimately ran off the end of the runway [[concepts/assistive-technology|at]] approximately 130 knots (faster than V1), shot through the approach lights, and slammed into a wooden pier [[concepts/structure|structure]] in Bowery Bay. The impact tore the aircraft into three main sections, with portions of the fuselage submerged in shallow water. Tragically, two passengers were killed due to crushing injuries from the pier [[concepts/structure|structure]] intruding into the cabin, and the Captain sustained injuries. However, due to the relatively low impact speed and the absence of fire, the crash was largely survivable for the remaining 61 occupants, who were promptly evacuated by the cabin crew and rescued by arriving emergency services. The subsequent investigation found that the accident was not due to mechanical failure or catastrophic weather, but primarily a result of critical failures in crew [[concepts/communication|communication]], [[concepts/coordination|coordination]], and [[concepts/decision-making|decision-making]]. This led to significant industry-wide changes, including the redesign of the 737 rudder trim switch, the widespread [[concepts/adoption|adoption]] of formal Crew Resource Management (CRM) training (mandated by the FAA by 1994), an increased emphasis on maximum performance rejected takeoff procedures (including explicit auto-brake use), and more conservative crew scheduling practices to avoid pairing inexperienced pilots.
