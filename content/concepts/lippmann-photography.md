# Lippmann Photography

Lippmann photography is a color photography process invented by Gabriel Lippmann in 1891 that captures color through interference patterns rather than pigments or dyes. The technique uses a single photographic plate coated with a fine-grain emulsion, which is exposed through the back against a mercury mirror. [[concepts/light|Light]] waves reflect off the mirror and interfere with the incoming light, creating standing wave patterns within the emulsion that correspond to different wavelengths. When these patterns are later illuminated, they reconstruct the original colors through the same interference mechanism.

## Structural Color and the Nature of the Image

The colors produced by Lippmann photography are not true pigmentary colors but rather examples of [[concepts/structural-color|structural color]]—the same phenomenon that creates iridescence in butterfly wings and oil films. The emulsion records the physical [[concepts/structure|structure]] of light itself rather than absorbing and reflecting specific wavelengths as conventional color photography does. This distinction is fundamental: a Lippmann photograph displays color through the arrangement of microscopic layers and their interaction with light, making the image highly dependent on viewing angle and lighting conditions.

## Historical Significance and Limitations

Although Lippmann won the Nobel Prize in [[concepts/physics|Physics]] in 1908 for his invention, the process never achieved commercial success. The photographs required extremely long exposures, precise laboratory conditions, and were difficult to view and reproduce. Modern color photography using chemical dyes proved far more practical, despite being based on a fundamentally different principle. Lippmann photography remains primarily of historical and scientific interest, demonstrating an alternative approach to capturing color that predates and differs significantly from standard photographic methods.
