# Volcanic Explosivity Index

The Volcanic Explosivity Index (VEI) is a systematic scale developed to quantify and compare the relative magnitude and explosivity of volcanic eruptions. Published in 1982 by geologists Walker, Crowell, and Lipman, the VEI provides a standardized framework for classifying eruptions based on measurable characteristics including eruption column height, volume of material ejected, and duration of the eruption. The scale ranges from 0 to 8, with each increment representing a tenfold increase in eruption magnitude.

## Classification and Characteristics

The VEI assigns numerical values to eruptions based on observed and estimated [[concepts/parameters|parameters]]. Lower values (0-2) represent effusive or mildly explosive eruptions with limited ash dispersal, while mid-range values (3-4) indicate significant explosive activity affecting regional areas. Higher values (5-8) represent catastrophic eruptions with global atmospheric effects, though eruptions rated 6 or above are rare in recorded history. The index also incorporates qualitative descriptions such as eruption style, ash column characteristics, and extent of impact zones.

## Application and Limitations

The VEI has become the primary tool for volcanologists and geologists to communicate eruption severity and compare historical and prehistoric events on a common scale. It enables assessment of volcanic hazards and helps inform risk management and climate modeling. However, the index has limitations: historical eruptions may lack precise data, and the scale is not linear in all measured parameters. Additionally, the VEI does not capture all aspects of eruption impact, such as lahars, lava flow extent, or specific hazards to nearby populations.
