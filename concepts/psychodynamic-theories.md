# Psychodynamic Theories

## Overview
Psychodynamic Theories constitute a class of psychological theories that emphasize the role of unconscious psychological processes, early childhood experiences, and internal conflicts in shaping personality and behavior. Rooted in Psychoanalysis developed by Sigmund Freud, these theories have evolved to include contributions from Anna Freud, Melanie Klein, Donald Winnicott, and Jungian Analysis.

### Core Tenets
- **Unconscious [[concepts/motivation|Motivation]]**: Behavior is driven by unconscious desires, fears, and memories.
- **Developmental Stages**: Personality develops through psychosexual or psychosocial stages; fixation at any stage affects adult behavior.
- **Defense [[concepts/causes|Mechanisms]]**: The ego employs strategies (e.g., Repression, Projection, Sublimation) to manage [[concepts/anxiety|anxiety]] from id-superego conflicts.
- **Transference/Countertransference**: Patients project feelings about [[concepts/significant-figures|significant figures]] onto the therapist; essential for therapeutic insight.
- **Therapeutic Alliance**: Emphasis on the relationship between therapist and patient to uncover repressed material.

## Major Schools & Figures
- **Classical Psychoanalysis**: Freud — structure of personality (Id, Ego, Superego); dream analysis.
- **Object Relations [[concepts/theory|Theory]]**: Klein, Winnicott — focus on internalized [[concepts/relationships|relationships]] with early caregivers rather than just drives.
- **Self [[concepts/psychology|Psychology]]**: Heinz Kohut — focus on the development of the self and narcissism.
- **Jungian Analysis**: [[entities/carl-jung]] — collective unconscious, archetypes, individuation.

## Critiques & Contemporary Relevance
While foundational, psychodynamic theory [[concepts/faces|faces]] criticism for:
- Lack of empirical falsifiability.
- Overemphasis on sexuality and early childhood.
- Length and cost of traditional analysis.

Modern [[concepts/adaptations|adaptations]] include brief psychodynamic therapy and integration with cognitive-behavioral frameworks. Critiques of diagnostic boundaries in developmental psychology, such as those regarding [[concepts/autism|autism]], highlight the tension between rigid categorical models and spectrum concepts.
