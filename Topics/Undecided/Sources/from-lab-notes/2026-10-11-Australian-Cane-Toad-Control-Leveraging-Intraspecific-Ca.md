## Australian Cane Toad Control: Leveraging Intraspecific Cannibalism
**Clip title:** Cane Toads are Vanishing Quickly in Australia —The Real Predator Was Terrifying
**[[entities/tasia-custode|Author]] / channel:** Natty Greens
**URL:** https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=5GF7rKpAMOw

### Summary
This video details the disastrous introduction and spread of the cane toad in Australia and outlines the scientific efforts to control this invasive species by exploiting its unique biology. Originally brought to Queensland in 1935 from Hawaii (where they were also introduced from Puerto Rico) to combat [[concepts/sugar|sugar]] cane beetles, the 2,400 toads failed in their intended purpose. The beetles lived on high stalks or underground, inaccessible to the toads. Instead, the toads multiplied unchecked, now numbering over 200 million across 1.2 million square kilometers, and caused catastrophic declines in native wildlife, including Northern Quolls and monitor lizards, by poisoning them.

A key discovery highlighted is the paradox that despite their rapid spread, only about 0.5% of cane toads survive to adulthood. Researchers found that Australian cane toad tadpoles are fiercely cannibalistic, attracted by the bufodienolide toxin present in the eggs and other tadpoles of their own species. This cannibalism is far more prevalent in Australia than in their native South [[entities/america|America]] due to the absence of natural predators and high population densities. Furthermore, Australian cane toad embryos have evolved to rapidly develop, hatching faster to escape the "cannibalism window," though this accelerated development comes at a cost of slower growth and higher mortality in later stages. Young toads also shifted to daytime activity to avoid being eaten by larger, nocturnal adult toads.

These biological insights led to innovative control methods. Scientists developed a "tadpole trap" using bufodienolide as a lure, which effectively attracts and captures cane toad tadpoles with minimal impact on native species (who are repelled by the toxin). Crucially, the captured tadpoles are *not* killed but kept alive in mesh [[concepts/containerization-technology|containers]] within the ponds. These living tadpoles continuously leak a chemical into the water that stunts the development of subsequent cane toad embryos and deters adult females from laying new eggs, effectively "sterilizing" the breeding site. This method is being rolled out commercially and trialed by various organizations. Additionally, some native predators have begun to adapt, either evolving resistance to the toad's poison or [[concepts/learning|learning]] safe feeding strategies (e.g., water rats removing organs, crows eating the underside).

Looking to the future, scientists are also exploring [[concepts/gene-editing|gene-editing techniques]] to create "[[entities/peter-omara|Peter]] Pan" tadpoles by knocking out the gene responsible for metamorphosis. These modified tadpoles would remain large tadpoles, consume three times as many toad eggs, and eventually die without ever becoming adult toads. This approach, while still in its testing [[concepts/phase|phase]] and subject to rigorous ecological assessment to avoid past mistakes, offers a potential long-term, self-sustaining [[concepts/solution|solution]]. The video concludes that after 90 years of largely ineffective attempts to kill adult toads, the most promising control strategies are emerging from a deep understanding of the cane toad's own complex biology and evolutionary responses within the Australian environment.
