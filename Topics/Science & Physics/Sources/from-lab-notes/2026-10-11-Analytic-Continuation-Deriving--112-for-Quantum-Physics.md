## Analytic Continuation: Deriving -1/12 for Quantum Physics Applications
**Clip title:** Why Does -1/12 Keep Appearing Everywhere In [[concepts/physics|Physics]]?
**[[entities/tasia-custode|Author]] / channel:** PBS Space Time
**URL:** https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=w6229Ivy_Lo

### Summary
This video explores the perplexing mathematical result where the sum of all positive integers (1 + 2 + 3 + 4 + ...) appears to equal -1/12, despite conventionally adding up to infinity. The host clarifies that this isn't a direct summation but rather a profound result derived from a sophisticated mathematical technique known as "[[concepts/analytic-continuation|analytic continuation]]," particularly of the [[entities/bernhard-riemann|Riemann]] [[concepts/zeta-function|Zeta Function]]. While the basic summation of natural numbers is indeed divergent, analytic continuation allows mathematicians to extend the definition of functions to domains where their initial series might not converge, revealing unexpected finite values.

The core of this mathematical "trickery" lies in the [[entities/bernhard-riemann|Riemann]] [[concepts/zeta-function|Zeta Function]], defined as the sum of 1/n^s for all positive integers n. This function traditionally converges for values of s greater than 1. However, through analytic continuation, the function can be smoothly extended to include values of s less than or equal to 1, where the original infinite series would diverge. At the specific point where s equals -1, this analytically continued function yields the value of -1/12. This doesn't mean that adding all positive integers literally results in a negative fraction, but rather that -1/12 represents the "zeta-regularized" part of this otherwise infinite sum.

Remarkably, this seemingly abstract mathematical result has tangible implications in [[concepts/physics|physics]], particularly in the realm of [[concepts/quantum-field-theory|quantum field theory]]. The video explains its application to the Casimir effect, where two uncharged, parallel conductive plates in a vacuum [[concepts/experience|experience]] an attractive force. This force arises from the quantum vacuum, which is not truly empty but filled with fluctuating electromagnetic fields. The presence of the plates restricts certain vibrational modes of these fields within the gap, leading to a slightly lower energy [[concepts/density|density]] inside compared to outside. Calculating this energy difference involves summing infinite series that diverge, but by applying regularization techniques—similar in principle to analytic continuation and often involving the -1/12 term—physicists can extract a finite, measurable force. This force has been experimentally verified, demonstrating the [[concepts/physical-reality|physical reality]] behind these mathematical procedures.

Furthermore, the -1/12 result also appears in [[concepts/string-theory|string theory]], a [[concepts/theory|theoretical framework]] attempting to unify all fundamental forces. When calculating the energy spectrum of fundamental vibrating strings, similar divergent sums arise. To ensure the mathematical [[concepts/logical-consistency|consistency]] of bosonic string theory, particularly to preserve Lorentz symmetry, physicists must regularize these infinite sums. This regularization, which again involves the -1/12 term, forces the theory to be formulated in a specific "critical dimension." For bosonic string theory, this dimension is 26. While modern superstring theories incorporate fermionic vibrations to reduce this to 10 dimensions, the underlying principle of managing infinities through regularization, often yielding the -1/12 contribution, remains fundamental. The takeaway is that these advanced mathematical techniques are not mere curiosities but essential tools that allow physicists to extract meaningful finite quantities from infinite sums, providing deep insights into the fundamental nature of reality, from the [[concepts/type-ia-supernovae|vacuum energy]] of space to the fabric of spacetime itself.
