# Atmospheric Circulation Model

An [[concepts/atmospheric-dynamics|atmospheric circulation]] model is a mathematical and computational representation of how air moves across large [[concepts/musical-scales|scales]] in [[concepts/earths-atmosphere|Earth's atmosphere]]. These models simulate wind patterns, [[concepts/pressure|pressure]] systems, and heat distribution driven by [[concepts/solar-energy|solar radiation]] and planetary rotation. They form the foundation of [[concepts/weather-forecasting|weather forecasting]], climate research, and understanding persistent global phenomena such as the Hadley, Ferrel, and Polar cells. By solving [[concepts/fluid-dynamics|fluid dynamics]] equations on a grid, these tools allow scientists to predict short-term weather events and long-term climate trends with increasing accuracy.

The development of these models relies heavily on empirical data from controlled environments and global observation networks. Studies such as the [[concepts/controlled-climate-experiment|Biosphere 2 experiment]] have provided critical insights into closed-system atmospheric dynamics, helping researchers refine how air mass exchange and thermal gradients behave in complex, bounded environments. This [[concepts/experimental-evidence|experimental data]] complements satellite observations and ground-based meteorological stations, ensuring that [[concepts/theoretical-frameworks|theoretical frameworks]] align with real-world physical constraints.

Modern atmospheric circulation models are categorized into global climate models (GCMs) and regional climate models (RCMs). GCMs simulate the entire planet's atmosphere to study broad climatic patterns, while RCMs provide higher-[[concepts/solution|resolution]] data for specific geographic areas. These models are essential for analyzing the impacts of greenhouse gas emissions, ozone depletion, and other anthropogenic factors on global weather systems, enabling policymakers and scientists to assess future environmental [[concepts/scenarios|scenarios]].
