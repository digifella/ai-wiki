## Fried Rice Formula: Essential Techniques for Rice Treatment and Mix-ins
**Clip title:** The Formula to Great Fried Rice with Any Ingredients | Techniquely with Lan Lam
**Author / channel:** [[entities/america|America]]'s Test Kitchen
**URL:** https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=_ODdLLEKo24

### [[concepts/summary|Summary]]
This video from America's Test Kitchen, hosted by Lan Lam, delves into the universal "formula" for making excellent fried rice, regardless of its diverse global styles. Lam begins by showcasing three distinct examples: Korean Kimchi Bokkeumbap, a Chinese-American brown rice version with pork and shrimp, and British Kedgeree with smoked salmon. She emphasizes that despite these culinary variations, core components, operational order, and rice treatment methods are largely universal, providing a foundation for countless creative fried rice dishes.

A crucial element highlighted is the importance of using stale or leftover rice. This is due to a process called retrogradation, where cooked rice starches crystallize when cooled, making the grains firm, dry, and easily separated—ideal for achieving a non-mushy fried rice. Refrigeration accelerates this staling effect significantly. If using freshly cooked rice, Lam recommends spreading it thinly on a baking sheet to cool completely before refrigerating for about 30 minutes, which maximizes moisture evaporation. Brown rice naturally resists clumping due to its intact bran layer, making it another excellent choice that doesn't require pre-staling.

The [[concepts/preparation|preparation]] of "mix-ins" is another key step. While scrambled eggs are a standard, other proteins and vegetables offer flexibility. Lam advises cutting all ingredients uniformly and into small pieces to ensure even cooking and that each bite contains a variety of flavors. For eggs, cracking and salting them early helps denature proteins, leading to a looser, more easily blended texture. Crucially, Lam underscores the importance of having all ingredients and tools prepared as *mise en place* before cooking, as stir-frying is a fast-paced method where quick additions are essential. A wok is preferred for its shape and capacity, allowing for easy tossing without spillage, though a cast-iron skillet can also be used with extra care.

When it comes to the frying process, mix-ins are cooked individually and seasoned sequentially to ensure optimal doneness and flavor development for each component. Adequate fat is vital to thoroughly coat the rice, often requiring more than one might initially think. Aromatics, such as scallions, are gently heated to infuse the oil, not browned. The rice, ideally at room temperature and broken into individual grains, is then added to the wok and generously seasoned with salt and pepper (carbohydrates benefit from ample seasoning). If rice clumps are stubborn, a tablespoon or two of water can act as a lubricant, provided it's cooked off to prevent sogginess. Liquid seasonings like soy sauce or oyster sauce are added after oil-based ones to minimize moisture interaction with starches.

In conclusion, Lan Lam illustrates that crafting excellent fried rice is achievable by following a few fundamental principles: utilizing stale or properly prepared fresh rice, uniformly cutting and sequentially cooking mix-ins, ensuring thorough seasoning of the rice, and having a well-organized workspace for efficient stir-frying. By mastering this core formula, cooks gain the confidence and creativity to experiment with a vast array of ingredients and flavor combinations, yielding dynamic and satisfying fried rice dishes, often finished with personalized garnishes for added zest.
