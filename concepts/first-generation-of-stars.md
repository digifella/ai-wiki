# First Generation Of Stars

[[concepts/population-iii-stars|Population III stars]] constitute the universe's first generation of [[concepts/stellar-objects|stellar objects]], forming approximately 100 to 200 million years after the [[concepts/big-bang|Big Bang]]. These primordial stars emerged from clouds composed exclusively of hydrogen and helium, lacking the heavy elements that characterize later stellar generations. Because they were formed from pristine material, they contained no [[concepts/metal|metals]], distinguishing them fundamentally from subsequent Population II and Population I stars.

Theoretical models suggest that Population III stars were significantly more massive than modern stars, potentially reaching hundreds of times the mass of the Sun. This extreme mass resulted in high core temperatures and pressures, leading to rapid [[concepts/fusion-power|nuclear fusion]] and short lifespans of only a few million years. Their rapid evolution and eventual collapse into [[concepts/supernovae|supernovae]] were crucial for seeding the [[concepts/early-universe|early universe]] with heavier elements, enabling the formation of subsequent generations of stars and planetary systems.

Recent astronomical observations have provided evidence for the existence of these ancient stars. The [[concepts/james-webb-space-telescope|James Webb Space Telescope]] (JWST) detected spectral signatures consistent with Population III stars within the [[concepts/gn-z11-galaxy|high-redshift galaxy GN-z11]]. This discovery offers critical observational support for cosmological models regarding the epoch of reionization and the chemical enrichment history of the early universe.
