# Laces

Laces are thin cords or straps threaded through eyelets or holes in fabric or leather to secure clothing and footwear. They function through mechanical binding, creating friction and tension between the laced material and the cord itself, rather than relying on adhesion. Simple lacing systems use a single cord woven back and forth through parallel holes, while more elaborate arrangements employ multiple passes, crisscross patterns, or decorative configurations.

Archaeological evidence suggests lacing systems date to prehistoric times, with early examples found in ancient Egypt and Mesoamerica. In ancient Egypt, laces were used to secure sandals and tunics, often crafted from linen or leather. Similarly, in Mesoamerican cultures, lacing techniques were employed in the construction of footwear and ceremonial garments, demonstrating the widespread independent development of this fastening method across different civilizations.

The engineering principles underlying laces have influenced the development of other fastening technologies, including zippers. The mechanical interaction between the lace and the eyelets provides a reliable means of adjustment and security, which remains relevant in modern footwear and apparel design. Contemporary laces are made from a variety of materials, including cotton, polyester, and synthetic fibers, each offering different properties in terms of durability, elasticity, and resistance to wear.
