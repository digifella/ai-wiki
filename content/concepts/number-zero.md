# Number Zero

Zero emerged as a mathematical concept in [[concepts/ancient-india|ancient India]], likely developing during the early centuries of the Common Era. Unlike earlier [[concepts/number-system|numeral systems]] that lacked a symbol for absence or emptiness, the Indian mathematical tradition formalized zero as both a placeholder in positional notation and as a number in its own right. This dual function was essential for the development of the decimal system, which eventually replaced Roman numerals and other cumbersome numeral systems across the world.

The concept of zero carried philosophical and religious significance in [[concepts/indian-civilization|Indian culture]], influenced by Buddhist and Hindu mathematical [[concepts/human-cognition|thinking]]. The Sanskrit term "śūnya" (शून्य), meaning emptiness or void, connected mathematical zero to philosophical concepts of nothingness. This cultural foundation allowed Indian mathematicians to treat zero not merely as a notational convenience but as a legitimate mathematical entity that could be used in calculations.

## Western Resistance and Adoption

The introduction of zero to Western [[concepts/mathematics|mathematics]] faced considerable resistance during the medieval period. European scholars and mathematicians were skeptical of a symbol representing nothing, viewing it as philosophically problematic or even heretical. This resistance delayed the adoption of the Hindu-Arabic numeral system in [[entities/europe|Europe]] until the 12th and 13th centuries, when merchants and mathematicians recognized its practical advantages for commerce and calculation. By the Renaissance, zero had become integral to European mathematics, enabling advances in algebra, calculus, and scientific notation that would define the modern era.
