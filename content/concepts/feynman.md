# Feynman

[[entities/richard-feynman|Richard Feynman]] (1918–1988) was an American theoretical physicist who made foundational contributions to [[concepts/quantum-mechanics|quantum mechanics]] and quantum electrodynamics during the twentieth century. His work fundamentally shaped modern [[concepts/physics|physics]], particularly through the development of Feynman [[concepts/diagrams|diagrams]]—graphical representations of particle interactions that became indispensable tools for calculating quantum processes. He shared the Nobel Prize in Physics in 1965 for his work on quantum electrodynamics alongside Julian Schwinger and Sin-Itiro Tomonaga.

## Scientific Methodology

Feynman was known for articulating a distinctive approach to scientific investigation. He emphasized the importance of making educated guesses about how [[entities/nature|nature]] works, performing precise calculations based on those guesses, and then comparing predictions rigorously against experimental observations. This cycle of hypothesis, computation, and [[concepts/empirical-validation|empirical validation]] reflected his conviction that understanding required both mathematical precision and a deep physical intuition about natural phenomena.

## Broader Intellectual Contributions

Beyond [[concepts/theoretical-physics|theoretical physics]], Feynman maintained an active interest in the nature of knowledge and [[concepts/cognition|cognition]]. He reflected on the distinction between mathematical formalism and genuine understanding, arguing that the ability to apply equations did not necessarily imply comprehension of underlying principles. He also considered questions about [[concepts/machine-intelligence|machine intelligence]] relative to human thought, exploring what aspects of cognition might be mechanically reproducible and which remained distinctly human intellectual capacities.
