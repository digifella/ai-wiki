# Solid Core

The Earth's inner core, located beneath the outer core at depths exceeding 5,100 kilometers, is traditionally modeled as a solid sphere composed primarily of iron and nickel. This structure exists under extreme pressure and temperature conditions that theoretically stabilize the metallic lattice. However, recent seismic data has challenged the conventional view of the inner core as a homogeneous solid, suggesting instead the presence of complex structural variations or novel states of matter.

## Seismic Anomalies

Observations of seismic waves propagating through the inner core reveal significant directional dependence in their velocity, a phenomenon known as seismic anisotropy. These anomalies indicate that the material properties within the core vary depending on the direction of wave travel, which cannot be fully accounted for by standard models of a uniform solid. The discrepancy between predicted and observed wave speeds has led researchers to investigate alternative explanations for the core's internal composition and physical state.

## Implications for Matter Physics

The inability of conventional models to explain these seismic anomalies has prompted scientific inquiry into the existence of new states of matter within the Earth's deep interior. Potential explanations include the alignment of iron crystals, the presence of lighter elements altering the core's density, or the formation of exotic high-pressure phases of iron. Further research is required to determine whether these findings represent a distinct phase of matter or complex dynamic processes within the existing solid framework.
