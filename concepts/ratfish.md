# Ratfish

Ratfish are deep-sea fish belonging to the subclass Chimaera, making them distant relatives of sharks and [[concepts/rays|rays]] rather than true fish. Despite their fish-like [[concepts/presence|appearance]], they are [[concepts/cartilaginous-fish|cartilaginous fish]] more closely related to elasmobranchs than to teleosts. They are characterized by their elongated bodies, large eyes adapted to low-[[concepts/light|light]] environments, and venomous [[concepts/spines|spines]] on their dorsal fins. Their common name derives from their rat-like appearance, particularly their pointed snouts and tapering tails.

## Habitat and Distribution

Ratfish inhabit ocean depths typically ranging from 200 to 2,600 meters below the surface. They are found in continental slopes and deep-sea environments across various oceans worldwide. Their anatomical [[concepts/adaptations|adaptations]]—particularly their large eyes and sensory organs—reflect their [[concepts/specialization|specialization]] for life in the twilight and aphotic zones where light is minimal or absent.

## Ecology and Behavior

Ratfish are benthic feeders that consume invertebrates and small fish found on or near the seafloor. They use their specialized sensory systems, including electroreceptive organs, to locate prey in darkness. Their venomous spines likely serve as defensive structures against potential predators in their deep-sea environment.

## Evolutionary Significance

Ratfish represent an ancient [[concepts/evolutionary-lineage|lineage]] of cartilaginous fish with fossil records extending back hundreds of millions of years. Their study provides insights into the evolution of shark relatives and the adaptations that enable survival in extreme deep-sea conditions.
