# Explosive Volcanism

Explosive volcanism refers to [[concepts/volcanic-eruptions|volcanic eruptions]] characterized by the violent and rapid expulsion of rock, ash, gas, and magma from a volcanic vent. These events occur when magma with high [[concepts/viscosity|viscosity]] and high dissolved gas content becomes pressurized within the volcanic system, leading to a sudden and forceful [[concepts/deployment|release]] of energy. This mechanism stands in [[concepts/contrast|contrast]] to effusive volcanism, where [[concepts/lava-flows|lava]] emerges more gently from a volcano without violent ejection.

The intensity of an explosive eruption depends on several factors, including the viscosity of the magma, the volume of dissolved gases, and the rate of decompression. High-viscosity magmas, such as rhyolite or dacite, trap gases more effectively than low-viscosity basalts, allowing [[concepts/pressure|pressure]] to build until the surrounding rock fractures. When this pressure is released, the rapid expansion of gases shatters the magma into fine particles, creating pyroclastic flows and ash plumes that can reach the stratosphere.

A notable example of this phenomenon is the Cerberan [[concepts/supervolcano|Supervolcano]], located northeast of [[concepts/melbourne-region|Melbourne]], Victoria. This geological feature exemplifies the scale and impact of supervolcanic events, which are defined by their ability to eject massive volumes of material over extended periods. Such eruptions have significant implications for regional [[concepts/geology|geology]] and global climate systems due to the injection of aerosols and ash into the [[concepts/earths-atmosphere|atmosphere]].
