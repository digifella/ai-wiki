# 2026 04 12 Feynman Knowing Versus Understanding

Richard Feynman, an American theoretical physicist renowned for his work in quantum electrodynamics, formulated a persistent critique of superficial learning throughout his career as both a researcher and educator. His observations focused on a fundamental distinction between knowing the name or definition of a phenomenon and understanding how it actually functions. This perspective emerged from his experiences teaching and observing students who could recite complex terminology yet failed to grasp the underlying physical mechanisms.

## The Nature of Superficial Knowledge

Feynman argued that memorizing definitions allows individuals to speak confidently about subjects without possessing genuine insight. He illustrated this through anecdotes where students could define terms like "energy" or "momentum" but could not apply them to solve novel problems or explain real-world occurrences. This type of knowledge is fragile and context-dependent, crumbling when faced with situations that deviate from standard textbook examples.

## Deep Comprehension and First Principles

In contrast, Feynman advocated for understanding through first principles, where one breaks down complex systems into their most basic components and reasons up from there. This approach requires visualizing processes and tracing causal chains rather than relying on analogies or rote memorization. By focusing on the "why" and "how" rather than just the "what," learners develop a robust mental model that adapts to new information and facilitates creative problem-solving.

## Educational Implications

This distinction has significant implications for pedagogy in the sciences. Feynman emphasized that true education involves stripping away jargon to reveal the core logic of a concept. He believed that if a subject cannot be explained simply, the explainer likely does not understand it well enough. Consequently, his legacy includes a rigorous standard for intellectual honesty, urging scholars and students alike to prioritize depth of comprehension over the appearance of expertise.
