# Battery Reliability

Battery reliability represents a critical evaluation metric for electric vehicles, encompassing the ability of battery systems to maintain performance, retain capacity, and operate safely throughout their intended lifespan. As lithium-ion batteries degrade over time through chemical cycling, [[concepts/heat-stress|thermal stress]], and manufacturing defects, understanding which vehicles demonstrate dependable energy [[entities/storage|storage]] solutions and which present significant risks has become essential for consumers and fleet operators making long-term investment decisions.

## Performance and Degradation

Battery performance is measured primarily through capacity retention rates, which indicate how much usable energy a battery can store after extended use. Most modern EV batteries are designed to retain 70–80% of their original capacity after 8–10 years or 100,000–150,000 miles, depending on driving conditions, climate, and charging practices. Real-world degradation varies considerably across manufacturers, with some vehicles experiencing steeper capacity loss due to thermal management limitations, aggressive fast-charging protocols, or cell chemistry choices. Temperature extremes, frequent rapid charging, and deep discharge cycles accelerate degradation, making battery reliability heavily dependent on [[concepts/vehicle-design|vehicle design]] and user behavior.

## Reliability Assessment

Evaluating battery reliability requires combining manufacturer warranty data, independent [[concepts/testing|testing]] results, and documented consumer reports of premature failures or capacity loss. Vehicles with comprehensive battery warranties (typically 8 years or more), robust thermal management systems, and consistent performance across multiple model years tend to demonstrate higher reliability. Conversely, models with documented issues such as unexpected capacity fade, thermal runaway incidents, or manufacturing defects warrant careful consideration before purchase.
