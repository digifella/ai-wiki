## Physicists' Critique: Helion Energy's $15 Billion Nuclear Fusion Machine Cannot Work
**Clip title:** This $15 Billion [[concepts/hydrogen-fusion|Nuclear Fusion]] Machine Can’t Work, Physicists Say
**[[entities/tasia-custode|Author]] / channel:** Sabine Hossenfelder
**URL:** https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=wwB4hB9QJX8

### Summary
The video discusses the current state of [[concepts/hydrogen-fusion|nuclear fusion]] energy, particularly focusing on the challenges faced by startups like Helion, despite a significant surge in private investment. The host, [[entities/sabine-hossenfelder|Sabine Hossenfelder]], highlights that while fusion startups are continually pushing back their completion dates, a clearer picture is emerging regarding the viability of different technological approaches. She points out a "circularity" in the investment landscape, where major tech companies like [[concepts/google-search|Google]], [[entities/microsoft|Microsoft]] (via [[concepts/whisper-transcription|OpenAI]] CEO [[entities/sam-altman|Sam Altman]]), and Nucor are not only funding fusion startups but also signing contracts to purchase hypothetical future electricity, creating a speculative bubble around the industry. Helion, in particular, has seen its valuation soar to an astonishing $15.5 billion, tripling since January, despite growing [[concepts/scientific-skepticism|scientific skepticism]].

[[entities/helion-energy|Helion Energy]]'s innovative approach involves a "field-reversed configuration" (FRC) where two plasma blobs are accelerated by magnets, collide, compress, and ideally fuse, with the goal of direct energy extraction. However, this method has recently come under harsh criticism from multiple independent scientific groups. [[entities/chef-john|John]] Slough, a pioneer in FRC research, argues that Helion's aggressive approach runs into a "fundamental aspect" of FRC, leading to "catastrophic loss of flux" before fusion can occur. He explicitly states he "can't see anything in the [[concepts/physics|physics]]" that would allow their machine to work.

Further critiques come from a Max [[entities/planck|Planck]] Institute group, which contends that while initial energy transfer might favor heavy particles, this energy quickly redistributes, preventing nuclear ignition and [[concepts/fat-rendering|rendering]] the approach impractical for power production. A French research group added that only a tiny fraction (around 1%) of the magnetic field energy actually heats the plasma, leading to massive [[concepts/energy-dissipation|energy loss]] and concluding that "net energy is never achieved" in realistic [[concepts/scenarios|scenarios]]. Helion's response to this criticism has been largely dismissive, claiming the critics are looking at an "old design plan" and that they "know how to fix the problem but won't tell you how," while quietly delaying their net power target from 2028 to 2029 or 2030.

Sabine concludes by expressing her personal skepticism about investing in Helion. She [[concepts/notes|notes]] that other less experimental fusion approaches, such as magnetic confinement (e.g., Tokamaks, Stellarators), have shown more steady, albeit incremental, progress, while inertial confinement fusion (like that pursued by First [[concepts/light|Light]] Fusion) has largely stalled. Hybrid approaches, like the Z-pinch, represent a higher risk due to less understood [[concepts/physics|physics]] but could potentially yield faster results if successful. Overall, she believes it is highly unlikely that any entity [[entities/will|will]] achieve net power from nuclear fusion before the end of the decade, summarizing the situation as "understood physics is slow, poorly understood physics is fast... right up until you understand it."
