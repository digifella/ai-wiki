# Attachment Theory

Attachment Theory is a psychological framework developed primarily by John Bowlby in the 1950s that explains how early [[concepts/relationships|relationships]] between infants and caregivers influence emotional and social development. The [[concepts/theory|theory]] proposes that children form emotional bonds with their primary caregivers as an evolutionary adaptation for survival. Bowlby argued that the quality of these early attachments significantly shapes personality, relationship patterns, and [[concepts/mental-health|mental health]] throughout the lifespan. This framework emerged from observations of separated children and became foundational to understanding human psychological development.

## Attachment Styles

Research by Mary Ainsworth and others identified distinct attachment patterns in infants based on their [[concepts/responses|responses]] to caregiver separation and reunion. [[concepts/secure|Secure]] attachment develops when caregivers respond consistently to a child's needs, resulting in confidence in relationships and emotional [[concepts/regulation|regulation]]. Insecure attachment patterns—including anxious-ambivalent, dismissive-avoidant, and disorganized styles—emerge from inconsistent, neglectful, or unpredictable caregiving. These early patterns tend to persist into adulthood, influencing how individuals form and maintain relationships.

## Contemporary Applications

Attachment Theory has become widely applied in clinical psychology, educational settings, and parenting guidance. Therapists use attachment frameworks to understand relationship difficulties and trauma responses in adults. The theory has also informed policy discussions around childcare, foster care, and institutional settings. While the core concepts remain influential, contemporary research examines cultural variations in attachment expression and the plasticity of attachment patterns across the lifespan.
