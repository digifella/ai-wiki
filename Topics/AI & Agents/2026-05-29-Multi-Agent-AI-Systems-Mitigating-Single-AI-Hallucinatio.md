## Multi-Agent AI Systems: Mitigating Single AI Hallucinations for High-Stakes Applications
**Clip title:** Multi [[concepts/ai-productivity-agents|AI Agent Systems]]: When One AI Brain Isn’t Enough
**Author / channel:** IBM Technology
**URL:** https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=kYkZI3oj2W4

### Summary
The video, presented by [[entities/ibm|IBM]] [[entities/ai-engineer|AI Engineer]] [[entities/bri-kopecki|Bri Kopecki]], addresses a critical issue with current single [[concepts/agentic-ai|AI agents]]: their inability to express uncertainty, often leading to confident but factually incorrect answers. Kopecki highlights that while this "[[concepts/data-hallucination|hallucination]] problem" might be acceptable for low-stakes tasks like summarizing emails, it poses a significant liability in high-stakes domains such as [[concepts/health-care|healthcare]], financial transactions, or legal [[concepts/compliance|compliance]]. She argues that [[concepts/large-language-model-llm|Large Language Models]] (LLMs) are fundamentally designed to produce plausible output rather than to recognize the boundaries of their own knowledge, making confidence without [[concepts/verification|verification]] a dangerous feature, not a beneficial one.

To mitigate this inherent flaw, the video proposes adopting "[[concepts/expertise-based-ai-assistants|multi-agent systems]]" – an institutional wisdom that humans have developed over centuries. Kopecki illustrates this with examples from various fields: medicine utilizes second [[concepts/opinions|opinions]] and tumor boards, finance employs the "four-eye principle" for significant transactions, and aviation relies on co-pilots and checklists. These systems are built on the understanding that even experts can be fallible under pressure, and therefore, [[concepts/trust|trust]] is earned through rigorous [[concepts/verification|verification]] and redundancy, not merely confidence.

A compelling historical example of a successful multi-[[entities/agent|agent]] system is [[concepts/nasa|NASA]]'s Apollo 11 Mission Control in 1969. During the lunar module's descent, unexpected alarms blared. Instead of a single individual making the critical "Go/No Go" decision, dozens of specialists, each an expert in a specific system (like guidance, flight dynamics, life support, and [[concepts/communication|communication]]), simultaneously monitored various [[concepts/parameters|parameters]]. A young engineer named Jack Garman, backed by his team, quickly recognized the alarms as a non-critical computer overload, allowing the Flight Director, Gene Kranz, to confidently give the "Go" command, leading to a successful moon landing. This demonstrated the power of [[concepts/swarm-intelligence|collective intelligence]], specialized expertise, and a clear protocol for resolving disagreements.

Applying this wisdom to AI, Kopecki suggests designing systems where one agent generates an answer, another verifies it (acting as a "Jack Garman" to cross-check facts and catch hallucinations), and a third plays the role of an "adversary" or "red team" to intentionally try and break the system. This automated "four-eyes principle" or "tumor board [[concepts/assistive-technology|at]] machine [[concepts/speed|speed]]" ensures that confidence is earned through rigorous internal verification. For low-stakes [[concepts/software|applications]], a single [[concepts/ai-agent|AI agent]] might suffice. However, for high-stakes environments where errors carry severe consequences—from patient harm to regulatory violations—integrating multi-agent verification directly into AI [[concepts/architecture|architecture]] is not just beneficial, but essential for building truly trustworthy systems.
