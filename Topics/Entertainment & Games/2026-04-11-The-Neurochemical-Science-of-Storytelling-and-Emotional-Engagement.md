## The Neurochemical Science of Storytelling and Emotional Engagement
**Clip title:** The magical [[concepts/neurological-impact-of-narrative|science of storytelling]] | [[entities/david|David]] JP Phillips | TEDxStockholm
**Author / channel:** TEDx Talks
**URL:** https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=Nj-hdQMa3uA

### Summary
David J.P. Phillips' TEDx Stockholm talk, "The Magical Science of [[concepts/storytelling|Storytelling]]," delves into the profound neurological impact of storytelling and how it can be leveraged for effective communication. Phillips opens by recounting [[entities/rob-walker|Rob Walker]]'s "[[entities/significant-objects|Significant Objects]]" project, where ordinary, inexpensive items bought for an average of $1 were given fictional stories by authors. When resold on eBay, these objects fetched significantly higher prices, demonstrating that a story alone could dramatically increase an item's perceived value – for instance, a horse head figurine bought for $0.99 sold for $62.95, an increase of over 6,000%. Phillips posits that this phenomenon is rooted in [emotional investment](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Emotional_investment), highlighting how stories can bypass our [[concepts/critical-thinking|critical thinking]] and objective observation.

Phillips explains that emotional investment is the key to storytelling's power, using the analogy of watching a James Bond movie. Despite its unrealistic nature, viewers become deeply invested, admiring Bond, and might even be influenced to purchase products associated with him, like an Omega watch, for significantly high prices. This happens because our brains are "tricked" by the narrative. He likens this to the experience of falling in [[concepts/love|love]], where our critical faculties are temporarily "hijacked" by neurochemicals, making us less objective. Phillips argues that storytelling can intentionally induce similar states by triggering the release of specific "[Angel's Cocktail](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Angel%27s_Cocktail)" hormones in the brain: [Dopamine](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Dopamine), [Oxytocin](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Oxytocin), and [Endorphins](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Endorphins).

The "Angel's Cocktail" comprises three key hormones and their effects. Dopamine, associated with suspense and cliff-hangers, enhances focus, [[concepts/motivation|motivation]], and [[concepts/memory|memory]]. Phillips demonstrates this by building anticipation in his own story. Oxytocin, released through [[concepts/empathy|empathy]] and personal narratives, fosters generosity, trust, and bonding. He illustrates this with a deeply personal and moving story about his son. Finally, Endorphins, stimulated by laughter and humor, promote positive feelings, creativity, and [[concepts/stress|stress]] reduction. Conversely, Phillips briefly mentions the "[Devil's Cocktail](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Devil%27s_Cocktail)" (cortisol and adrenaline), which makes people intolerant, irritable, uncreative, critical, and prone to bad decisions – often the default state in stressful work environments.

Phillips concludes by advocating for "Functional Storytelling" as a way to consciously harness these neurological effects. He provides three actionable takeaways: first, believe that everyone is a natural storyteller, inherently good at it since birth; second, actively write down your own stories, as you likely have three to four times more than you realize; and third, index these stories based on which "Angel's Cocktail" hormone they are most likely to evoke (dopamine for suspense, oxytocin for [[concepts/empathy|empathy]], endorphins for laughter). By understanding and applying these principles, communicators can intentionally trigger desired emotional responses, fostering better engagement, understanding, and [[concepts/connection|connection]], contrasting storytelling's ancient efficacy with the comparatively new and often less effective tool of PowerPoint.
