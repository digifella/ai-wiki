# Rays

Rays are [[concepts/cartilaginous-fish|cartilaginous fish]] belonging to the superorder Batoidea, making them close relatives of sharks and [[concepts/chimaeras|chimaeras]]. Like sharks, rays possess skeletons composed entirely of cartilage rather than bone, and they share a common ancestor within the larger group of cartilaginous fishes. Rays are distinguished by their flattened body shape, which evolved as an adaptation to benthic (bottom-dwelling) lifestyles, and their gill slits are positioned on the underside of their bodies rather than on the sides as in sharks.

## Evolution and the Golden Age of Sharks

Rays diversified significantly during the Paleozoic and Mesozoic eras, particularly during periods when shark diversity was [[concepts/assistive-technology|at]] its peak. The evolutionary relationship between rays and sharks reveals how cartilaginous fish adapted to different ecological niches—while sharks remained active swimmers in the water column, rays increasingly specialized for life on the seafloor. This divergence demonstrates the adaptive flexibility of the cartilaginous fish body plan and how related lineages can exploit different environmental opportunities.

## Modern Diversity

Today, rays comprise thousands of species with varying morphologies and lifestyles. Many rays are benthic foragers, using their flattened bodies to hunt along the ocean floor, while others have evolved more specialized [[concepts/adaptations|adaptations]] such as the venomous [[concepts/spines|spines]] of stingrays or the electrical organs of electric rays. Despite their diversity, all rays share the fundamental cartilaginous skeleton and gill [[concepts/structure|structure]] inherited from their shark relatives.
