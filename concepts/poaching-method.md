# Poaching Method

Poaching is a moist-heat cooking technique in which food is gently simmered in liquid at temperatures below boiling, typically between 160–180°F (70–82°C). The surrounding liquid, known as the poaching medium, conducts heat gradually to cook the food evenly while maintaining its delicate structure. This method is particularly valued for producing tender results while preserving the natural flavour and texture of the ingredient, making it suitable for proteins and fruits that can become tough or dry when subjected to higher heat.

The choice of poaching liquid significantly influences the final taste of the dish. Common mediums include water, stock, wine, milk, or fruit juices, each imparting distinct characteristics to the food. For instance, Normandy-style poached salmon is traditionally prepared in cider, which is then reduced with cream to create a rich sauce. The acidity and sweetness of the liquid can complement or contrast with the primary ingredient, allowing for versatile culinary applications ranging from delicate fish to ripe pears.

Proper execution requires careful temperature control to prevent the liquid from reaching a rolling boil, which can cause proteins to contract and become rubbery or cause fruits to disintegrate. The food is usually submerged completely or partially, depending on the recipe, and is often removed from the heat once it reaches the desired internal temperature. This gentle approach ensures that the structural integrity of the food is maintained, resulting in a moist and tender final product.
