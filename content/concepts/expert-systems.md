# Expert Systems

Expert systems were AI [[concepts/software|programs]] developed primarily in the 1980s that attempted to capture and reproduce human expert knowledge through explicit rules and logical [[concepts/inference|inference]]. These systems encoded [[concepts/domain-specific-knowledge|domain-specific knowledge]] provided by human experts into formal rule sets, allowing them to [[entities/make|make]] decisions and provide recommendations in specialized fields such as medical diagnosis, mineral exploration, and equipment troubleshooting. Despite initial enthusiasm and commercial success, expert systems encountered significant practical limitations as their complexity scaled and their brittleness in handling novel situations became apparent.

## Theoretical Foundations and Critique

The development of expert systems was influenced by philosophical work on human expertise and [[concepts/learning|learning]], particularly Hubert [[entities/dreyfus|Dreyfus]]'s critique of [[concepts/ai-technologies|artificial intelligence]] and his model of [[concepts/skill|skill]] acquisition. However, the assumptions underlying expert systems—that expertise could be fully articulated as explicit rules and that human [[concepts/decision-making|decision-making]] followed formal logical processes—proved increasingly problematic. Subsequent criticism from researchers including [[entities/gobel|Gobel]] and [[entities/chassy|Chassy]] challenged whether the rule-based approach captured how human experts actually function, suggesting that much expert knowledge is tacit, contextual, and acquired through experience rather than formal instruction.

## Legacy and Model Training

The limitations encountered by expert systems informed later approaches to [[concepts/training|training]] [[concepts/ai-models|AI models]]. Rather than attempting to explicitly encode all relevant knowledge, subsequent systems increasingly relied on learning from data and examples. This shift reflected recognition of what expert systems had implicitly demonstrated: that the most effective path to capable AI systems might lie not in formalizing human expertise, but in developing learning mechanisms that could acquire patterns from experience, much as human experts themselves do.
