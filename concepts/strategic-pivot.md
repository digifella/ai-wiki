A strategic pivot is a fundamental change in a company's business strategy, direction, or focus. Unlike incremental [[concepts/adjustments|adjustments]] to existing operations, a pivot represents a significant shift in how a company creates value, targets customers, or competes in its market. This may involve entering new markets, abandoning existing product lines, adopting new business models, or reallocating substantial resources to different areas of the organization.

## Common Drivers

Strategic pivots typically occur in response to changing market conditions, technological disruption, competitive pressure, or shifts in customer demand. A company may also pivot when its current business model becomes unsustainable or when [[concepts/leadership|leadership]] identifies a more promising opportunity. External events, such as regulatory changes or economic downturns, can trigger pivots as well. Some pivots are reactive—responding to threats or failures—while others are proactive, based on emerging opportunities or founder insight.

## Implementation and Risk

Executing a strategic pivot requires significant [[concepts/organizational-adaptation|organizational change]], including shifts in resource allocation, operational focus, and sometimes workforce [[concepts/writing|composition]]. Pivots carry substantial risk, as they divert resources from existing operations and depend on execution in unfamiliar markets or with untested business models. [[concepts/success|Success]] depends on the company's ability to learn quickly, maintain stakeholder confidence during the transition, and retain or develop the capabilities needed for the new direction.
