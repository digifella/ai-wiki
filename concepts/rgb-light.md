# Rgb Light

RGB light refers to the additive color model based on red, green, and blue wavelengths, which forms the foundation of how digital displays generate color by emitting light. The human eye contains three types of cone cells sensitive to different wavelengths: long (red, ~700 nm), medium (green, ~546 nm), and short (blue, ~435 nm). The brain combines signals from these receptors to perceive the full spectrum of visible colors. By varying the intensity of these three primary wavelengths, digital displays—including screens, projectors, and LEDs—can simulate a wide range of hues through the simultaneous stimulation of these specific photoreceptors.

This mechanism relies on the principle of metamerism, where different spectral power distributions produce the same perceived color sensation. Unlike subtractive color models used in printing, which rely on pigments absorbing light, RGB systems create color by adding light energy. This distinction is critical in understanding the difference between true spectral color and the illusion of color generated by mixed wavelengths. The resulting perception is a neurological construct rather than a direct representation of a single monochromatic wavelength.

The relationship between RGB technology and structural color highlights the contrast between emission-based and reflection-based color generation. Structural color, as seen in nature or Lippmann photography, arises from the physical interference of light with microstructures, producing colors that are spectrally pure and angle-dependent. In contrast, RGB displays produce color through the additive mixing of broad-spectrum emissions. While RGB can approximate the appearance of structural colors, it does so by tricking the eye’s cone cells rather than manipulating light physics at the microscopic level.

Research into Lippmann photography and structural color often examines how true color is preserved without the limitations of pigment degradation or viewing angle constraints inherent in some additive systems. The study of these phenomena underscores the fundamental difference between the biological processing of RGB signals and the physical interaction of light with matter. Understanding this distinction clarifies why digital color is an illusion of perception, whereas structural color is a physical property of the material itself.
