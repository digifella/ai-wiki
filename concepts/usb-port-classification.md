# USB Port Classification

USB ports are categorized by their physical connector form factors and the electrical standards they supports. The original USB standard introduced USB-A, a rectangular connector designed for host devices like computers, while USB-B connectors were created for peripherals such as printers and external drives. As consumer devices became increasingly compact, USB Micro-B emerged as a smaller alternative and dominated mobile phones and portable electronics for over a decade. Each physical connector type remained distinct and mechanically incompatible with the others.

## Standard Connector Types

USB-A remains the most common connector for host devices and has remained largely unchanged since its introduction. USB-B, larger and squarer in form, was primarily used on printers and external storage devices. USB Micro-B became ubiquitous in smartphones and tablets, providing a smaller footprint suitable for mobile devices. These three types represent the primary legacy connector standards that defined the USB ecosystem for many years.

## USB-C and Modern Standards

USB-C introduced a symmetrical, reversible connector design that eliminated the need for separate host and peripheral connector types. Unlike its predecessors, USB-C supports multiple electrical standards including USB 2.0, USB 3.0, and USB 3.1, as well as alternative protocols like Thunderbolt 3 and DisplayPort. This flexibility and backwards compatibility through adapters has positioned USB-C as the successor to the fragmented connector landscape of earlier USB generations, though legacy connector types remain in use across many existing devices.
