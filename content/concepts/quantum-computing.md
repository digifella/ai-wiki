# Quantum Computing

Quantum computing represents a fundamental departure from classical computation, leveraging quantum mechanical phenomena such as superposition and entanglement to process information. Unlike [[concepts/classical-bits|classical bits]] that exist as either 0 or 1, quantum bits ([[concepts/qubits|qubits]]) can exist in multiple states simultaneously, potentially enabling exponential speedups for specific [[concepts/computational-problems|computational problems]]. Current quantum computers remain in early developmental stages, with existing systems containing dozens to hundreds of qubits and facing significant challenges in error correction and coherence maintenance.

## Topological Approaches and Majorana Systems

Topological quantum computing architectures offer a promising path toward more stable, scalable quantum systems. These approaches exploit topological properties of quantum states to achieve inherent protection against certain types of errors. Majorana fermions—exotic quasiparticles predicted to exist in topological superconductors—represent a particularly focused area of research for building fault-tolerant qubits. Such systems aim to address the decoherence problems that currently limit quantum computer performance and [[concepts/computational-scaling|scaling]].

## Cryptographic Implications and Q-Day

The development of sufficiently powerful quantum computers poses a direct threat to current cryptographic infrastructure. Many widely-deployed encryption schemes, including RSA and elliptic curve [[concepts/cryptography|cryptography]], would become vulnerable to quantum algorithms such as Shor's algorithm. This potential future scenario—sometimes referred to as "Q-Day"—has prompted significant research into [[concepts/post-quantum-security|post-quantum cryptography]]. Lattice-based cryptographic approaches and other quantum-resistant algorithms are being standardized and implemented to ensure data security in an era when large-scale quantum computers become available.
