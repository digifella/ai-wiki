# Habitat Recovery

**Habitat Recovery** refers to the process of restoring degraded ecosystems to a functional, self-sustaining state, often involving the removal of stressors (e.g., [[concepts/invasive-species|invasive species]], pollution) and the reintroduction of native [[concepts/biodiversity|biodiversity]]. It bridges the gap between passive [[concepts/preservation|conservation]] and active Restoration Ecology.

## Core Principles
- **Succession Management**: Guiding ecological succession from pioneer species to climax communities.
- **Invasive Species Control**: Managing non-native flora/fauna that outcompete native species.
- **Soil Remediation**: Restoring soil [[concepts/health|health]] and microbiome diversity.
- **Biodiversity Reintroduction**: Re-establishing native plant and animal populations.

## Case Studies & Applications

### Banks Peninsula Rewilding: Gorse-Based Restoration
A notable example of unconventional habitat recovery involves the work of botanist [[entities/hugh-wilson|Hugh Wilson]] on [[concepts/banks-peninsula]], New Zealand. Contrary to standard eradication protocols for invasive [[concepts/gorse]] (*Ulex europaeus*), Wilson utilized the species as a foundational tool for [[concepts/ecosystem-restoration|ecosystem restoration]].

- **Methodology**: Instead of immediate removal, Wilson allowed gorse to establish on [[concepts/degraded-farmland|degraded farmland]], leveraging its nitrogen-fixing capabilities and dense structure to stabilize soil and create microclimates.
- **Ecological Succession**: The gorse acted as a nurse crop, facilitating the return of native understory plants and eventually supporting broader biodiversity.
- **Outcome**: This approach demonstrated that invasive species can be integrated into restoration strategies to accelerate recovery in severely degraded landscapes, challenging the binary view of "weed vs. native."
