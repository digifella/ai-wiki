# Irrational Number

An irrational number is a real number that cannot be expressed as a ratio of two integers. Formally, it is any real number that cannot be written in the form $p/q$, where $p$ and $q$ are integers and $q \neq 0$. This definition distinguishes irrational numbers from rational numbers, which include all integers, finite [[concepts/decimal-places|decimals]], and repeating decimals. The set of irrational numbers, when combined with the set of rational numbers, constitutes the entire set of real numbers.

The decimal representation of an irrational number is non-terminating and non-repeating. Unlike rational numbers, whose decimal expansions either end or enter a repeating cycle, the digits of an irrational number continue infinitely without any periodic pattern. This property is a direct consequence of the number's inability to be represented as a fraction. For example, the square root of 2 ($\sqrt{2}$) and Euler's number ($e$) are classic examples of irrational numbers derived from geometric and exponential contexts, respectively.

The most prominent irrational number is pi ($\pi$), defined as the ratio of a circle's circumference to its diameter. Despite its geometric origin, $\pi$ cannot be expressed exactly as a simple fraction, and its decimal expansion extends infinitely without repetition. Other significant irrational numbers include the golden ratio ($\phi$) and various roots of non-perfect squares. The existence of irrational numbers was historically significant, as it challenged the [[concepts/ancient-greek|ancient Greek]] belief that all quantities could be described by integer ratios, leading to the development of rigorous proofs in [[concepts/mathematics|mathematics]].
