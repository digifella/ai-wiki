# Western Thought

Western thought encompasses the philosophical, intellectual, and cultural traditions that emerged primarily in [[entities/europe|Europe]] from ancient Greece onward, later spreading to the Americas and other regions through colonization and [[concepts/cultural-exchange|cultural exchange]]. These traditions form a loosely connected body of [[concepts/ideas|ideas]] rather than a [[concepts/unified-system|unified system]], characterized by particular emphases on rationalism, individual agency, and systematic categorization of knowledge. Key periods include ancient Greek [[concepts/philosophy|philosophy]], medieval scholasticism, the Renaissance, the Enlightenment, and modern scientific and philosophical movements.

## Major Intellectual Traditions

Western thought is commonly divided into several overlapping traditions. Ancient Greek philosophy established foundational approaches to logic, metaphysics, and [[concepts/ethics|ethics]] through thinkers like Plato and Aristotle. The medieval period saw the [[concepts/integration|integration]] of Christian theology with classical philosophy. The Renaissance and Enlightenment prioritized empiricism, [[concepts/scientific-method|scientific method]], and human reason as paths to understanding [[entities/nature|nature]] and society. Later developments included Romanticism, which emphasized emotion and intuition, and modern movements ranging from positivism to existentialism to postmodernism.

## Contrasts with Eastern Thought

Scholars often discuss Western thought in relation to Eastern philosophical traditions, including Chinese, Indian, and Japanese systems. While Western thought is frequently characterized as emphasizing dualism, linear causality, and analytical separation of components, Eastern systems are often described as more holistic and cyclical. However, this binary distinction has been critiqued as an oversimplification, as both Western and Eastern traditions contain diverse approaches that do not fit neat categorical boundaries.
