# Lens Compression

Lens compression is an optical effect that occurs when using longer focal lengths, where objects at varying distances appear closer together than they actually are in physical space. This happens because telephoto lenses have a narrower field of view, which compresses the spatial relationships in a scene. The effect becomes more pronounced with increasingly longer focal lengths—a 200mm lens produces more noticeable compression than an 85mm lens. This is a direct result of how longer focal lengths render perspective, effectively flattening depth relationships in the final image.

## Technical Basis

Lens compression is not a property of the lens itself but rather a perceptual consequence of perspective and framing. When using longer focal lengths, the photographer must stand farther away from a subject to frame it similarly to a wider lens. This greater distance, combined with the narrower angle of view, causes foreground and background elements to appear stacked closely together. Objects separated by considerable distance appear nearly equal in size and proximity, reducing the sense of depth that would be apparent to someone viewing the scene directly.

## Practical Applications

Photographers commonly use lens compression to enhance visual impact in portraiture, wildlife, and landscape photography. In these contexts, the flattened perspective can isolate subjects from their backgrounds, create compelling layering of elements, or emphasize the scale relationships between distant objects. Understanding compression helps photographers choose appropriate focal lengths to achieve desired spatial effects in their compositions.
