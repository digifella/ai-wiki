Summary

The video introduces an innovative long-term energy storage solution developed by the Swiss company Season, aiming to store surplus renewable energy for months with minimal loss. The core of this technology is a thermochemical absorption heat storage system that utilizes a conventional caustic soda (sodium hydroxide) solution and water. The primary goal is to address the mismatch between solar energy production (abundant in summer) and heating demand (highest in winter), offering a sustainable alternative to fossil fuels and traditional battery storage.

The system operates using a reversible thermochemical process. In the summer, excess energy from sources like solar panels or waste heat is used to evaporate water from the caustic soda solution, concentrating it in one tank (an endothermic reaction). The separated water is stored in a second tank. Come winter, this process is reversed: the stored water is fed into an evaporator, and its vapor is absorbed by the highly concentrated caustic soda solution (an exothermic reaction). This absorption releases significant heat, which can then be transferred to a building’s heating system. Key advantages include using widely available materials, being non-flammable and non-explosive (unlike hydrogen), and providing high flow temperatures suitable even for poorly insulated older buildings, all while consuming minimal electricity during heat release, thus supporting grid stability.

Season is already implementing this technology in several pilot projects, including a 36-apartment building in Bochum, Germany, where the system will utilize energy from a photovoltaic array and the electricity spot market. Regarding safety, while caustic soda is corrosive (with a pH of up to 14), the company states it is stored in robust double-walled tanks or tanks with spill containment, mitigating environmental risks. Maintenance is designed to be straightforward, and crucially, the chemical potential of the caustic soda solution does not deplete over time, allowing for indefinite use.

Despite its potential, the technology faces several significant hurdles. One major challenge is the substantial space requirement: a typical single-family home requiring 100% heat from this system would need approximately 48,000 liters of caustic soda solution, far exceeding the capacity of conventional oil tanks and limiting its application to larger buildings or industrial processes. The initial investment costs are also high, with an estimated payback period of 15-25 years, and claims of cost-effectiveness compared to other storage methods are yet to be independently verified. Furthermore, the technology is not yet market-ready, and current energy market structures often fail to financially incentivize long-term seasonal storage, favoring short-term charge/discharge cycles based on price fluctuations. However, the system offers a promising non-electrical solution for grid stabilization during peak heating periods without relying on rare earth elements.
