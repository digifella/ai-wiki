# Circadian Rhythms

Circadian rhythms are endogenous biological cycles that repeat approximately every 24 hours, regulating sleep-wake patterns and numerous other physiological processes. The term is derived from the Latin words *circa* (around) and *dies* (day). These internal rhythms are generated by the body’s biological clock and are synchronized with external environmental cues, most notably the light-dark cycle, through a process known as entrainment.

## The Master Clock and Entrainment

The suprachiasmatic nucleus (SCN), a small region within the brain's hypothalamus, serves as the master clock in mammals. It receives direct input from the retina regarding ambient light levels, allowing it to adjust the body's internal timing to match the external day-night cycle. This synchronization ensures that physiological functions, such as hormone release, body temperature regulation, and metabolism, occur at optimal times. Disruption of this alignment, often caused by irregular light exposure or shift work, can lead to sleep disorders and other health issues.

## Physiological and Behavioral Impacts

Beyond sleep, circadian rhythms influence a wide array of bodily functions, including alertness, cognitive performance, and immune response. Hormones such as melatonin and cortisol follow distinct circadian patterns, with melatonin levels rising in the evening to promote sleep and cortisol peaking in the morning to facilitate wakefulness. The molecular mechanism underlying these rhythms involves a transcription-translation feedback loop of specific "clock genes" within cells throughout the body. Maintaining regular sleep-wake times and exposure to natural light during the day are primary methods for supporting healthy circadian function.
