### Summary
The video explores the history and characteristics of the Mid-Atlantic accent—a constructed hybrid of American and British English that was widely used in Hollywood films, elite American institutions, and radio broadcasts during the mid-20th century. According to the speaker, the accent was not native to any region but was taught in elite schools, drama academies, and elocution classes to convey sophistication, education, and status.

Key features of the Mid-Atlantic accent included the "disappearing R" (dropping the "r" sound at the end of words like "father" or "car"), the broad "a" sound in words like "bath" or "dance," distinct and crisp "t" sounds, a "hidden h" sound in words starting with "wh," and an extra "y" sound in words like "duty" or "new." Figures like Cary Grant, Katharine Hepburn, and Franklin D. Roosevelt famously spoke with this accent, making it synonymous with power and glamour.

The accent’s decline began with major cultural shifts in the mid-20th century. The end of the studio system in Hollywood, the rise of television bringing everyday speech into living rooms, the post-WWII democratization of higher education, and the counterculture movement of the 1960s all contributed to the rejection of elitist speech. By the 1970s, the Mid-Atlantic accent had lost its status as a sign of education and power, eventually becoming a cinematic marker for pompous characters or past eras.
