# Perfect Scrambled Eggs

Perfect scrambled eggs represent a cooking technique aimed at achieving a creamy, custard-like texture with small, delicate curds. This desirable consistency contrasts with the rubbery, dry, or watery results that come from improper heat management or timing. The technique relies on controlling protein coagulation, which begins at relatively low temperatures: egg whites start to set around 63°C (145°F), while yolks begin coagulating around 65°C (149°F).

## Temperature and Timing

The primary method for achieving perfect scrambled eggs involves cooking at low to medium-low heat, typically between 65–75°C (150–170°F), and stirring frequently to control curd formation. Cooking at higher temperatures accelerates protein bonding, causing large curds to form rapidly and expelling moisture, resulting in a dry texture. Conversely, cooking at too low a temperature risks undercooking the eggs or requiring excessive time at the stove. Most recipes recommend removing eggs from heat slightly before they reach full firmness, as residual heat continues the cooking process.

## Technique Variables

Several factors influence the final texture: the type and amount of fat (butter, cream, or oil) incorporated affects creaminess; the size and frequency of stirring determines curd size; and additions such as cream or milk can slow protein coagulation and extend the cooking window. The ratio of liquid to solids, pre-cooking temperature of the pan, and individual egg freshness also play minor but measurable roles in consistency.
