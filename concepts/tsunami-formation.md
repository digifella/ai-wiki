# Tsunami Formation

Tsunamis are ocean waves generated by sudden, large-scale disturbances that displace significant volumes of water vertically. Unlike wind-driven surface waves, which develop gradually through friction with moving air, tsunamis originate from impulsive events that transfer energy efficiently through the entire water column. This mechanism allows the waves to travel across ocean basins at speeds ranging from 500 to 800 kilometers per hour in deep water. In the open ocean, these waves often have wavelengths exceeding 100 kilometers and may appear as low as one meter in height, making them largely undetectable to ships at sea.

## Primary Generation Mechanisms

The most common cause of tsunamis is seismic activity, particularly undersea earthquakes occurring at subduction zones where tectonic plates converge. When the seafloor undergoes vertical displacement during a megathrust earthquake, it pushes the overlying water column upward or downward, initiating the wave. Other significant sources include volcanic eruptions, which can displace water through explosive force or subsequent collapse of volcanic structures, and submarine landslides triggered by seismic shaking or sediment instability. These events share the critical characteristic of moving a massive volume of water rapidly, distinguishing them from meteorological waves.

## Propagation and Shoaling

As tsunami waves approach shallow coastal waters, their speed decreases due to friction with the seabed, while their wavelength shortens and height increases dramatically. This process, known as shoaling, concentrates the wave's energy into a smaller vertical space, often resulting in a rapid rise in sea level that can inundate coastal areas. The initial wave may not be the largest; subsequent waves can arrive minutes or hours later, with intervals determined by the geometry of the source and the bathymetry of the ocean floor. The total energy of a tsunami is conserved over long distances, allowing it to impact coastlines thousands of kilometers from its origin with minimal loss of intensity.
