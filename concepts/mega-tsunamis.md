# Mega Tsunamis

Mega tsunamis are exceptionally large tsunami events with wave heights exceeding 100 meters, generated by catastrophic geological phenomena rather than typical seismic activity. While conventional tsunamis resulting from seafloor earthquakes usually produce waves of 10–30 meters in height, mega tsunamis are caused by sudden, massive displacements of water triggered by extreme geological events. These rare occurrences represent some of the most powerful water movements in Earth's systems.

## Generation Mechanisms

Mega tsunamis are primarily generated by volcanic flank collapses, where the sudden failure of a volcano's slope displaces an enormous volume of water. The 1888 eruption of Ritter Island in Papua New Guinea exemplifies this process, producing a wave estimated at approximately 100 meters in height. Other potential sources include large submarine landslides, meteor impacts, and caldera collapses. The instantaneous nature of these events—occurring in seconds to minutes—distinguishes them from tsunamis generated by earthquakes, which displace water more gradually through vertical seafloor motion.

## Historical Significance and Impact

Although mega tsunamis are extremely rare on human timescales, geological evidence suggests they have occurred throughout Earth's history. The 1755 Lisbon earthquake and the 2004 Indian Ocean earthquake generated large tsunamis, but mega tsunami events of the scale produced by volcanic collapses are separated by centuries or millennia. Their rarity makes detailed study difficult, though numerical modeling and geological surveys of past events have improved understanding of their dynamics and potential hazards to coastal populations.
