# Tectonic Plate

A tectonic plate is a large, rigid segment of Earth's lithosphere that floats on the semi-fluid asthenosphere beneath it. The lithosphere is divided into several major plates and numerous smaller ones, with boundaries where plates meet marking zones of intense geological activity. These plates are in constant motion, moving [[concepts/assistive-technology|at]] rates typically between 2 and 10 centimeters per year, driven by convection currents in Earth's mantle.

## Plate Interactions and Boundaries

The geological consequences of plate motion depend on how plates interact at their boundaries. Where plates diverge, new oceanic crust forms at mid-ocean ridges through seafloor spreading. Where plates converge, one plate may slide beneath another in a process called subduction, or they may collide, creating mountains and thickened continental crust. Transform boundaries occur where plates slide horizontally past one another. These interactions directly generate most of Earth's [[concepts/earthquakes|earthquakes]] and [[concepts/volcanic-eruptions|volcanic eruptions]], concentrating seismic and volcanic activity along plate margins rather than in plate interiors.

## Historical Significance

The [[concepts/theory|theory]] of [[concepts/tectonic-plate-shifts|plate tectonics]], developed in the 1960s, unified earlier observations about continental drift and seafloor spreading into a comprehensive framework for understanding Earth's surface dynamics. This model explained long-standing puzzles about earthquake and volcano [[concepts/distribution|distribution]], mountain formation, and the apparent fit of continental coastlines across ocean basins.
