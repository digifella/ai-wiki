# Qanats

Qanats are underground channel systems engineered to transport water across arid and semi-arid regions for irrigation and domestic consumption. The system works by tapping into groundwater sources—typically aquifers or springs at higher elevations—and conveying water through gently sloping tunnels to lower-lying agricultural lands or settlements. This design allows gravity to move water reliably over considerable distances while minimizing evaporation losses that would occur in open-surface canals.

## Structure and Construction

A typical qanat consists of a series of vertical shafts connected by a sloped underground tunnel. The shafts, spaced at intervals of 20 to 40 meters, serve multiple functions: they allow workers to excavate and maintain the tunnel, provide ventilation, and enable water to be extracted at intermediate points. The tunnel gradient is carefully engineered to maintain steady flow without causing excessive erosion or sediment buildup. Construction is labor-intensive and requires precise surveying to ensure proper slope and alignment, often accomplished using simple tools and techniques refined over centuries.

## Historical Development and Distribution

Qanats originated in Persia (modern-day Iran) around the first millennium BCE and spread throughout the Middle East, North Africa, and Central Asia. The technology proved particularly valuable in regions where surface water was scarce or unreliable. Major qanat systems still operate in countries including Iran, Afghanistan, Morocco, Tunisia, and Spain (where they are called "acequías"), sustaining agriculture and communities in water-limited environments. Many ancient qanats remain functional today, though maintenance challenges and changing water availability have led to abandonment of some systems.
