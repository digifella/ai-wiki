# Lens Compression

Lens compression is an optical effect where objects at varying distances appear closer together than they physically are. This phenomenon occurs when using longer focal lengths, which have a narrower field of view that alters the perceived spatial relationships within a scene. The effect is not caused by the lens itself changing the distance between objects, but rather by the camera's position relative to the subject and the angle of view, which flattens depth relationships in the final image.

The degree of compression becomes more pronounced with increased focal length and the distance between the camera and the subject. By moving further away from the subject and zooming in, photographers can minimize the apparent gap between foreground and background elements. This technique is often used in portrait photography to create a more flattering look and in landscape photography to emphasize layers of distant terrain.

In the context of panoramic photography and AI agent workflows, this principle is utilized to create wide-angle compositions that retain the spatial flattening characteristics of long lenses. By shooting and stitching multiple images, systems can simulate the look of a telephoto lens across a wider field of view. This allows for the creation of expansive scenes where distant objects appear tightly packed, enhancing the sense of scale and depth without the distortion typical of ultra-wide-angle lenses.
