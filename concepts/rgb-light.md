# RGB Light

RGB light refers to the additive color model based on red, green, and blue wavelengths, which forms the foundation of how digital displays generate color by emitting light. The human eye contains three types of cone cells sensitive to different wavelengths: long (red, ~700 nm), medium (green, ~546 nm), and short (blue, ~435 nm). The brain combines signals from these receptors to perceive the full spectrum of visible colors. By varying the intensity of these three primary wavelengths, digital displays—including screens, projectors, and LEDs—can produce the range of colors visible to humans.

## Lippmann Photography and Structural Color

Lippmann photography presents an alternative approach to color reproduction that does not rely on RGB primaries. This technique, developed by Gabriel Lippmann in 1891, captures color through interference patterns created by light reflecting within a photographic emulsion, encoding color information structurally rather than through pigments or dyes. The resulting images display true color through physical structure, achieving colors by constructive and destructive interference of light waves rather than by mimicking human color perception through three primary channels.

The distinction between RGB reproduction and structural color highlights a fundamental difference in how color can be captured and displayed. RGB-based systems create the appearance of full-color images by exploiting the brain's color perception mechanisms, essentially creating a controlled illusion that matches what the eye interprets as color. Structural color approaches, by contrast, encode color information in the physical properties of materials themselves, producing colors that remain consistent regardless of viewing angle or observer physiology. This distinction raises questions about the nature of color perception and the relationship between how color appears and how it is physically produced.
