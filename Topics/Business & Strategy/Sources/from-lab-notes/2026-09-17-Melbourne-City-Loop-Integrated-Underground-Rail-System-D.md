## Melbourne City Loop: Integrated Underground Rail System Design and Operations
**Clip title:** Melbourne's Underground City Loop - Explained!
**Author / channel:** Taitset
**URL:** https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=3m_NYdwO4FI

### Summary
The [[concepts/melbourne-city-loop|Melbourne City Loop]] is an underground rail line built in stages during the early 1980s, encircling the city's [[concepts/central-business-district|Central Business District]] (CBD). Its primary purpose was to distribute passengers more evenly across the CBD and alleviate congestion at the heavily used [[concepts/flinders-street-station|Flinders Street Station]]. Crucially, the City Loop is not a dedicated subway system but an integrated part of Victoria's broader heavy rail network, a distinction that contributes to its unique and often confusing operational patterns compared to circular rail systems in other cities. The underground section of the loop includes three stations: Flagstaff, Melbourne Central, and Parliament, complementing the above-ground hubs of Flinders Street and Southern Cross. The term "City Loop" primarily refers to the underground lines running along the northern and eastern sides of the CBD.

The loop's unusual design consists of four completely independent, single-track tunnels, each catering to a specific group of suburban lines (Northern, Clifton Hill, Burnley, and Caulfield). Each underground station features two island platforms, one above the other, serving different groups. Physically, these tunnels are not a complete circle but rather "fish-hook" shaped, with one end connecting to Flinders Street and Southern Cross, and the other extending out to the suburbs. Historically, a key operational characteristic was the bi-directional use of these single-track tunnels, with the direction of travel changing midway through the day to accommodate peak hour commutes. This unique system was designed to optimize train movements, allowing trains to run into the loop before Flinders Street in the morning peak and directly to Flinders Street before running out via the loop in the evening peak, also facilitating train stabling at the former Jolimont Yard.

However, this design created significant challenges, making direct travel between certain key stations impossible during specific times of the day, leading to passenger frustration. Recent changes have addressed some of these issues: the Clifton Hill loop was made permanently clockwise in 2013 due to a poorly designed junction at Jolimont that created bottlenecks for counter-clockwise trains. Similarly, the Caulfield loop became permanently counter-clockwise in 2021 to accommodate new, longer HCMT trains, requiring signal adjustments that were only implemented for this direction. Despite these improvements, the Northern and Burnley groups still feature daily direction changes on weekdays, leading to ongoing directional travel limitations and potential confusion for passengers. Compounding this, public transport agencies have struggled to clearly communicate these complex routes, with generic "via the City Loop" signage failing to fully inform passengers of the specific underground section or direction, often leading to a "peering out the window" ritual at Flinders Street to verify a train's path.

In contrast, the Sydney City Circle, built between the 1920s and 1950s, offers a much simpler and more intuitive design. It operates as a double-track loop allowing trains to travel in both directions at all times. Its "partly deflated balloon" shape, with Central Station located south of the CBD, means trains consistently pass through Central as the first stop when entering or the last stop when leaving the city. This design avoids the directional running issues and difficult routes found in Melbourne, providing consistent direct travel options and more evenly distributed capacity. While Melbourne's geographic layout (with Flinders Street centrally located) might have precluded a similar "balloon" design, the video concludes that despite its operational quirks and the resulting confusion, the City Loop remains a vital component of Melbourne's rail network. Future relief is anticipated with the completion of the [[concepts/metro-tunnel|Melbourne Metro Tunnel]], which will divert several lines and free up capacity in the existing loop.

### Video Description & Links
#### Description
The underground City Loop is at the centre of Melbourne's busy suburban railway system, currently operated by Metro Trains Melbourne. It's quite an unusual design, being made up of four completely independent single track tunnels, some of which change direction half way through the day. This video explains the physical layout of the Loop, the reason for the direction change, and some of the other confusing aspects of this part of the network. The Melbourne suburban network is 1600mm/5'3" Broad Gauge, and electrified at 1500v DC. 

This video includes several clips taken out the window of a Hitachi train travelling through the Loop. These were taken during the Hitachi farewell tour, and you can watch the full thing here: https://youtu.be/h6u2n3Hqlww
