# Supernovae

Supernovae are stellar explosions of extraordinary brightness that occur when certain [[concepts/stellar-objects|stars]] reach the end of their lives. These events release enormous amounts of energy across the electromagnetic spectrum, briefly outshining entire galaxies of billions of stars. A single supernova can be as luminous as the Sun [[entities/will|will]] be over its entire 10-billion-year lifetime. Supernovae are crucial cosmic events that seed the universe with heavy elements and can be observed across vast distances, making them valuable tools for cosmological research.

## Types and Mechanisms

Supernovae occur through different physical mechanisms. Type [[concepts/information-architecture|Ia]] supernovae result from [[concepts/binary-stars|binary star systems]] where a white dwarf accretes material from a [[concepts/companion|companion]] star until thermonuclear runaway triggers a thermonuclear explosion. Type II supernovae occur when massive stars ([[concepts/assistive-technology|at]] least eight times the Sun's mass) exhaust their nuclear fuel and undergo gravitational core collapse, rebounding in a catastrophic explosion. Other varieties, including Type Ib and Ic supernovae, involve massive stars that have lost their outer hydrogen layers before exploding.

## Observational Significance

Supernovae observations have provided fundamental insights into cosmic [[concepts/structure|structure]] and expansion. Type Ia supernovae, which have relatively consistent peak brightness, serve as "standard candles" for measuring distances to distant galaxies. Their use in the 1990s provided evidence for cosmic acceleration and the existence of dark energy. Modern observations with instruments like the [[concepts/jwst|James Webb Space Telescope]] continue to detect supernovae in distant and early galaxies, helping astronomers understand stellar populations and the chemical evolution of the universe across [[concepts/cosmic-time|cosmic time]].
