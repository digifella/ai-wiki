# Usb Port Classification

USB ports are categorized by their physical connector form factors and the electrical standards they support. The original USB standard introduced USB-A, a rectangular connector designed primarily for host devices such as computers and power adapters. Conversely, USB-B connectors were developed for peripherals, including printers and external hard drives, creating a clear distinction between host and device roles in early implementations.

As consumer electronics evolved toward more compact designs, the physical limitations of USB-A and USB-B became apparent. This led to the emergence of USB Micro-B, a smaller connector that dominated the mobile phone and portable electronics market for over a decade. Despite these variations in size and shape, each physical connector type remained mechanically incompatible with the others, requiring specific ports for each standard.

The industry eventually moved toward USB-C to address the need for a universal, reversible connector that could handle higher power delivery and data speeds. Unlike its predecessors, USB-C is not merely a physical shape but a specification that defines both the connector geometry and the underlying protocols, allowing for greater flexibility in device design and functionality. This shift has simplified the user experience by reducing the variety of proprietary cables required for different devices.
