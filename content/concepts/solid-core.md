# Solid Core

The [[concepts/inner-core|Earth's inner core]] presents a geophysical puzzle that challenges conventional understanding of matter under extreme conditions. Seismic waves traveling through the inner core exhibit anomalies that cannot be fully explained by standard [[concepts/models|models]] of solid iron under high pressure and temperature. These anomalies include unusual wave propagation patterns and directional variations in seismic velocity that suggest the inner core's [[concepts/structure|structure]] is more complex than previously thought.

## Seismic Evidence

[[concepts/seismic-data|Seismic data]] from [[concepts/earthquakes|earthquakes]] and controlled explosions reveals that P-waves and S-waves behave unexpectedly as they pass through the inner core. The velocity of these waves varies depending on direction, a phenomenon called anisotropy, and shows patterns that differ from what would be expected from a homogeneous iron [[concepts/crystalline-structure|crystalline structure]]. These observations have led researchers to propose that the inner core may contain a previously unknown state of matter or [[concepts/phase|phase]] of iron that has distinct [[concepts/material-properties|physical properties]].

## Proposed Explanations

Scientists have hypothesized that extreme conditions in the inner core—temperatures exceeding 9,000 Kelvin and pressures of approximately 330 to 360 gigapascals—may produce exotic material states or unique crystalline arrangements not stable under normal or laboratory conditions. These novel states of matter could explain the seismic anomalies observed in the region. Understanding the true [[entities/nature|nature]] of the inner core's composition remains an active area of research that bridges geophysics, materials [[concepts/science|science]], and [[concepts/planetary-science|planetary science]].
