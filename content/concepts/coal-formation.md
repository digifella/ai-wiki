# Coal Formation

Coal forms from the accumulated remains of ancient plants that were buried and subjected to heat and pressure over millions of years. This process of coalification transforms plant material into progressively denser forms: peat, lignite, bituminous coal, and anthracite. The vast coal deposits found in geological strata were laid down primarily during specific periods in Earth's history when conditions favored both extensive plant growth and the [[concepts/preservation|preservation]] of organic material in burial environments.

## Connection to the High Oxygen Period

During the [[concepts/high-oxygen-period|High Oxygen Period]], a time of elevated [[concepts/oxygen-saturation|atmospheric oxygen levels]] in Earth's history, conditions were particularly favorable for coal formation. The oxidation of rock surfaces, notably evident in the red-colored iron oxide deposits of [[concepts/red-centre-of-australia|Australia's Red Centre]], occurred simultaneously with the deposition of major coal and oil reserves. This correlation suggests that the geochemical conditions of the High Oxygen Period created an optimal window for the formation and preservation of these fossil fuels, a phenomenon that was not repeated in subsequent geological epochs.

The reddening of rocks during this period resulted from the chemical weathering and oxidation of iron-bearing minerals [[concepts/assistive-technology|at]] the surface, while in deeper burial environments, organic material was being transformed into coal through thermal and compressional processes. This dual process—surface oxidation coupled with subsurface coalification—makes the High Oxygen Period distinctive in the geological record for its role in generating the world's major coal reserves.
