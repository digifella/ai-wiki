# Methane Bubbles

Methane bubbles are gas-filled cavities that form within steel during high-temperature hydrogen attack (HTHA). This degradation mechanism occurs when hydrogen gas penetrates steel at elevated temperatures and chemically reacts with carbon in the material to produce methane gas. Because methane diffuses through steel much more slowly than atomic hydrogen, it accumulates at internal sites—particularly at grain boundaries and around carbide particles—creating localized pressure that generates void-like cavities.

## Formation and Distribution

The formation of methane bubbles is a progressive process driven by the chemical reaction between diffusing hydrogen and the carbides present in the steel microstructure. As methane accumulates, the internal pressure exceeds the yield strength of the surrounding metal, leading to the nucleation and growth of voids. These bubbles typically initiate at grain boundaries and interfaces with carbide particles, where carbon concentration is highest and diffusion pathways are restricted. Over time, the coalescence of these individual bubbles can lead to significant microstructural damage, reducing the mechanical integrity of the steel component.

## Detection and Implications

The presence of methane bubbles is a critical indicator of HTHA damage, which can lead to cracking and eventual failure of pressure vessels and piping systems. Due to the subsurface nature of these cavities, they are commonly detected using ultrasonic nondestructive testing (NDT). Ultrasonic methods can identify the acoustic impedance changes caused by the gas-filled voids, allowing engineers to assess the extent of degradation without dismantling the equipment. Early detection is essential for maintaining safety and preventing catastrophic failures in high-temperature industrial environments.
