# Structural Color

Structural color refers to color produced by the physical [[concepts/structure|structure]] of a material rather than by pigments or dyes. Unlike pigmentary color, which results from the absorption and reflection of [[concepts/light|light]] by chemical compounds, structural color emerges from the way light interacts with microscopic or nanoscopic patterns, layers, and geometries within a material. These structures manipulate light through interference, diffraction, and scattering, creating colors that can appear vivid and sometimes iridescent.

## Lippmann Photography and Structural Color

[[concepts/lippmann-photography|Lippmann photography]] is an early color photography process developed by Gabriel Lippmann in 1891 that captures [[concepts/images|images]] using interference patterns rather than chemical dyes. When light enters a photographic emulsion, it reflects off a silver layer [[concepts/assistive-technology|at]] the back, creating standing wave patterns that record the precise wavelengths of light. Upon development, these interference patterns in the emulsion become capable of reconstructing the original colors when illuminated. Crucially, this means Lippmann photographs contain no actual color pigments—the colors viewers see are structural colors produced by the physical layers within the photographic [[entities/medium|medium]] itself.

## True Color vs. Optical Illusion

The distinction between true color and optical illusion becomes significant in the context of structural color. While structural colors are sometimes characterized as "illusions," they are fundamentally as real as pigmentary colors—both are valid ways that materials produce the perception of color. The key difference lies in mechanism: pigmentary color depends on the material's chemical composition, while structural color depends on its physical [[concepts/architecture|architecture]]. In Lippmann photography, the resulting colors are not illusory but rather a genuine optical phenomenon recorded through the interference properties of light itself, making the image a direct [[concepts/encoding|encoding]] of the light wavelengths that were present during [[concepts/exposure|exposure]].
