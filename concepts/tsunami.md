# Tsunami

A tsunami is a series of large ocean waves generated by the sudden displacement of a significant volume of water. The primary triggers include submarine earthquakes, volcanic eruptions, underwater landslides, and meteorite impacts. Among these causes, undersea earthquakes account for the majority of destructive tsunamis globally. When the seafloor shifts abruptly during seismic activity, it vertically displaces the overlying water column, initiating waves that radiate outward from the epicenter at high speeds.

Tsunamis differ fundamentally from typical wind-driven waves in their physical characteristics and propagation mechanics. While wind waves are surface phenomena with short wavelengths, tsunamis are shallow-water waves with wavelengths that can extend for hundreds of kilometers. This allows them to travel across entire ocean basins at speeds comparable to jet aircraft, losing minimal energy in the process. As the waves approach shallow coastal waters, their speed decreases while their height increases dramatically, a process known as shoaling.

The impact of a tsunami is determined by the magnitude of the initial displacement and the bathymetry of the coastal region. Upon reaching the shore, the wave may manifest as a rapidly rising tide, a breaking wave, or a powerful surge, depending on the local topography. The resulting inundation can cause extensive damage to infrastructure and loss of life. Modern early warning systems utilize seismic data and deep-ocean pressure sensors to detect potential tsunamis and issue alerts to vulnerable coastal communities.
