# Invasive Species

**Invasive species** are non-native organisms whose introduction [[concepts/causes|causes]] or is likely to cause economic or environmental harm or harm to human [[concepts/health|health]]. They disrupt local ecosystems by outcompeting [[concepts/native-flora|native flora]] and fauna, altering habitat structures, and changing nutrient cycles.

## Characteristics & Impact
- **Rapid proliferation**: Lack of natural predators allows [[concepts/exponential-growth|exponential growth]].
- **Resource competition**: Displacement of native species through superior resource acquisition ([[concepts/light|light]], water, nutrients).
- **Habitat alteration**: Modification of soil chemistry, fire regimes, or hydrology.
- **Hybridization**: Genetic dilution of native populations.

## Management Strategies
- **Eradication**: Physical removal or chemical control (herbicides/pesticides).
- **Containment**: Preventing spread to uninfected areas.
- **Biological Control**: Introduction of natural enemies (high risk of secondary invasion).
- **[[concepts/preservation|Restoration]]**: Reintroduction of native species to re-establish [[concepts/ecological-balance|ecological balance]].

## Case Study: Gorse (*Ulex europaeus*) in New Zealand
[[concepts/gorse|Gorse]] is a highly invasive shrub in New Zealand, known for its thorny structure, nitrogen-fixing capabilities, and ability to form dense monocultures that suppress native regeneration. Traditional management involves mechanical removal or herbicide application, often followed by replanting natives.

### Alternative Approach: Utilization in Rewilding
Recent approaches challenge the immediate eradication paradigm by leveraging invasive species as ecological stepping stones.

- **[[entities/hugh-wilson|Hugh Wilson]]’s Methodology**: On [[concepts/banks-peninsula|Banks Peninsula]], botanist Hugh Wilson utilized gorse stands not as targets for immediate destruction, but as nurse crops for [[concepts/ecosystem-restoration|ecosystem restoration]].
- **Ecological Mechanism**:
    - Gorse fixes atmospheric nitrogen, enriching degraded soils.
    - Dense gorse thickets provide shelter and microclimates for native seedling establishment.
    - Allows for gradual [[concepts/ecological-succession|succession]] rather than abrupt removal, which can leave soil vulnerable to erosion or reinvasion.
- **Outcome**: Successful rewilding of [[concepts/degraded-farmland|degraded farmland]] where native [[concepts/biodiversity|biodiversity]] returned beneath and around the gorse canopy, demonstrating that invasive species can sometimes facilitate rather than solely hinder restoration if managed strategically.
