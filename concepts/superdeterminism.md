# Superdeterminism

Superdeterminism is an interpretation of quantum mechanics that seeks to reconcile quantum theory with local realism by rejecting the assumption of statistical independence. In standard quantum experiments, it is typically assumed that the settings of measurement devices are chosen freely and independently of the particles being measured. Superdeterminism argues that this "free choice" assumption is invalid, proposing instead that the state of the particles and the choice of measurement settings are correlated due to common causes in their shared past. This correlation implies that the outcomes of experiments are predetermined, thereby allowing for a local realistic description of quantum phenomena without requiring non-local influences.

The concept was notably developed by physicist Gerard 't Hooft, who argued that if the universe is fundamentally deterministic, then the apparent randomness of quantum mechanics arises from a lack of knowledge about the underlying initial conditions. Under this view, the Bell inequalities, which are violated in quantum experiments and usually cited as proof against local realism, do not rule out local hidden variables because the derivation of these inequalities relies on the assumption that the experimenter's choices are uncorrelated with the particle states. By removing this assumption, superdeterminism suggests that local hidden variable theories can remain viable.

Critics argue that superdeterminism undermines the scientific method itself, as it implies that no experiment can ever truly test a hypothesis if the experimental setup is inherently correlated with the system under study. This leads to concerns about unfalsifiability, as any experimental result can be explained by post-hoc correlations. Despite these philosophical objections, the theory remains a subject of active research in foundational physics, particularly in efforts to formulate a deterministic underlying theory for quantum mechanics, such as cellular automaton interpretations.
