# Water Bath

The water bath, also known as a bain-marie, is a cooking technique in which a container of food is placed inside a larger vessel filled with hot water. The surrounding water provides gentle, indirect heat that distributes evenly around the cooking vessel, preventing rapid temperature fluctuations and hot spots that can damage sensitive foods. This method allows cooks to maintain precise control over temperature, as water heats and cools more gradually than direct flame or oven air.

## How It Works

The physics of the water bath relies on water's thermal properties. Water has a high heat capacity, meaning it absorbs and releases heat slowly and steadily. When a cooking vessel sits in hot water, heat transfers gradually through the walls of the inner container. The maximum temperature of the surrounding water is limited to its boiling point (approximately 100°C at sea level), which prevents the inner food from overheating even if the outer oven or heat source is much hotter.

## Common Applications

Water baths are widely used for cooking custards, crème brûlée, cheesecakes, and other delicate desserts that require gentle, even heating to set properly without curdling or cracking. They are also employed in preparing pâtés, terrines, and some egg-based dishes. In scientific and industrial settings, water baths serve as temperature-controlled environments for heating solutions and conducting experiments that require stable, moderate heat.
