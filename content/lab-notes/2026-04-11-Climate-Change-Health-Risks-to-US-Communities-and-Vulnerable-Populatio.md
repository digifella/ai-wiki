## Climate Change: Health Risks to U.S. Communities and Vulnerable Populations
**Clip title:** how climate affects [[concepts/population-health|community health]] - full video
**Author / channel:** Centers for Disease Control and [[concepts/preventive-care|Prevention]] (CDC)
**URL:** https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=JywsWktvODc

### Summary
The video, "How Climate Affects Community Health," highlights the increasing health risks that a changing climate poses to communities across the [[entities/united-states|United States]]. Scientific consensus indicates that global [[concepts/climate-change|climate change]] is leading to more frequent and severe [[concepts/extreme-weather-events|extreme weather events]], [[concepts/unhealthy-air-quality|unhealthy air quality]], and [[concepts/disease-outbreaks|disease outbreaks]], impacting more people in more places.

The video details several specific health impacts. Extreme heat contributes to [[concepts/heat-stroke|heat stroke]], heat cramps, [[concepts/heat-stress|heat exhaustion]], [[concepts/dehydration|dehydration]], and even death, with pregnant women, individuals with heart or lung conditions, young children, older adults, athletes, and outdoor workers being particularly vulnerable. Severe storms and flooding can cause direct injuries or fatalities, contaminate food and drinking water, expose people to bacteria, viruses, and toxic chemicals, and lead to mold growth. These events also disrupt access to essential healthcare services and inflict significant [[concepts/mental-health|mental health]] tolls on both affected residents and first responders.

Furthermore, changing climate conditions worsen air quality and contribute to the spread of diseases. Warmer temperatures result in longer and stronger pollen seasons, triggering [asthma](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Asthma) attacks and [allergies](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Allergies). They also exacerbate [air pollution](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Air_pollution) by increasing the density of dangerous airborne particles, while more frequent droughts heighten the risk of wildfires that release harmful pollutants. Pests like ticks, mosquitoes, fleas, and rodents thrive in warmer climates, leading to faster multiplication, wider geographical spread, and longer periods of disease transmission for illnesses such as [Lyme disease](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Lyme_disease), [West Nile virus](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/West_Nile_virus), Zika, and [Hantavirus](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Hantavirus). Those who spend extended time outdoors in affected areas are most at risk.

To address these escalating threats, communities are encouraged to prepare and adapt. Strategies include establishing cooling centers, planting trees to mitigate urban heat, and educating residents on personal protection during heatwaves. For storms and flooding, communities should identify vulnerable neighborhoods, upgrade critical infrastructure, and disseminate information on staying safe, such as avoiding flooded roads. To combat air quality issues and pest-borne diseases, health departments can implement air quality alert systems, advise on safe outdoor activity, track disease outbreaks, and educate residents on preventative measures like using insect repellent.

The Centers for Disease Control and Prevention (CDC) supports these efforts through its "Climate-Ready States and Cities Initiative," which utilizes the **BRACE (Building [[concepts/resilience|Resilience]] Against Climate Effects) framework**. This five-step process guides health departments to: 1) identify climate impacts and at-risk populations, 2) quantify associated health problems, 3) assess science-based interventions, 4) develop and oversee adaptation plans, and 5) evaluate their effectiveness for continuous improvement. The overarching message emphasizes the critical need for proactive, coordinated community [[concepts/responses|responses]] to build [[concepts/resilience|resilience]] against the complex health challenges presented by climate change.
