## Why The Dutch Abandoned Australia After Finding it First?

### Summary
**Main Topic:**
The video explores why Dutch sailors in the 17th century discovered and mapped large parts of Australia (which they called "New Holland"), yet chose not to settle or stay there. 

**Key Points Discussed:**
- **The VOC's Mission:** The Dutch East India Company (VOC) was solely focused on profit, specifically the lucrative spice trade in Asia (nutmeg, cloves, pepper, cinnamon).
- **Navigation Shortcuts:** To reach Batavia (modern-day Jakarta) faster, Dutch captains sailed past the Cape of Good Hope and rode the powerful westerly winds known as the "Roaring Forties," but this shortcut often led them to overshoot and crash onto Australia’s harsh, dry west coast.
- **Unfavorable Discoveries:** Dutch captains like Dirk Hartog (1616) and subsequent expeditions found dry scrublands, poor soil, no major rivers, and hostile encounters with Indigenous Australians, leading them to report that Australia had nothing worth having.
- **Tragic Shipwrecks:** Several Dutch ships, notably the *Batavia* in 1629, wrecked off the coast, leading to horrific mutinies, mass murders, and stranded survivors, further cementing the area's bad reputation.
- **The Missing East Coast:** Because of the prevailing westerly winds, Dutch ships consistently hit the west coast and never discovered Australia’s fertile, rainy east coast (where Sydney, Melbourne, and Brisbane are today). That area was later claimed by British navigator James Cook in 1770.

**Conclusions and Takeaways:**
- The Dutch discovered parts of Australia purely by accident as a result of navigational routes designed for speed toward Asian spice markets.
- Because their expeditions repeatedly landed on Australia’s harshest side, the VOC concluded that the continent was not worth colonizing or trading with, leaving its fertile eastern side undiscovered by them. 
- Ultimately, the video suggests that geography and wind patterns—rather than a lack of exploration—determined how and where Australia was charted and settled.

#### Description
In 1606, a small Dutch ship called the Duyfken touched the coast of a continent no European had ever mapped. Over the next decades, Dutch captains charted thousands of kilometers of it and named it New Holland, more than 150 years before Captain Cook. Then they walked away. Why?

This is the story of the Dutch East India Company (the VOC), the spice profits that sent its ships across the Indian Ocean, and the shortcut that kept throwing them at the wrong coastline. Along the way: Dirk Hartog's pewter plate (1616), the wreck of the Batavia (1629) and the massacre that followed, the Vergulde Draeck and the Zuytdorp, Abel Tasman's voyages, and the one side of Australia the Dutch never saw, the east coast, where Sydney, Melbourne and Brisbane stand today.

The Dutch judged Australia by its worst coastline, because that was the only one the wind would show them.

Question for the comments: if the Dutch had found the east coast first, would they have stayed, or would the company still have walked away for nutmeg?
