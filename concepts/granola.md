# Granola

Granola is a breakfast cereal primarily composed of rolled oats, nuts, seeds, and a sweetening agent such as honey or sugar. The ingredients are mixed together and baked until they bind into crunchy clusters and brown. This baking process distinguishes it from other oat-based cereals by creating a textured, toasted product that is often denser and sweeter than traditional oatmeal.

The composition of granola varies widely depending on regional preferences and dietary needs. While oats form the base, common additions include almonds, pecans, sunflower seeds, and dried fruits. Manufacturers and home cooks may adjust the ratio of sweeteners and fats to achieve different levels of crunchiness and flavor profiles, ranging from lightly sweetened to heavily glazed varieties.

Historically, granola originated in the late 19th century as a health food promoted by Dr. John Harvey Kellogg and his brother Will Keith Kellogg at the Battle Creek Sanitarium in Michigan. Initially developed as a digestible alternative to meat-heavy diets, it was part of a broader movement toward vegetarianism and holistic health. Over time, the recipe evolved from a simple, unsweetened oat mixture to the commercially produced, sweetened clusters widely consumed today.

In modern contexts, granola is typically eaten with milk or yogurt, or consumed as a snack on its own. It is also frequently used as a topping for smoothie bowls and desserts. The commercial production of granola has led to significant variations in nutritional content, with some products containing high levels of added sugars and oils, while others emphasize whole grains and minimal processing to align with health-conscious dietary trends.
