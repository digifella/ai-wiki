# Non Collapse

Dark matter particles do not undergo gravitational collapse into black holes or other compact objects in the manner that ordinary matter does. This fundamental difference arises from dark matter's lack of electromagnetic interaction. Ordinary matter, composed of atoms and molecules, can radiate energy away as electromagnetic radiation during gravitational contraction. This energy dissipation allows baryonic matter to lose angular momentum and collapse into dense structures such as stars, planets, and black holes.

In contrast, dark matter interacts primarily through gravity and, potentially, the weak nuclear force, but not electromagnetism. Without electromagnetic interactions, dark matter particles cannot emit photons or other radiative forms of energy to dissipate kinetic energy. Consequently, as dark matter clouds contract under their own gravity, the particles maintain their high velocities and orbital angular momentum, preventing them from settling into a dense, static configuration.

This inability to dissipate energy results in dark matter forming diffuse, extended halos around galaxies rather than compact cores. The gravitational potential well created by these halos influences the dynamics of visible matter, but the dark matter itself remains spread out. This structural distinction is critical for understanding galaxy formation and the large-scale distribution of mass in the universe, as it ensures that dark matter does not clump into the same compact objects as baryonic matter.
