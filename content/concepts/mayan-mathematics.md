# Mayan Mathematics

The [[concepts/mayan-culture|Maya civilization]] developed a sophisticated numerical system between roughly 2000 BCE and the Spanish conquest in the 16th century. Their [[concepts/mathematics|mathematics]] was primarily developed to serve practical needs: tracking time through calendars, managing trade, and performing astronomical observations. The system used a base-20 (vigesimal) notation rather than the base-10 system common in modern Western mathematics, and included the concept of zero—a significant [[concepts/innovation|innovation]] that appeared in few ancient cultures.

## Practical Application and Feynman's Reference

Physicist Richard Feynman referenced Mayan mathematics in discussions about the [[entities/nature|nature]] of mathematics itself. Feynman used the Maya as an example of a civilization that developed mathematical competence not through abstract theoretical understanding, but through concrete [[concepts/problem-solving|problem-solving]]. The Maya needed to calculate calendar cycles and predict astronomical events; their mathematics emerged from these practical requirements rather than from philosophical inquiry into mathematical principles. This observation supported Feynman's broader argument that mathematics functions primarily as a tool for describing and predicting physical phenomena, rather than existing as an abstract system independent of application.

## Historical Significance

Mayan mathematical achievements enabled the creation of one of history's most accurate calendars and allowed for complex architectural and astronomical calculations that remained impressive even after European [[entities/contact|contact]]. However, much detailed knowledge of their mathematical methods was lost following the Spanish conquest and the suppression of Mayan [[concepts/writing|writing]] systems. Modern understanding of Mayan mathematics relies on interpretation of surviving codices and inscriptions, particularly those recording astronomical and calendrical data.
