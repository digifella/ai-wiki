# Social Identity Theory

Social Identity Theory (SIT) is a psychological and sociological framework developed primarily by Henri Tajfel and John Turner in the 1970s and 1980s. The [[concepts/theory|theory]] explains how individuals derive part of their self-concept from their membership in social groups and how this group membership influences behavior, attitudes, and intergroup relations. According to SIT, people tend to categorize themselves and others into groups based on various characteristics such as ethnicity, nationality, profession, or shared interests.

## In-group Bias and Group Behavior

A central premise of Social Identity Theory is that individuals are motivated to maintain a positive self-image by enhancing the status of groups to which they belong. This [[concepts/motivation|motivation]] often leads to in-group favoritism, where people view their own group more favorably than out-groups, sometimes regardless of objective differences in quality or merit. The theory demonstrates that even arbitrary or minimal group distinctions can trigger this bias, suggesting that group identification operates at a fundamental psychological level.

## Applications and Implications

Social Identity Theory has been applied across numerous fields including organizational behavior, [[concepts/conflict|conflict]] resolution, education, and [[concepts/population-health|public health]]. The theory helps explain phenomena such as prejudice, discrimination, group [[concepts/cohesion|cohesion]], and intergroup conflict. It also provides insight into how social movements form and how collective identity shapes individual action. Understanding SIT has practical implications for reducing intergroup tensions and designing more inclusive social environments.
