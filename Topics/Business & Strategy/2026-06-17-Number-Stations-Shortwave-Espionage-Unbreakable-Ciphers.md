## Number Stations: Shortwave Espionage, Unbreakable Ciphers, Covert Operations

### Summary
The video "[[entities/number-stations|Number Stations]]: The Worst Kept Secret" explores the intriguing world of number stations, comparing them to the unsettling tones of national alert systems like the Emergency Broadcast System (EBS) and Emergency Alert System (EAS). The host highlights how these official alert systems are designed to be deliberately jarring with loud, dissonant two-toned alerts and often primitive [[concepts/text-to-speech-model|text-to-speech]] voices to grab [[concepts/attention-mechanisms|attention]] during emergencies. Number stations, however, predate these systems by decades and operate under a veil of mystery, broadcasting continuous or scheduled numerical sequences, tones, and sometimes even children's voices.

The core technology enabling number stations is [[concepts/shortwave-radio|shortwave radio]]. Initially deemed unreliable for direct communication, shortwave signals proved to be excellent for "skywave" or "skip" propagation, bouncing off Earth's ionosphere to travel thousands of miles across continents and oceans. This characteristic makes the sender's and receiver's location difficult to trace. Early hobbyists and enthusiasts scanning the shortwave spectrum discovered these unusual broadcasts, which often began with an identifiable sound (like a folk song or character [[concepts/tone|voice]]) followed by groups of numbers recited by human or synthetic voices.

The codes used in number stations are designed to be virtually unbreakable, typically employing either book ciphers (where numbers correspond to words in a pre-arranged book) or, more commonly, one-time pads. A one-time pad involves a unique, truly random sequence of letters or numbers used as a key for encryption and decryption, with both the sender and receiver possessing identical copies. Critically, each part of the pad is used only once and then destroyed, making the message mathematically impossible to decrypt without that specific, ephemeral key. This method, combined with the untraceable nature of shortwave radio, ensures that even if a message is intercepted, its content remains secret, and the recipient cannot be identified solely from the broadcast.

While the codes themselves are unbreakable without the one-time pad, the broadcast locations of some number stations have been successfully triangulated by amateur radio enthusiasts. For instance, "The Lincolnshire Poacher" was tracked to a British Royal Air Force base in Cyprus, and the "Yosemite Sam" station was traced to a U.S. military contractor's site, leading to its swift shutdown and removal from public records. These incidents, alongside the persistent operation of stations like [[entities/russia|Russia]]'s "The Buzzer" (broadcasting continuously for over 50 years), provide strong evidence that these are government-operated facilities for espionage.

The video concludes that number stations are indeed a "worst-kept secret" because their primary purpose is undeniably to communicate with spies. Despite advances in digital communication, their continued use stems from their unique advantages: untraceable shortwave broadcasts for recipients, unbreakable one-time pad encryption, and the tactic of "hiding in plain sight" as an anomalous radio signal. Governments prefer to pretend these stations don't exist, as confirmed by an anonymous UK government spokesperson who stated these stations are "what you suppose they are" and "not for public consumption."
