# Retro Computing

[[concepts/historical-computing|Retro computing]] refers to the use, collection, and restoration of older computer [[concepts/hardware|hardware]] and [[concepts/software|software]] systems, typically from previous decades. This practice encompasses everything from maintaining functional vintage machines to [[concepts/running|running]] period-appropriate software on original equipment. Enthusiasts engage with [[concepts/legacy-computing|retro computing]] for various reasons: historical interest, nostalgia, educational purposes, or appreciation for the [[concepts/design|design]] constraints and solutions of earlier computing eras.

## Hardware and Restoration

The hardware aspect of retro computing involves acquiring, repairing, and maintaining vintage computers, peripherals, and components. Common platforms include personal computers from the 1970s through 1990s (such as [[entities/apple|Apple]] II, Commodore 64, and early [[entities/ibm|IBM]] PCs), [[concepts/gaming|gaming]] consoles, and minicomputers. Restoration projects often require sourcing replacement parts, troubleshooting failed components, and adapting systems for modern [[concepts/power|power]] supplies or [[entities/storage|storage]] media while preserving original functionality.

## Emulation and Software Preservation

Emulation plays a significant role in retro computing [[concepts/accessibility|accessibility]]. Software emulators recreate the behavior of older systems on contemporary hardware, allowing users to run historical software without owning original equipment. This approach supports software [[concepts/preservation|preservation]] efforts, as many programs and digital media from earlier computing eras face obsolescence risk due to physical degradation or technical incompatibility with modern systems. Emulation also enables broader study of [[concepts/history-of-computing|computing history]] without the expense and expertise required for hardware restoration.
