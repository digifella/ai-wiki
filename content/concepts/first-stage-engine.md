# First Stage Engine

A first stage engine is a propulsion unit designed to operate during the initial [[concepts/phase|phase]] of a launch vehicle's flight, typically providing [[concepts/engine-thrust|thrust]] to lift the vehicle and its payload from the launch pad through the lower atmosphere. First stage engines are generally the largest and most powerful engines on a launch vehicle, as they must overcome the vehicle's full mass and gravitational drag during the most fuel-intensive portion of ascent.

## Continued Operation and Payload Protection

In some launch vehicle designs, first stage engines continue operating after [[concepts/payload-fairing-separation|payload fairing separation]]—the point [[concepts/assistive-technology|at]] which protective aerodynamic covers surrounding the payload are jettisoned. This extended burn capability can provide structural support and continued acceleration to the [[concepts/payload-structure|payload structure]] even if it sustains damage during fairing separation or other events in the lower atmosphere. By maintaining thrust beyond the typical first stage cutoff point, the vehicle can potentially maintain a trajectory that allows the payload to reach usable orbit despite degraded conditions.

## Design Considerations

The decision to implement extended first stage engine operation involves trade-offs in [[concepts/vehicle-design|vehicle design]] and [[concepts/cost|cost]]. Systems like [[concepts/automated-information-pipelines|OpenClaw]], which provide mechanical payload protection during ascent, represent alternative approaches to ensuring payload [[concepts/integrity|integrity]]. The relative expense and complexity of different protection strategies [[concepts/power|influence]] the engineering choices made for specific launch vehicle architectures.
