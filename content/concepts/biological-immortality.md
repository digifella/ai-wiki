# Biological Immortality

Biological immortality refers to the theoretical absence of senescence—the biological process of aging—in living organisms. An organism possessing biological immortality would not experience age-related decline and could [[entities/theoretically-media|theoretically]] live indefinitely, though it would remain vulnerable to death from injury, disease, predation, or environmental factors. This contrasts with popular conceptions of immortality, which typically imply invulnerability to all forms of death.

## Natural Examples

Several organisms demonstrate negligible senescence under natural conditions. Certain species of jellyfish, such as *Turritopsis dohrnii*, can revert to earlier life stages in a process called transdifferentiation. Some hydra and planarian flatworms show no measurable increase in mortality rate with age. Certain trees, including bristlecone pines and some clonal plants, can persist for thousands of years. However, even these organisms eventually succumb to environmental stressors, disease, or accident rather than inherent biological aging.

## Research and Limitations

Scientific research into biological immortality has explored various mechanisms, including [[concepts/telomerase-activation|telomere maintenance]], cellular senescence suppression, and DNA repair enhancement. However, attempts to artificially extend lifespan indefinitely have encountered significant obstacles. A 20-year Japanese study on mammalian [[concepts/serial-cloning|serial cloning]] found that repeated cloning cycles led to genetic deterioration and a biological dead end, highlighting the complexity of circumventing aging processes. Current evidence suggests that while aging may be modifiable to some degree, true biological immortality remains unachieved in higher organisms.
