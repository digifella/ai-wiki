# Retro Computing

[[concepts/historical-computing|Retro computing]] refers to the use, restoration, and [[concepts/preservation|preservation]] of computer hardware and software from earlier eras, typically spanning the 1970s through 1990s, though definitions vary among practitioners. The field encompasses both maintaining original machines in working condition and executing period-appropriate software, whether on authentic hardware or through emulation. As [[concepts/history-of-computing|computing history]] gained recognition as a subject worthy of preservation, [[concepts/legacy-computing|retro computing]] evolved from niche hobby activity into a more established practice with dedicated communities, museums, and archives.

## Hardware and Restoration

Retro computing practitioners work with older computer systems including personal computers, [[concepts/gaming|gaming]] consoles, mainframes, and minicomputers. Restoration efforts involve repairing degraded components, replacing failed power supplies and capacitors, and sourcing replacement parts that may no longer be manufactured. Some enthusiasts focus on maintaining machines in original condition, while others undertake modifications or upgrades. The challenge of sourcing spare parts has created secondary markets and motivated some practitioners to design and manufacture replacement components.

## Emulation and Software Preservation

Beyond physical restoration, retro computing includes running legacy software through emulation on modern systems. Emulators replicate the behavior of older hardware architectures, allowing users to [[concepts/experience|experience]] historical software without requiring original equipment. This approach addresses both preservation concerns—ensuring software remains accessible as original hardware becomes obsolete—and practical limitations, as authentic systems may be rare, expensive, or require significant space and maintenance.

## Community and Cultural Significance

Retro computing communities share knowledge through forums, publications, and events dedicated to specific platforms or eras. The practice serves multiple purposes: technical interest in older architectures, nostalgic engagement with formative computing experiences, and serious historical documentation of computing's development. Museums and [[entities/tomasz-janowski|academic]] institutions increasingly recognize retro computing collections as important cultural artifacts worthy of systematic preservation.
