# Wimps

Weakly Interacting Massive Particles (WIMPs) are hypothetical particles proposed as leading candidates for dark matter, the invisible substance estimated to comprise approximately 85% of the matter in the universe. Unlike ordinary baryonic matter, WIMPs interact primarily through gravity and the weak nuclear force, making them extraordinarily difficult to detect through conventional means. Their name derives from their defining properties: they possess significant mass while interacting only weakly with normal matter.

Direct detection experiments attempt to observe WIMPs by measuring the recoil of atomic nuclei when struck by a passing WIMP. These underground detectors, such as those located in deep mines or under ice, are shielded from cosmic rays to minimize background noise. Despite decades of increasingly sensitive searches, no definitive signal has been confirmed, leading to tighter constraints on the possible mass and interaction cross-section of these particles.

An alternative detection method involves indirect observation through astrophysical signals. If WIMPs are their own antiparticles, they may annihilate upon collision, producing standard model particles such as gamma rays, positrons, and neutrinos. Researchers analyze data from space-based telescopes and ground-based observatories for excess gamma-ray emissions originating from dense dark matter regions, such as the Galactic Center or dwarf spheroidal galaxies, to find evidence of this annihilation process.

The theoretical framework for WIMPs is often motivated by supersymmetry, a proposed extension of the Standard Model of particle physics. In many supersymmetric models, the lightest supersymmetric particle is stable and possesses the necessary mass and interaction strength to account for the observed dark matter density. While the lack of experimental confirmation has prompted interest in alternative candidates like axions or sterile neutrinos, WIMPs remain a central focus of both particle physics and cosmological research.
