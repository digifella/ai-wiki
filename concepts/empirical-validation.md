# Empirical Validation

[[concepts/experimental-validation|Empirical validation]] is the process of testing theoretical predictions against observations or experiments in the natural [[entities/earth|world]]. It forms the foundation of the [[concepts/scientific-method|scientific method]] by ensuring that [[concepts/ideas|ideas]] about how nature works are grounded in measurable reality rather than speculation alone. Without empirical validation, even the most elegant mathematical theories remain unproven hypotheses.

## Feynman's Three-Step Framework

Physicist [[entities/richard-feynman|Richard Feynman]] articulated a practical approach to empirical validation often summarized as a three-step cycle. The first step involves formulating a hypothesis or "guess" regarding the behavior of a physical system. This is followed by the second step, where the implications of the guess are worked out through calculation or [[concepts/reasoning|logical deduction]] to produce specific, [[concepts/observable-consequences|testable predictions]].

The final and most critical step is comparing the results of these computations with the results of nature, obtained through [[concepts/scientific-experiment|experiment]] or observation. If the calculated predictions match the [[concepts/experimental-evidence|experimental data]], the hypothesis gains credibility; if they diverge, the hypothesis is considered incorrect or incomplete. This [[concepts/iterative-refinement|iterative process]] emphasizes that the ultimate arbiter of scientific truth is not mathematical beauty or authority, but agreement with observed reality.
