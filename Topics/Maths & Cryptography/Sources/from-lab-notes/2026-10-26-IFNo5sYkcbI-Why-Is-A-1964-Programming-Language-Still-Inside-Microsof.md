## Why Is A 1964 Programming Language Still Inside Microsoft Excel?

### Summary
Here is a summary of the video:

**Main Topic:**
The video explores the history, impact, and lasting legacy of the BASIC programming language, from its creation in 1964 by Dartmouth math professors John Kemeny and Thomas Kurtz to its widespread adoption in the home computer era and influence on modern software.

**Key Points:**
*   **Origins:** BASIC was created at Dartmouth College to be a simple, accessible programming language for students who were not scientists or engineers, utilizing time-sharing systems to allow multiple users to access a single computer.
*   **Adoption and Spread:** Despite initial criticism from computer scientists who viewed it as too simplistic, BASIC was embraced by hardware manufacturers and hobbyists. It became a staple of early home computers like the Apple II, Commodore PET, and Radio Shack TRS-80.
*   **The Microsoft Connection:** Bill Gates and Paul Allen developed Microsoft BASIC for the Altair 8800, which helped launch Microsoft and made BASIC the industry standard for home computers in the 1980s.
*   **Persistence:** Despite having many variations and facing criticism for encouraging unstructured code ("spaghetti code"), descendants of BASIC, such as Visual Basic and Visual Basic for Applications (VBA), continue to be widely used in software like Microsoft Excel.

**Conclusions/Takeaways:**
BASIC revolutionized computing by democratizing programming, making it accessible to everyday users rather than just experts. Its philosophy of free access and ease of use laid the foundation for the personal computer boom and continues to influence software development today.

#### Description
If you ever typed 10 PRINT "HELLO" and 20 GOTO 10 on a computer in a department store and walked away, you were using BASIC, a language two Dartmouth professors created in 1964 and gave away.

This is the story of BASIC. It begins with John Kemeny, a teenage refugee from Budapest who fed punched cards into IBM machines at Los Alamos and later worked as Albert Einstein's assistant, and Thomas Kurtz, who wanted a name that was "simple but not simple-minded." At 4 a.m. on May 1, 1964, in the basement of College Hall, Kemeny and a student typed RUN on neighboring terminals, and time-sharing and BASIC were born.

From there it went to GE time-sharing, HP and DEC minicomputers, David Ahl's BASIC Computer Games, a Teletype bought with rummage sale money at Lakeside School, and the Altair BASIC that Bill Gates and Paul Allen wrote before they owned an Altair. Then came the Open Letter to Hobbyists, Applesoft, Commodore's WAIT 6502,1 easter egg, Dijkstra's "mentally mutilated," True BASIC, Visual Basic and the VBA that still runs inside Excel.
