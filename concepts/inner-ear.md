# Inner Ear

The **inner ear** (labyrinth) is the innermost part of the vertebrate ear, responsible for hearing and balance. It consists of the **cochlea** for auditory transduction and the **vestibular system** for equilibrium.

## Anatomy
- **Cochlea**: A spiral-shaped, fluid-filled structure containing the Organ of Corti, where mechanical sound waves are converted into neural signals via hair cells.
- **Vestibular System**: Includes the semicircular canals and otolith organs (utricle and saccule) that detect rotational and linear acceleration.
- **Fluids**: Contains Endolymph (high K+) within the membranous labyrinth and Perilymph (high Na+) in the bony labyrinth.

## Function
- **Auditory Transduction**: Sound vibrations travel through the Ossicles to the Oval Window, creating pressure waves in the cochlear fluid that stimulate hair cells.
- **Neural Transmission**: Signals are transmitted via the [[concepts/auditory-nerve]] (Cranial Nerve VIII) to the brainstem and auditory cortex.
- **Balance**: Maintains posture and spatial orientation by detecting head position and [[concepts/exercise|movement]].

## Clinical & Technological Context
- **Hearing Loss**: Damage to hair cells or the auditory nerve results in sensorineural hearing loss, which is often permanent.
- **[[concepts/preservation|Restoration]] Technologies**:
	- [[concepts/cochlear-implant|Cochlear Implant]] Development: From [[concepts/electrical-stimulation|Electrical Stimulation]]
	- Cochlear implants bypass damaged hair cells by directly stimulating the auditory nerve with electrical impulses, allowing perception of sound for individuals with severe to profound hearing loss.
