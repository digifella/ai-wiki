# USB Port Classification

USB ports and cables have evolved significantly since the original standard's introduction, with modern USB-C representing a substantial shift in connector design and capability. Unlike earlier USB [[concepts/open-standards|standards]] that relied on distinct connector types (USB-A, Micro-USB), USB-C uses a single reversible connector form factor. This unified approach was intended to simplify [[concepts/user-experience-design|user experience]], but the underlying complexity of USB specifications has created considerable confusion in the market.

## Cable Specifications and Capabilities

[[concepts/usb-20|USB-C cables]] vary widely in their actual capabilities despite using identical physical connectors. Cables differ in their power delivery capacity, data transfer speeds, and supported protocols. A cable may support USB 2.0 speeds (480 Mbps), [[concepts/usb-30|USB 3.0]] (5 Gbps), USB 3.1 (10 Gbps), or newer standards, while also varying in power delivery ratings from basic charging to high-wattage specifications suitable for laptops. The physical appearance of cables provides no reliable indicator of these specifications, making it difficult for consumers to identify compatible cables for their devices.

## Market Challenges

The lack of standardized labeling and clear differentiation between cable types has led to widespread frustration among users attempting to purchase reliable USB-C cables. Manufacturers often omit specifications from packaging, and marketing [[concepts/terminology|terminology]] can be misleading or inconsistent. This has resulted in [[concepts/scenarios|scenarios]] where users purchase cables that are incompatible with their devices or lack sufficient power delivery for their intended use, despite the cables meeting the USB-C physical standard.
