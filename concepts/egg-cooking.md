# Egg Cooking

Egg cooking involves applying heat to transform the proteins and fats within the egg, resulting in diverse textures and flavors. The process relies on the differential [[concepts/denaturation|denaturation]] temperatures of egg components: egg white proteins typically denature between 60–65°C, while yolk proteins require higher temperatures of approximately 65–70°C. This thermal gap allows for precise control over the final [[concepts/logical-consistency|consistency]], enabling cooks to achieve states ranging from soft, runny yolks with set [[concepts/whites|whites]] to fully firm structures. [[concepts/success|Success]] in egg [[concepts/preparation|preparation]] depends primarily on managing three variables: temperature, duration, and the inclusion of fats or liquids.

Boiling is a fundamental method where eggs are submerged in hot water, with timing dictating the degree of doneness. Soft-boiled eggs feature liquid yolks and barely set whites, while hard-boiled eggs have fully coagulated whites and yolks. Beyond boiling, other techniques include scrambling, which involves constant agitation to create small curds, and [[concepts/seafood-poaching|poaching]], where eggs are cooked in simmering water without their shells. Each method exploits the same underlying protein chemistry but applies different mechanical and thermal stresses to alter the outcome.

The addition of ingredients such as butter, milk, or acid can further modify the cooking process. Fats coat protein strands to inhibit coagulation, leading to tenderer textures, while acids can lower the pH and accelerate protein setting. Understanding these interactions allows for consistent results across various culinary applications, from breakfast dishes to baked goods, ensuring the desired [[concepts/payload-structure|structural integrity]] and mouthfeel are achieved.
