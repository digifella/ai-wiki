### Summary
The video provides a compelling historical overview of the Japanese [[concepts/shinobi|Shinobi]], aiming to differentiate their reality from the popular, often supernatural, "ninja" myth. It begins by illustrating a fictional 1580 [[concepts/assassination|assassination]] attempt on warlord [[entities/oda-nobunaga|Oda Nobunaga]] by a stealthy figure, only to reveal that such a killer would have been a doctor or spy, not a theatrical martial artist with smoke bombs and throwing stars. The true Shinobi, or Shinobi no Mono (person of stealth), emerged from the chaotic Sengoku period in mountainous regions like Iga and Koka, where villagers developed sophisticated espionage, ambush, and stealth tactics to protect their autonomy amidst warring feudal lords (Daimyo).

These tactical Shinobi gained a fearsome reputation, particularly in Iga. Their [[concepts/skills|skills]] were put to the ultimate test when Oda Nobunaga, aiming to unify Japan, invaded Koka and then Iga in the late 16th century. Though Oda's son failed in an initial attack, Oda himself launched a massive invasion in 1581, burning villages and temples. The Iga warriors, using their Shinobi tricks like fake reinforcements and night raids, fiercely resisted but were eventually overwhelmed. However, Oda's demise came not from a mythical ninja, but from a disloyal subordinate. Following Oda's death, his ally Tokugawa Ieyasu, needing to escape enemy territory, was famously guided through the perilous Iga mountains by Hattori Hanzo and his Iga/Koka samurai. Their success, relying on local knowledge and alliances rather than supernatural abilities, cemented Tokugawa's path to becoming Shogun.

With Tokugawa's ascension, the Edo period brought two centuries of peace and stability to Japan. The need for overt guerrilla warfare diminished, but the Shogunate still required discreet intelligence. Descendants of the Iga and Koka Shinobi were employed as "castle detectives" or secret police in Edo. These Shinobi no Mono blended into society as servants or priests, gathering intelligence on feudal lords and uncovering conspiracies, like the audacious plot by Marubashi Chuya to burn Edo castle. They were integral to maintaining the Shogunate's iron-fisted stability through surveillance and repression, embodying stealth and quiet [[concepts/power|influence]] rather than direct assassination.

The video concludes by explaining the divergence between historical Shinobi and the modern ninja myth. While the Shinobi as a professional class eventually faded with Japan's forced opening to the West in the mid-19th century and the subsequent Meiji Restoration, their legend was just beginning. During the peaceful Edo period, Kabuki theater and literature romanticized their exploits. In the 20th century, particularly after World War II, this mythology was further amplified through movies, manga, and popular culture, introducing iconic but historically inaccurate elements such as the straight ninja sword, throwing stars (shuriken), and exaggerated martial arts (ninjutsu). Thus, the "ninja" widely recognized today is a romanticized, relatively recent construct, fundamentally different from the adaptable and pragmatic intelligence gatherers and guerrilla fighters known as Shinobi.
