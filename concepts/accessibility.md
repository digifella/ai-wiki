# Accessibility

Accessibility in [[concepts/user-experience-design|user experience design]] refers to the practice of creating products, systems, and digital content that are usable by people with diverse abilities and disabilities. This includes individuals with visual, auditory, motor, and cognitive impairments, as well as those experiencing temporary or situational limitations such as a broken arm or use of a product in bright sunlight. Accessibility is most effectively integrated throughout the entire design and development process from the outset, rather than being addressed as an afterthought or bolted on after initial development.

## Core Principles

[[concepts/inclusive-design|Accessible design]] operates on several foundational principles. **Perceivable** design ensures information is presented in ways users can recognize through their available senses—for example, providing text alternatives for images and captions for videos. **Operable** systems allow users to navigate and interact through various input methods, not just a mouse or keyboard. **Understandable** interfaces use clear language, predictable navigation, and straightforward [[concepts/instructions|instructions]]. **Robust** systems function reliably across different assistive technologies and devices.

## Implementation and Impact

Implementing accessibility requires consideration of specific needs and technologies. Screen readers help blind and low-[[concepts/computer-vision|vision]] users navigate digital content, while keyboard navigation accommodates those unable to use a mouse. [[concepts/color-contrast|Color contrast]] ratios ensure readability for people with color [[entities/visually-impaired|blindness]] or low vision. Captions and transcripts benefit deaf and hard-of-hearing users, but also help in noisy environments or when [[concepts/audio-modality|audio]] cannot be played.

Designing for accessibility often benefits all users. Clear navigation aids people with cognitive disabilities but also helps users in unfamiliar contexts. Larger text helps aging users and those on [[concepts/portable-devices|mobile devices]]. Simple, direct language benefits non-native speakers and people with cognitive differences alike. Rather than a specialized practice, accessibility is increasingly recognized as fundamental to good user experience design.
