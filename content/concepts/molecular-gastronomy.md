# Molecular Gastronomy

Molecular gastronomy is the application of scientific principles and techniques to understand and manipulate food during cooking and [[concepts/preparation|preparation]]. It bridges [[concepts/culinary-arts|culinary arts]] with chemistry and [[concepts/physics|physics]], examining how ingredients behave under different conditions of heat, pressure, and chemical interaction. Rather than following tradition alone, practitioners use scientific knowledge to control outcomes and develop new [[concepts/cooking-methods|cooking methods]].

## Core Techniques and Methods

Common techniques in molecular gastronomy include spherification (creating gel spheres from liquid), gelification (transforming liquids into gels), foams created through the introduction of air, and the use of hydrocolloids and emulsifiers to alter [[concepts/texture|texture]] and [[concepts/logical-consistency|consistency]]. Many practitioners employ precision temperature control, such as sous vide cooking, to achieve consistent results. The field also encompasses the study of how flavors develop and interact, and how chemical reactions like the [[concepts/maillard-reaction|Maillard reaction]] contribute to taste and aroma.

## Applications and Context

Molecular gastronomy emerged as a formal discipline in the 1980s and gained prominence through high-end restaurants that experimented with unconventional presentations and cooking methods. Beyond fine dining, the principles have practical applications in food science, product development, and understanding [[concepts/nutrition|nutrition]] and digestion. The approach has also generated wider interest in how everyday cooking relies on chemical and physical processes that can be deliberately controlled or modified.
