A strategic pivot represents a fundamental shift in a company's business strategy, direction, or core focus. Unlike incremental adjustments to existing operations, a pivot signifies a significant change in how an organization creates value, targets its customer base, or competes within its market. This transformation often involves entering new markets, abandoning established product lines, adopting entirely new business models, or reallocating substantial resources to different areas of the organization.

## Drivers and Context

Strategic pivots are typically driven by external pressures such as technological disruption, changing consumer preferences, or intense competitive dynamics. Internal factors, including the failure of current product-market fit or the discovery of new opportunities during research and development, also necessitate such shifts. The decision to pivot is usually a response to data indicating that the current trajectory is unsustainable or suboptimal for long-term growth.

## Implementation and Risks

Executing a pivot requires significant organizational agility and often involves redefining key performance indicators to align with the new strategic direction. While pivots can revitalize a stagnant business or capture emerging market segments, they carry substantial risks, including loss of brand equity, employee resistance, and financial uncertainty. Successful pivots depend on clear communication, rapid iteration, and the ability to leverage existing assets in novel ways to maintain stakeholder confidence.
