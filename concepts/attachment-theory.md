# Attachment Theory

Attachment [[concepts/theory|Theory]] is a psychological framework primarily developed by [[entities/chef-john|John]] Bowlby in the 1950s. It posits that the early emotional bonds formed between infants and their primary caregivers are critical for healthy emotional and social development. Bowlby viewed these bonds not merely as byproducts of feeding or comfort, but as an [[concepts/adaptationist-theory|evolutionary adaptation]] designed to ensure the survival of the child through proximity-seeking behaviors that offer [[concepts/secure|protection]] from threats.

The theory distinguishes between different patterns of attachment, such as secure, anxious-ambivalent, and avoidant styles, which arise from the [[concepts/logical-consistency|consistency]] and responsiveness of caregiving. These early interactions create internal working models—mental representations of self and others—that guide an individual's expectations and behaviors in future [[concepts/relationships|relationships]] throughout life. Subsequent research by Mary Ainsworth further expanded the framework by identifying specific behavioral [[concepts/indicators|indicators]] of attachment [[concepts/security|security]] through observational studies.

While originally focused on child development, the theory has been extended to understand adult romantic relationships, personality formation, and psychopathology. It suggests that the quality of early attachment influences an individual's ability to regulate emotions, form trusting relationships, and cope with [[concepts/stress|stress]] in adulthood. The framework remains a foundational concept in developmental [[concepts/psychology|psychology]], psychiatry, and social work, providing a lens for understanding the long-term impact of early relational experiences.
