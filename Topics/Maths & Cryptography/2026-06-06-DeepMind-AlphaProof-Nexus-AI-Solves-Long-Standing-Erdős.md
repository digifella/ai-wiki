## DeepMind AlphaProof Nexus: AI Solves Long-Standing Erdős Math Problems
**Clip title:** [[concepts/2026-04-29-google-deepmind|DeepMind]]’s New AI Found A Strange New Way To Think
**Author / channel:** Two Minute Papers
**URL:** https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=Dkqzqw8rxXI

### Summary
This video discusses [[concepts/2026-04-29-google-deepmind|DeepMind]]'s recent breakthrough with its [[concepts/ai-system|AI system]], [[concepts/alphaproof-nexus|AlphaProof Nexus]], in solving long-standing [[concepts/mathematical-problems|mathematical problems]]. The system successfully tackled 9 out of 353 attempted [[concepts/erdős|Erdős]] problems, which are decades-old open mathematical conjectures left by the legendary Hungarian mathematician [[entities/paul|Paul]] [[concepts/erdős|Erdős]]. Despite a high "failure rate" of 97.5% on the attempted problems, the video emphasizes that solving even a few of these problems, some of which have remained unsolved for 56 years, for a mere couple of hundred dollars per problem, is an "incredibly good" and significant achievement in [[concepts/mathematics|mathematics]].

The video addresses common criticisms that these AI solutions aren't "fundamentally new." It counters this by presenting a timeline of AI's progress in [[concepts/mathematics|mathematics]]: from GPT-3 struggling with basic addition four years ago, to systems grappling with high school math competitions two years ago, winning mathematical Olympiad [[concepts/major-gold-deposit|gold]] a year ago, and now, solving 50-year-old open problems. This rapid advancement highlights an [[concepts/exponential-growth|exponential growth]] curve, which the presenter refers to as "The First Law of Papers" – suggesting that current limitations should not overshadow future potential, as AI capabilities are expanding rapidly.

The core [[concepts/innovation|innovation]] behind AlphaProof Nexus lies in its "tournament system." A mathematician first formalizes an open problem in [[concepts/lean|Lean]], a mathematical [[concepts/proof|proof]] language. Then, a Prover Subagent (an LLM combined with AlphaProof) attempts to construct a [[concepts/proof|proof]]. This proof is then checked by a Proof Validator, which, if it finds errors, not only rejects the proof but also explains *why* it's incorrect. Crucially, a cheaper Rater Subagent (another AI) evaluates multiple proposed solutions, assigning them ELO scores, much like a chess ranking system. This subagent selects the "better" [[concepts/solution|solution]] from a pair, even if both are technically incorrect, based on its perceived quality. This [[concepts/iterative-refinement|iterative process]] allows the system to continuously evolve and refine proofs, building a "reliable system out of unreliable parts" until a formally verified [[concepts/solution|solution]] is achieved.

While the system's achievements are lauded, the video also acknowledges limitations, including a selection bias where only problems easier to formalize were tested (350 out of 1200 total [[concepts/erdős-problems|Erdős problems]]). Furthermore, smaller [[concepts/ai-models|AI models]] solved [[concepts/concept-of-nothingness|zero]] problems, indicating that a "beefy AI [[concepts/assistive-technology|at]] the core" is still necessary. The key takeaway from this development is a shift in the [[concepts/philosophy|philosophy]] of [[concepts/ai-research|AI research]]: instead of solely focusing on making individual [[concepts/ai-models|AI models]] smarter, the emphasis is now on creating robust "algorithmic harnesses" or "multi-[[entities/agent|agent]] [[concepts/loops|loops]]" around existing AI models. This approach leverages the strengths of multiple agents and iterative refinement to guide even less reliable AI components towards solving incredibly complex problems with high confidence and verifiable results.
