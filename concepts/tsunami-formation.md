# Tsunami Formation

Tsunamis are ocean waves generated by sudden, large-scale disturbances that displace significant volumes of water vertically. Unlike wind-driven surface waves, which develop gradually through friction with moving air, tsunamis originate from impulsive events that transfer energy efficiently through the entire water column. This mechanism allows the waves to travel across ocean basins at speeds ranging from 500 to 800 kilometers per hour in deep water. In the open ocean, these waves often have wavelengths exceeding 100 kilometers and may appear as low as one meter in height, making them nearly imperceptible to ships at sea.

The primary drivers of tsunami formation are seismic activities, particularly undersea earthquakes caused by the sudden slip of faults along tectonic plate boundaries. When the seafloor shifts vertically during an earthquake, it displaces the overlying water mass, initiating the wave. Other significant causes include volcanic eruptions, which can cause rapid water displacement through explosive force or caldera collapse, and submarine landslides triggered by seismic activity or sediment instability. These events share the common characteristic of moving a large mass of water vertically rather than horizontally, which is essential for generating the long-wavelength waves characteristic of tsunamis.

As these waves approach shallow coastal waters, their speed decreases due to friction with the seabed, while their height increases dramatically through a process known as shoaling. The energy that was previously distributed throughout the deep water column becomes concentrated in a smaller depth, causing the wave to steepen. This transformation results in the rapid rise or fall of sea levels at the coast, often manifesting as a series of waves rather than a single breaking wave. The impact on shorelines depends on the local bathymetry and coastal geometry, which can either amplify or dissipate the wave's energy.
