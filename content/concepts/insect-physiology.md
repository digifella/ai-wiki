# Insect Physiology

Insect physiology is the study of the biological structures and functional systems that enable insects to survive, grow, and reproduce. This field examines how insect bodies are organized [[concepts/assistive-technology|at]] multiple [[concepts/musical-scales|scales]]—from cellular and organ systems to whole-organism behavior—and how these structures interact with environmental conditions. Insects represent the most diverse group of animals on Earth, and their physiological [[concepts/adaptations|adaptations]] reflect this variety, ranging from metabolic processes and sensory systems to locomotion and communication mechanisms.

## Cognition and Behavior

Recent research has expanded understanding of insect [[concepts/cognition|cognition]], particularly in social insects like [[concepts/bumblebees|bumblebees]]. Studies demonstrate that bumblebees possess cognitive abilities previously underestimated, including [[concepts/learning|learning]], [[concepts/memory|memory]] formation, and [[concepts/problem-solving|problem-solving]] [[concepts/capabilities|capabilities]]. This cognitive capacity supports complex social behaviors and [[concepts/decision-making|decision-making]] within colonies, contributing to what researchers describe as [[concepts/cumulative-culture|cumulative culture]]—the transmission and refinement of learned behaviors across generations within a population.

## Environmental Adaptation

Insects display remarkable physiological adaptations to environmental stressors. Research on bumblebees has documented adaptive [[concepts/responses|responses]] to flooding and other environmental challenges, revealing how insects can modify their physiology and behavior in response to changing conditions. These adaptations operate at multiple biological levels, including individual tolerance mechanisms and collective responses shaped by social [[concepts/structure|structure]] and communication within colonies.
