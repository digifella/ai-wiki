# Standard Model

The Standard Model is the foundational theoretical framework in particle physics that describes elementary particles and their interactions through three of the four known fundamental forces: the electromagnetic, weak nuclear, and strong nuclear forces. Developed incrementally from the 1960s onward through contributions by numerous physicists, the model successfully unifies quantum mechanics with special relativity to explain particle behavior at subatomic scales. It organizes matter into quarks and leptons, with their interactions mediated by gauge bosons.

The matter particles are divided into two main categories: quarks, which combine to form composite particles such as protons and neutrons, and leptons, which include electrons and neutrinos. These fermions interact via the exchange of force-carrying bosons. The photon mediates the electromagnetic force, the W and Z bosons mediate the weak force responsible for radioactive decay, and gluons mediate the strong force that binds quarks together. The Higgs boson, discovered in 2012, is associated with the Higgs field, which grants mass to other elementary particles.

Despite its success, the Standard Model does not incorporate gravity, the fourth fundamental force, nor does it explain dark matter or dark energy. Recent precision tests, such as measurements from muon g-2 experiments, have provided stringent constraints on the model's predictions, occasionally revealing tensions that may hint at new physics. Concurrently, investigations into quark substructure continue to probe the limits of the model's description of matter, driving ongoing research into potential extensions or alternative theories.
