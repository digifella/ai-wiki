# Lippmann Photography

Lippmann photography is a color photography process invented by French physicist Gabriel Lippmann in 1891. Rather than relying on chemical dyes or pigments to reproduce color, the technique captures color through optical interference—the interaction of light waves with themselves. The process involves placing a fine-grain photographic emulsion in direct contact with a mercury mirror. When light enters the plate from the front, it reflects off the mirror and interferes with the incoming light waves, creating microscopic stationary interference patterns within the emulsion.

These interference patterns record the wavelength and phase of the incident light, effectively storing the color information in the physical structure of the emulsion layers. This method establishes a direct link between the captured image and structural color, where the perceived hue is determined by the physical arrangement of the material rather than by light-absorbing pigments. The resulting images are characterized by their exceptional stability and vibrancy, as they do not fade over time due to the degradation of chemical dyes.

The distinction between true color and illusion in this context highlights the fundamental difference between subtractive color mixing and wave interference. While traditional photography creates color through the selective absorption of light by pigments, Lippmann photography generates color through the constructive and destructive interference of light waves. This structural approach ensures that the recorded color is an intrinsic property of the image's physical formation, offering a unique perspective on the nature of light and color reproduction.
