# First Stage Engine

A first stage engine is a propulsion unit that operates during the initial phase of a launch vehicle's flight, providing the thrust necessary to lift the vehicle and its payload from the launch pad through the lower atmosphere. These engines are typically the largest and most powerful on a launch vehicle, as they must generate sufficient thrust to overcome the vehicle's total mass and gravitational forces during the most fuel-intensive portion of ascent. The primary function of the first stage is to accelerate the rocket to a velocity and altitude where subsequent stages can efficiently continue the journey to orbit.

The operational timeline of the first stage extends beyond the initial liftoff and through the separation of the payload fairing. In certain mission profiles or failure scenarios, the first stage engine may continue burning after fairing separation. This continued propulsion can allow the payload structure to maintain flight stability and trajectory even if the fairing has sustained damage, potentially preserving the integrity of the payload during the critical early ascent phase.

Design considerations for first stage engines prioritize high thrust-to-weight ratios and reliability. They are engineered to handle the dynamic pressures of atmospheric flight and the structural loads associated with maximum aerodynamic pressure (Max Q). Once the propellant is depleted, the first stage is typically jettisoned to reduce mass for the upper stages, which then take over to achieve the precise orbital insertion required for the mission.
