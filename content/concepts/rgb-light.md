# RGB Light

RGB [[concepts/light|light]] refers to the additive color model based on red, green, and blue wavelengths, which the human eye perceives and the brain combines to create the full spectrum of visible colors. This model forms the foundation of how digital displays, from screens to projectors, generate color by emitting light rather than reflecting it.

## True Color vs. Structural Color

The distinction between true color and [[concepts/structural-color|structural color]] illuminates how RGB light relates to [[concepts/physical-reality|physical reality]]. True color results from the absorption and reflection of specific wavelengths by pigments and dyes—the actual chemical composition of an object determines which colors it reflects. Structural color, by [[concepts/contrast|contrast]], arises from the physical [[concepts/structure|structure]] of a material's surface or internal layers, which manipulate light through interference, diffraction, or scattering, rather than through chemical absorption. A Lippmann photograph demonstrates this principle: it records interference patterns created by light waves rather than capturing pigments, yet can reproduce colors with remarkable [[concepts/accuracy|accuracy]] by recreating the same optical structures that produce color in [[entities/nature|nature]].

## Implications for Perception

This distinction has profound implications for understanding color perception. When viewing an RGB display, the brain perceives color through stimulation of three types of cone cells, each sensitive to different wavelengths. Structural color in nature—such as in butterfly wings or peacock feathers—achieves similar perceptual results through entirely different physical mechanisms. Both pathways ultimately produce the same signal in human [[concepts/computer-vision|vision]], revealing that "color" as we experience it is fundamentally a perceptual phenomenon, not an absolute property of objects themselves.
