## Quantum Gravity Limits on Time Measurement Precision
**Clip title:** The Universe Has a Hard Limit on Time Precision
**Author / channel:** Anton Petrov
**URL:** https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=XA9n8QdM-jU

### Summary
This video explores a fascinating new study from 2025, published in [[concepts/physical-review-research|Physical Review Research]], which delves into the [[concepts/fundamental-limits|fundamental limits]] of [[concepts/time-measurement-precision|time measurement precision]]. The main topic revolves around the idea that the universe itself might contain an inherent uncertainty in spacetime, preventing [[entities/us|us]] from measuring time with absolute precision. This isn't attributed to flaws in our timekeeping devices but rather to a fundamental "fuzziness" woven into the fabric of reality, stemming from the interaction between [[concepts/quantum-mechanics|quantum mechanics]] and [[concepts/gravitational-pull|gravity]].

The [[entities/speaker|speaker]] first establishes the foundation of modern technology on [[concepts/quantum-mechanics|quantum mechanics]], highlighting the "measurement problem" where quantum superpositions collapse into definite classical states upon observation. While the standard explanation often posits this as an instantaneous, observer-driven event, many physicists seek a deeper, more objective understanding. This leads to the discussion of "spontaneous collapse models" or "objective-collapse theories," such as the Diósi-Penrose (DP) and Continuous Spontaneous Localization (CSL) models. These theories propose that [[concepts/wavefunction-collapse|wave function collapse]] is a continuous, spontaneous process, not triggered by conscious observation. For microscopic particles, these collapses are rare, but for macroscopic objects (like [[entities/us|us]]), the sheer number of constituent atoms means billions of such collapses occur every second, effectively rendering the object solid and classical.

The core [[concepts/innovation|innovation]] of the study lies in linking these spontaneous collapse models to [[concepts/gravitational-pull|gravity]]. According to Einstein's general relativity, mass curves spacetime. The models suggest that if a particle exists in a quantum superposition (i.e., in two [[entities/places|places]] [[concepts/assistive-technology|at]] once), it would attempt to curve spacetime in two different ways simultaneously. This creates a "bizarre tension" in spacetime, which prefers to "relax" by forcing the particle to a single, definite location. This "gravitization of quantum mechanics" not only provides a potential explanation for how our classical world emerges from [[concepts/quantum-indeterminacy|quantum uncertainty]] but also implies that the very [[concepts/flow|flow]] of time itself must [[concepts/experience|experience]] tiny fluctuations or "jitters." This inherent noise in time sets an ultimate, fundamental limit on how precisely we can ever measure it.

The study then calculates these fundamental limits. Under the Diósi-Penrose model, the precision limit is approximately 10⁻³¹ seconds per year, while the CSL model predicts a slightly larger uncertainty of around 10⁻²⁸ seconds per year. To put this into perspective, the most accurate atomic clocks available today (optical atomic clocks based on strontium atoms) can measure time with an uncertainty of about 10⁻¹¹ seconds per year. This means the universe's predicted inherent time uncertainty is quintillions of times smaller than what even our most sophisticated clocks can currently detect, posing no immediate threat to modern technologies like GPS. However, this theoretical blueprint offers exciting avenues for future experimental [[concepts/physics|physics]], pushing the boundaries of clock precision to potentially test these collapse models and advance our understanding towards a unified [[concepts/theory|theory]] of [[concepts/quantum-gravity|quantum gravity]].
