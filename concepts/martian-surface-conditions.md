# Martian Surface Conditions

Mars presents an environment with extreme physical parameters that differ fundamentally from Earth. The planet possesses a thin atmosphere composed primarily of carbon dioxide, which offers negligible shielding against solar radiation and cosmic rays. Surface temperatures average approximately −65°C (−85°F), with seasonal and diurnal fluctuations causing poles to reach −125°C and equatorial regions to occasionally exceed 0°C during midday. Atmospheric pressure at the surface is roughly 1% of Earth's, resulting in a state where liquid water is unstable and rapidly sublimates or freezes.

The regolith, or Martian soil, contains high concentrations of perchlorate salts, which are toxic to many known terrestrial microorganisms. These oxidants create a harsh chemical environment that degrades organic molecules, posing significant challenges for surface-based biological processes. Additionally, the lack of a global magnetic field allows charged particles from the sun to bombard the surface directly, further contributing to the sterilizing nature of the upper layers.

Despite these hostile surface conditions, subsurface environments may offer more habitable niches. Deeper layers of the crust could provide protection from radiation and maintain temperatures and pressures conducive to liquid brine. These subsurface habitats are considered potential refuges for microbial life, as they buffer against the extreme fluctuations and chemical toxicity found on the exposed surface.
