# Game Theory

Game theory is a mathematical framework for analyzing strategic interactions between rational decision-makers, known as players, who each seek to optimize their own outcomes. Developed formally in the mid-20th century by mathematicians including John von Neumann and Oskar Morgenstern, the field models situations where the result for any participant depends not only on their own choices but also on the choices of others. This interdependence distinguishes game theory from traditional optimization problems, where an agent's outcome is determined solely by their own actions and external factors.

The discipline is broadly categorized into cooperative and non-cooperative games. Cooperative game theory focuses on which coalitions of players will form and the distribution of surplus among them, often assuming binding agreements are possible. In contrast, non-cooperative game theory analyzes how individual rational players make decisions in isolation, without the ability to form binding contracts. This branch relies heavily on the concept of equilibrium, particularly the Nash equilibrium, where no player can benefit by unilaterally changing their strategy given the strategies of others.

Key concepts within the field include the distinction between zero-sum and non-zero-sum games. In zero-sum scenarios, one player's gain is exactly balanced by another's loss, representing pure competition. Non-zero-sum games allow for the possibility of mutual gain or mutual loss, introducing elements of cooperation and conflict. The field also examines sequential games, where players take turns, and simultaneous games, where decisions are made without knowledge of the others' choices, often utilizing tools like the minimax theorem or extensive form representations.

Applications of game theory extend beyond mathematics into economics, political science, biology, and computer science. In economics, it models market competition and auction design. In evolutionary biology, it explains the emergence of altruistic behaviors and species strategies through evolutionary game theory. In computer science, it informs the design of algorithms for multi-agent systems and network routing, providing a rigorous basis for understanding strategic behavior in complex, interactive environments.
