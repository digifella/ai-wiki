# Union Labor

Union labor refers to the collective organization of workers through labor unions to negotiate with employers over wages, benefits, and working conditions. By consolidating worker power, unions function as intermediaries between individual employees and management, enabling negotiations that individual workers typically cannot achieve alone. This collective bargaining model has become a central feature of labor markets in many developed economies, though union membership and [[concepts/power|influence]] vary significantly by country, industry, and time period.

## Historical Impact

Union movements have driven major labor reforms that reshaped working [[concepts/open-standards|standards]]. In the early-to-mid 20th century, unions played instrumental roles in establishing the eight-hour workday, workplace [[concepts/product-safety|safety standards]], child labor prohibitions, and minimum wage legislation—particularly in the [[entities/united-states|United States]] and [[entities/europe|Europe]]. These achievements fundamentally changed the relationship between employers and workers, establishing baseline protections that often became codified into law and extended even to non-unionized workers.

## Contemporary Role

Today, union labor operates in a different landscape than during its peak organizing periods. In the United States, union membership declined from roughly 35% of the workforce in the 1950s to approximately 10% in recent decades, with variation across sectors. Public sector unions remain relatively stronger, while private sector unionization has contracted significantly. Unions continue to negotiate contracts covering wages, benefits, pension plans, and grievance procedures, though their influence on broader labor standards has diminished compared to their historical role.
