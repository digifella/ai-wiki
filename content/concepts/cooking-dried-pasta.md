# Cooking Dried Pasta

Cooking dried pasta involves bringing salted water to a boil, adding pasta, and cooking until it reaches the desired tenderness. The traditional method cooks pasta in abundant water (typically a ratio of 4:1 water to pasta by weight) [[concepts/assistive-technology|at]] a rolling boil. [[concepts/cooking-time|Cooking time]] varies by pasta shape and thickness, generally ranging from 8 to 14 minutes, though manufacturers' guidance on packaging provides reliable estimates. Proper technique requires [[concepts/stirring-technique|stirring]] occasionally in the first minute to prevent sticking and tasting pasta shortly before the stated cooking time to assess doneness.

## Texture and Doneness

The [[concepts/texture|texture]] of cooked pasta depends on personal preference and dish requirements. Al dente (slightly firm to the bite) remains the standard in Italian cuisine and is preferred by most cooks, as it provides better texture [[concepts/contrast|contrast]] and [[concepts/sauce-adhesion|sauce adhesion]]. Softer pasta results from extended cooking time. The starch content of pasta affects how it absorbs water and develops texture; durum wheat semolina pasta, considered superior, releases less starch and maintains [[concepts/structure|structure]] better than lower-quality alternatives.

## Sauce Integration and Adhesion

Finishing pasta in the sauce—rather than combining them separately—improves [[concepts/flavor-integration|flavor integration]] and adhesion. Reserving pasta water before draining creates a starchy liquid that helps emulsify sauce and coat pasta more effectively. Adding pasta directly to simmering sauce allows the pasta to absorb flavors while the starch helps bind ingredients together. Undercooking pasta slightly (by 1-2 minutes) before this final cooking step prevents overcooking and encourages better starch interaction with the sauce.
