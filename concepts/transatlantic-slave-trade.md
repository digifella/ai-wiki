# Transatlantic Slave Trade

The Transatlantic Slave Trade was a forced migration system that operated primarily between the 16th and 19th centuries, transporting an estimated 12 to 15 million enslaved Africans across the Atlantic Ocean to the [[concepts/new-world|Americas]]. European traders, particularly from Portugal, Spain, [[entities/united-kingdom|England]], France, and the Netherlands, organized this commerce by establishing trading posts along African coasts and negotiating with African merchants and rulers to obtain captives. The trade became increasingly systematized and profitable over time, with the volume of enslaved people growing dramatically during the 18th century.

## Origins and Development

Portuguese merchants initiated European slave trading on the African coast in the mid-15th century, initially on a small scale. The trade expanded significantly following European colonization of the Americas in the late 15th century, driven by demand for labor on [[concepts/sugar|sugar]] plantations, tobacco farms, and other colonial enterprises. Over approximately three centuries, the trade evolved from a marginal commerce into one of the Atlantic [[entities/earth|world]]'s dominant economic systems.

## Routes and Scale

The typical slave trading voyage, known as the "Middle Passage," involved the transport of captives from African ports to Caribbean and American destinations. Conditions aboard slave ships were notoriously brutal, with mortality rates among captives ranging from 10 to 25 percent depending on voyage length and conditions. Ships returned to [[entities/europe|Europe]] with colonial products such as sugar, tobacco, and rum, creating a profitable triangular trade pattern.

The trade finally declined in the early 19th century as abolitionist movements gained strength in Europe and the Americas. Britain abolished the slave trade in 1807, though [[concepts/slavery|slavery]] itself persisted in many territories for decades longer. The Transatlantic Slave Trade's impacts on African societies, American demographic development, and global economic patterns remain subjects of significant historical study and ongoing reckoning.
