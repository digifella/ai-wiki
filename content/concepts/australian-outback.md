# Australian Outback

The Australian Outback is the vast interior region of Australia, encompassing approximately 70% of the continental landmass. It extends across parts of every Australian state and territory, representing one of the world's largest sparsely populated areas. The Outback is defined by arid and semi-[[concepts/arid-climate|arid climate]] conditions, with annual rainfall typically below 250 millimeters and temperatures that fluctuate dramatically between seasons.

## Geography and Landscape

The Outback's terrain is highly varied, featuring expansive desert plains, rocky ranges, salt lakes, and dry riverbeds. Major geographical features include the Great Victoria Desert, Great Sandy Desert, and Simpson Desert, along with significant rocky formations such as Uluru and the MacDonnell Ranges. Vegetation is sparse and specially adapted to extreme [[concepts/water-scarcity|water scarcity]], consisting primarily of spinifex grasses, acacia shrubs, and scattered stunted trees that can survive prolonged droughts.

## Settlement and Environment

Human settlement in the Outback is extremely limited, with communities often separated by hundreds of kilometers of remote terrain. Indigenous Aboriginal peoples have inhabited the region for over 60,000 years, developing deep ecological knowledge and sustainable practices suited to the harsh environment. Modern European settlement has been sporadic, concentrated in scattered towns and mining centers. The Outback experiences intense environmental conditions including extreme heat, severe droughts, and dust storms, making survival dependent on careful [[concepts/water-management|water management]] and adaptation to climatic extremes.
