# Oxygen Saturation

During the Carboniferous and Permian periods, spanning approximately 360 to 250 million years ago, Earth's atmospheric composition differed markedly from modern standards. Atmospheric oxygen concentrations peaked at roughly 35% during the Carboniferous, a significant increase from the contemporary baseline of 21%. This hyperoxic environment was primarily driven by the proliferation of vast tropical swamps and dense forests. These ecosystems facilitated high rates of photosynthesis, which released oxygen in quantities that exceeded the rate of organic matter decomposition.

The burial of this massive amount of organic carbon prevented it from reacting with atmospheric oxygen, effectively locking away the carbon in peat deposits that would later become coal. This imbalance between oxygen production and consumption led to the accumulation of free oxygen in the atmosphere. The resulting high oxygen levels influenced the evolution of terrestrial life, allowing for the development of larger arthropods and more complex respiratory systems in early tetrapods.

As the Permian period progressed, tectonic activity and climatic shifts altered these swampy environments, reducing the efficiency of organic carbon burial. Consequently, atmospheric oxygen levels gradually declined from their Carboniferous peaks. This long-term drawdown of oxygen and the corresponding rise in carbon dioxide set the stage for the geological conditions that characterize the Phanerozoic eon, linking ancient biological processes directly to the formation of the world's major fossil fuel reserves.
