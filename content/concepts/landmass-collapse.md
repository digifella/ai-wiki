# Landmass Collapse

Landmass collapse refers to the sudden, large-scale failure and displacement of rock or soil masses, resulting in rapid subsidence, lateral [[concepts/exercise|movement]], or catastrophic disintegration of terrain. These events occur when structural weaknesses, gravitational [[concepts/stress|stress]], or fluid pressures exceed the strength of underlying material. Collapses can be triggered by volcanic activity, seismic forces, erosion, or saturation, and may affect areas ranging from localised sinkholes to entire mountainsides or island flanks.

## Volcanic Flank Collapse

Volcanic islands are particularly susceptible to flank collapse due to the cumulative stress from repeated eruptions, intrusion of magma, and the inherent weakness of layered volcanic deposits. The 1888 collapse of [[entities/ritter-island|Ritter Island]] in [[entities/papua-new-guinea|Papua New Guinea]] exemplified this process: approximately 2,300 metres of [[entities/cuba|the island]]'s north flank failed suddenly, displacing an estimated 13 cubic kilometres of rock into the sea. This displacement generated a [[concepts/tsunami|tsunami]] with wave heights exceeding 100 metres, demonstrating the potential for landmass collapse to trigger secondary hazards with far-reaching consequences.

## Mechanisms and Triggers

Collapse mechanisms vary depending on geological context. Submarine and subaerial volcanic collapses often involve the destabilisation of steep slopes composed of fractured, hydrothermally altered rock. Other collapse types include sinkhole formation in soluble rock, mine-induced subsidence, and gravitational spreading along weak geological layers. The frequency and magnitude of such events [[entities/make|make]] understanding landmass collapse critical for coastal hazard assessment and long-term landscape evolution.
