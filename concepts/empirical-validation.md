# Empirical Validation

Empirical validation is the process of testing theoretical predictions against observations or experiments in the natural world. It forms the foundation of the scientific method by ensuring that ideas about how nature works are grounded in measurable reality rather than speculation alone. Without empirical validation, even the most elegant mathematical theories remain unproven hypotheses.

## Feynman's Three-Step Framework

Physicist Richard Feynman articulated a concise three-step approach to scientific validation that emphasizes the iterative nature of discovery. The first step involves formulating a hypothesis or "guess" based on existing knowledge and intuition. This is followed by the computation of the consequences of that guess, deriving specific, testable predictions from the theoretical model. The final step requires comparing these computed results with experimental data or natural observations to determine the validity of the initial hypothesis.

This framework highlights that scientific progress is not linear but cyclical. If the comparison reveals discrepancies, the hypothesis must be revised or discarded, leading to a new guess and subsequent computation. This rigorous loop ensures that scientific theories remain aligned with empirical evidence, preventing the acceptance of ideas that lack support in observable reality.
