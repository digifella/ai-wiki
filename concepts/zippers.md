# Zippers

A zipper is a fastening device consisting of two parallel strips of material fitted with interlocking teeth or coils that are joined or separated by a slider mechanism. The slider pulls the teeth together to close the zipper or pulls them apart to open it. This mechanical design allows for quick, reliable fastening of garments, bags, and other items. The basic principle—using small, precisely manufactured teeth that interlock under the action of a slider—has remained relatively consistent since the device's practical refinement in the early twentieth century.

## Development and History

The precursor to the modern zipper was the "clasp locker," invented by Whitcomb L. Judson in 1893, which utilized individual hooks and eyes. While innovative, it was cumbersome and prone to jamming. In 1917, Gideon Sundback, a Swedish-American engineer working for the Universal Fastener Company, developed the "Separable Fastener No. 2." Sundback’s design introduced the interlocking tooth mechanism that defines the modern zipper, increasing reliability and speed of operation. He also standardized the manufacturing process, allowing for mass production.

## Industrialization and Standardization

The widespread adoption of zippers was driven by industrial efficiency and standardization. In the 1920s and 1930s, companies like Talon and B.F. Goodrich (who coined the term "zipper" for rubber boots) popularized the device in fashion and footwear. However, the Japanese company YKK (Yoshida Kogyo Kabushikigaisha) became dominant in the latter half of the 20th century through vertical integration and rigorous quality control. YKK’s ability to manufacture every component of the zipper in-house allowed for unprecedented consistency, cost-effectiveness, and global distribution, making it the standard for the industry.

## Mechanical Engineering and Variants

The mechanical integrity of a zipper relies on the precise geometry of its teeth or coils and the tension applied by the slider. Two primary types exist: the toothed zipper, common in denim and jackets, and the coil zipper, made from a continuous spiral of nylon or polyester, which is more flexible and resistant to jamming. The slider contains a wedge-shaped insert that forces the teeth or coils to interlock or separate. Modern engineering has focused on materials science to improve corrosion resistance, tensile strength, and smoothness of operation, ensuring the device remains a critical component in textile manufacturing and industrial applications.
