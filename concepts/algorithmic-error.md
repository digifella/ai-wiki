# Algorithmic Error

Algorithmic error refers to failures in [[concepts/decision-making|automated decision-making]] systems that produce harmful, unjust, or unintended outcomes. These errors emerge from multiple sources: flawed [[concepts/open-source-philosophy|logic]] in system design, inadequate [[concepts/data-integrity|data quality]], insufficient testing before deployment, or the misapplication of [[concepts/algorithms|algorithms]] to contexts requiring human judgment. When algorithmic systems operate at scale—particularly in government administration, finance, [[concepts/health-care|healthcare]], and criminal justice—the consequences of errors can affect thousands of individuals simultaneously, often with limited visibility or [[concepts/accountability|accountability]].

## Sources and Causes

Algorithmic errors typically stem from technical failures, such as logical mistakes in code or mathematical models that fail to account for real-[[entities/earth|world]] complexity. Poor data quality—including incomplete, biased, or outdated information—can propagate errors throughout a system. Equally significant are conceptual failures: using an [[concepts/algorithm|algorithm]] for a task where it is fundamentally unsuitable, or failing to include adequate human oversight and appeal [[concepts/causes|mechanisms]]. Organizations may also rush deployment without rigorous testing, or fail to [[concepts/monitor-tool|monitor system]] performance after launch.

## Real-World Impact

The Australian [[concepts/government-automation-failure|Robodebt Scheme]] exemplified the severe consequences of algorithmic error in government. Between 2015 and 2019, an automated welfare system illegally recovered approximately AUD 1.5 billion from citizens by comparing tax office data with welfare records using a flawed averaging method. The algorithm produced systematic false positives, falsely identifying recipients as having undeclared income. The resulting debt notices caused psychological distress, financial hardship, and contributed to at least one documented suicide. The scheme was eventually ruled unlawful, leading to compensation payouts and a public inquiry.

## Mitigation and Governance

Addressing algorithmic error requires multiple safeguards: rigorous testing and validation before deployment, diverse data sources and [[concepts/quality-control|quality control]], transparent documentation of how algorithms function, and mandatory human review for high-stakes decisions. Many jurisdictions now require algorithmic impact assessments, independent audits, and accessible appeals processes. The principle of algorithmic accountability—ensuring systems and their operators can be held responsible for harms—has become increasingly important in policy discussions around automation in government and critical services.
