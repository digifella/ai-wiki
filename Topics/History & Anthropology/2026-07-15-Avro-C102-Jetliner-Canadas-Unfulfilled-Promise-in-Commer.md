## Avro C102 Jetliner: Canada's Unfulfilled Promise in Commercial Jet Aviation History
**Clip title:** Lecture: the Avro C102 Jetliner
**[[entities/tasia-custode|Author]] / channel:** Our Own Devices
**URL:** https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=-_cB1h2Fml8

### Summary
The [[entities/avro-c102-jetliner|Avro C102 Jetliner]], often overshadowed by the [[entities/avro-arrow|Avro Arrow]], represents a tragic and largely forgotten chapter in Canadian aerospace history. Introduced by [[entities/gilles-messier|Gilles Messier]], the video details how this revolutionary aircraft, the second [[concepts/purpose-built-jet|purpose-built jet]] transport to fly globally, had the potential to establish [[entities/canada|Canada]] as a leader in commercial aviation. Developed by [[entities/avro-canada|Avro Canada]], a company formed from the wartime Victory Aircraft Limited, the Jetliner was initially conceived to meet both military and civil aviation needs in the post-WWII era. Its visionary [[concepts/team-lead|lead engineer]], James Charles Floyd, steered the project towards jet propulsion, a decision that would prove both groundbreaking and problematic.

The initial plan for the Jetliner involved using two advanced Rolls-Royce Avon axial-[[concepts/flow|flow]] turbojet engines, a choice that would have put the aircraft years ahead of its time. However, the classification of the Avon engines as military secrets forced Avro to redesign the aircraft to accommodate four larger, heavier, and less efficient Rolls-Royce Derwent 5 centrifugal-[[concepts/flow|flow]] turbojets. Despite these [[entities/national-academies|engineering]] challenges, Avro aimed to meet the demanding requirements set by Trans-Canada Airlines (TCA) for a short-to-medium-haul airliner, including a 400 mph cruising [[concepts/speed|speed]] and a 4,000-foot runway capability. Yet, TCA's subsequent "unreasonable demands"—such as a fixed price, a three-year moratorium on sales to other airlines, and payment for a year of testing post-[[concepts/adoption|adoption]]—effectively shifted all commercial risk onto Avro and revealed TCA's hesitation to be the first North American airline to adopt jet technology. This reluctance was further compounded by regulatory issues concerning instrument landing systems in Canadian airports, which would have severely curtailed the Jetliner's range and [[concepts/efficient-operation|operational efficiency]].

Despite TCA's prevarication, the Canadian government, under C.D. Howe, and Avro's British parent company, Hawker Siddeley, provided critical funding to push the prototype through to completion. The Jetliner’s design, while conventional in [[concepts/presence|appearance]] to mitigate risks associated with new technologies like high cabin pressurization, was revolutionary in its propulsion. Its maiden flight on August 10, 1949, was a resounding [[concepts/success|success]], with test pilots praising its docile handling and quiet operation. The aircraft quickly shattered performance records, making the Toronto-New York route in a mere hour. This impressive performance attracted significant international [[concepts/attention-mechanisms|attention]], with American Airlines expressing [[entities/martin-keen|keen]] interest and the U.S. military considering it for various roles, including navigation training and air-to-air refueling. Even the eccentric aviation mogul Howard Hughes borrowed the Jetliner for evaluation, expressing strong desire to acquire and [[concepts/license|license]] its production.

However, the Jetliner's soaring [[concepts/success|success]] was cut short in February 1951. With the outbreak of the Korean War, the Canadian government redirected all of Avro's resources and priority towards the struggling CF-100 military interceptor program. This abrupt shift led to the cancellation of the Jetliner project, with the second prototype under construction being scrapped, and the first prototype relegated to serving as a photography platform for CF-100 trials. By November 1956, the original Jetliner was also dismantled, with only its nose section surviving as a museum exhibit. The video concludes that the cancellation of the Avro Jetliner, an aircraft 6-10 years ahead of its commercial rivals like the Caravelle and Boeing 737, was a significant loss for Canadian [[concepts/innovation|innovation]], largely attributable to misplaced governmental priorities, undue interference, and a tragic lack of foresight.

### Video Description & Links
#### Description
Lecture delivered to the Canadian Aviation Historical Society (CAHS) Winnipeg chapter on February 26, 2026.

Making its first flight in 1949, the Avro Canada [[concepts/ykk|Ltd.]] C102 Jetliner was only the second purpose-built commercial jet transport to fly (after the de Havilland Comet) and the first designed for the short-to-[[entities/medium|medium]] range regional market. A decade ahead of its closest competitors and loved by all who flew in it, the Jetliner seemed poised to dominate the North American aviation market. Tragically, however, due to industry conservatism, government interference, and other factors, the project was soon cancelled and the sole prototype cut up for scrap.
