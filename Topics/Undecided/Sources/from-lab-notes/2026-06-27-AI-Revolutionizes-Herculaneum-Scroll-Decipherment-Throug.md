## AI Revolutionizes Herculaneum Scroll Decipherment Through Virtual Unrolling
**Clip title:** AI is Unwrapping COMPLETE Herculaneum Scrolls - BREAKING ARCHAEOLOGY NEWS
**[[entities/tasia-custode|Author]] / channel:** Dig It With Raven
**URL:** https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=51XHIoV2HwU

### Summary
The video discusses the incredible archaeological news surrounding the [[concepts/herculaneum-papyri|Herculaneum Papyri]], a collection of over 1,800 papyrus scrolls discovered in the 18th century in the [[concepts/villa-of-the-papyri|Villa of the Papyri]] in Herculaneum. These scrolls, unique as the only surviving library from antiquity in its entirety, were carbonized and buried by the 79 CE eruption of [[concepts/mount-vesuvius|Mount Vesuvius]]. For centuries, reading these brittle, burnt scrolls has been an extremely difficult and often destructive task. Early attempts involved various chemical treatments and painstaking manual unrolling, sometimes taking years for a single scroll, with the added problem that the carbon-based ink would fade upon [[concepts/exposure|exposure]] to air.

In recent years, modern technology has revolutionized the approach to these fragile artifacts. Non-invasive techniques like multispectral imaging and shortwave-infrared hyperspectral imaging (SWIR HSI) are now used to digitize the scrolls by detecting the ink's distinct infrared reflective band. A process called "[[concepts/virtual-unrolling|virtual unrolling]]" has emerged as a groundbreaking method. In 2016, [[concepts/phil-moriarty|computer scientist]] Brent Seales successfully used virtual unrolling to read a charred parchment, and in 2023, his team, including classicist [[concepts/feynman|Richard]] Janko, employed [[concepts/ai-technologies|artificial intelligence]] to decipher a heavily damaged Herculaneum papyrus.

This led to two major breakthroughs announced in the video. The first is the virtual unrolling of an entire scroll, PHerc 1667, housed at the National Library of Naples. This scroll, previously deemed completely unreadable after a destructive attempt in the 1980s, revealed almost 1.5 meters of text across 20 columns. Assistant Professor Federica Nicolardi dated this text to the second or late third century BCE, making it one of the oldest Roman scrolls to survive. Its content appears to be a philosophical treatise on [[concepts/ethics|ethics]], arts, and human behavior, potentially a Stoic work by Chrysippus, an [[entities/tasia-custode|author]] whose extensive writings were thought lost to time. The second breakthrough is the identification of a new book title, "Philodemus, On Gods, Book 8," from scroll PHerc 139. This discovery confirms that "On Gods" was a multi-book work, a crucial insight as only the first book was previously known.

These advancements represent a quantum leap in recovering ancient literature. Historians and classicists are now able to uncover voices and [[concepts/ideas|ideas]] from 2,000 years ago that were previously lost forever. The ongoing "Vesuvius Challenge," accessible through scrollprize.org, invites individuals to contribute to solving the complex problems of ink detection and geometric reconstruction, offering prizes for successful decryption. This collaborative effort, bridging the humanities and sciences, is bringing ancient texts, data, and profound insights into the big thinkers of the Greco-Roman [[entities/earth|world]] back to life, fundamentally changing the study of antiquity.
