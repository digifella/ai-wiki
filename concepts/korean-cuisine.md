# Korean Cuisine

Korean cuisine encompasses the traditional cooking practices and food culture of Korea, characterized by bold, complex flavors and distinctive meal [[concepts/writing|composition]]. The cuisine developed through Korea's geography, climate, and historical circumstances, with regional variations reflecting local ingredients and cultural practices. Central to Korean dining is the principle of balance—in flavor, [[concepts/nutrition|nutrition]], and visual presentation—with meals typically organized around a main protein dish, rice, and an array of smaller side dishes called banchan.

## Key Ingredients and Flavors

Fundamental to Korean cooking are fermented ingredients, particularly gochugaru (red chili powder), gochujang (red chili paste), and doenjang (soybean paste), which provide depth and umami to many dishes. Garlic, sesame, soy sauce, and vinegar are also staples that shape the flavor profile of traditional Korean food. Fermentation practices, refined over centuries, preserve vegetables and create distinctive tastes central to the cuisine's identity.

## Common Dishes

Representative Korean dishes include bulgogi (marinated and grilled meat), bibimbap (mixed rice with vegetables and protein), kimchi (fermented vegetables), and various soups and stews. Grilled meats like dak bulgogi ([[concepts/dak-bulgogi|chicken bulgogi]]) are particularly popular, prepared by [[concepts/marinating|marinating]] meat in soy sauce, [[concepts/sugar|sugar]], garlic, and sesame before cooking. Meals are typically served communally, with multiple dishes shared among diners.

## Cultural Significance

Korean meals reflect cultural values of hospitality and family [[concepts/connection|connection]], with the [[concepts/preparation|preparation]] and sharing of food playing important roles in social gatherings and celebrations. Regional cuisines vary notably across different provinces, influenced by local agricultural products and historical trade practices.
