# High Oxygen Period

The High Oxygen Period denotes geological intervals characterized by atmospheric oxygen concentrations significantly exceeding the modern baseline of approximately 21 percent. The most prominent instance occurred during the Late Paleozoic Era, specifically within the Carboniferous period (roughly 359 to 299 million years ago). During this time, oxygen levels potentially surpassed 30 percent, creating an environment distinct from subsequent geological epochs. These elevated levels were sustained by extensive coal-forming forests and high marine productivity, which sequestered vast amounts of carbon in organic deposits before it could react with the atmosphere.

## Geological and Climatic Impacts

The massive burial of organic matter during this era led to widespread oxidation of surface rocks, contributing to the distinctive red coloration of geological formations in regions such as the Australian Outback. The removal of carbon dioxide from the atmosphere also triggered significant global cooling, leading to extensive glaciation in Gondwana. This period established key geological features that define the landscape of the continent's interior, linking biological productivity directly to long-term climatic and geochemical cycles.

## Biological Consequences

High atmospheric oxygen levels allowed for the evolution of larger arthropods and influenced the metabolic rates of terrestrial organisms. The abundance of oxygen supported the expansion of complex forest ecosystems, which in turn accelerated the weathering of silicate rocks and further regulated atmospheric composition. This feedback loop between biological activity and geochemical processes set the stage for the subsequent Permian-Triassic extinction event, which dramatically altered global oxygen levels and ecosystem structures.
