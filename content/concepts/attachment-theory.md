# Attachment Theory

Attachment [[concepts/theory|Theory]] is a psychological framework developed primarily by [[entities/chef-john|John]] Bowlby in the 1950s that explains how early [[concepts/relationships|relationships]] between infants and caregivers [[concepts/power|influence]] emotional and social development. The theory proposes that children form emotional bonds with primary caregivers as an evolutionary adaptation for survival and proximity-seeking. Bowlby argued that the quality of these early attachments significantly shapes personality, relationship patterns, and [[concepts/mental-health|mental health]] outcomes throughout the lifespan.

## Key Concepts

The theory identifies attachment as a biological system that evolved to keep infants close to protective figures during vulnerable periods. Bowlby distinguished between [[concepts/secure|secure]] attachment—characterized by trust in the caregiver's availability—and insecure attachment patterns, which develop when caregivers are unreliable or unresponsive. Mary Ainsworth, a collaborator, expanded the framework by identifying specific attachment styles through her "Strange Situation" experimental procedure, categorizing attachments as secure, anxious-ambivalent, or avoidant.

## Applications and Impact

Attachment Theory has influenced child development practices, clinical [[concepts/psychology|psychology]], and understanding of mental health conditions including [[concepts/anxiety|anxiety]], [[concepts/depression|depression]], and relationship difficulties. The framework suggests that early experiences with caregivers create internal working [[concepts/models|models]]—mental representations of self, others, and relationships—that individuals carry into adulthood. Contemporary research continues to examine how attachment patterns affect parenting practices, therapeutic relationships, and interpersonal functioning across different cultural contexts.
