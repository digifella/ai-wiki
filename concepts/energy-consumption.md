# Energy Consumption

Energy consumption represents a critical operational and environmental challenge in [[concepts/desalination|desalination]] processes used to meet agricultural water demands. Desalination—the removal of dissolved salts and minerals from seawater or brackish water—requires substantial energy inputs across all established technologies. The [[concepts/energy-demand|energy intensity of desalination]] directly affects both the economic viability of projects and their associated greenhouse gas emissions, making [[concepts/energy-efficiency|energy efficiency]] a central consideration for sustainable agricultural water supply.

## Energy Requirements by Technology

[[concepts/desalination-technology|Reverse osmosis]] (RO) is currently the most energy-efficient desalination method, typically requiring 3 to 10 kilowatt-hours (kWh) per cubic meter of freshwater produced. This technology relies on high-[[concepts/pressure|pressure]] pumps to force saline water through semi-permeable membranes, allowing water molecules to pass while retaining salts. The theoretical minimum energy required for the separation of salts from water is significantly lower, but practical inefficiencies in pumping, membrane resistance, and pre-treatment increase the actual energy demand.

Thermal desalination methods, such as [[concepts/msf-technology|multi-stage flash]] (MSF) distillation and multi-effect distillation (MED), generally consume more energy than RO processes. These technologies involve boiling saline water and condensing the resulting vapor to produce fresh water. MSF systems, which are often used in regions with abundant low-cost heat or waste energy, can require 10 to 15 kWh per cubic meter, while MED systems typically operate in the 8 to 12 kWh range. The higher energy intensity of thermal processes is due to the latent heat of vaporization required to change the [[concepts/phase|phase]] of water.

The source of energy used for desalination significantly influences its environmental footprint. In many [[concepts/arid-geography|arid regions]] with high solar irradiance, solar-powered desalination is being explored to reduce reliance on fossil fuels. However, the intermittency of [[concepts/renewable-energy|renewable energy]] sources poses challenges for the [[concepts/247-operation|continuous operation]] required by [[concepts/desalination-plants|desalination plants]]. Consequently, grid-connected facilities often depend on fossil fuel-based electricity, contributing to carbon emissions that counteract some of the environmental benefits of providing alternative water sources for [[concepts/agriculture|agriculture]].
