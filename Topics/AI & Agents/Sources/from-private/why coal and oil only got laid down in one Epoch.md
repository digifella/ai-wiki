What happened to oxygen levels as well as co2 during the epoch when fossil fuels were being created?

During the geologic epochs when fossil fuels were being formed—primarily the Carboniferous and Permian periods (roughly 360 to 250 million years ago)—atmospheric oxygen and carbon dioxide levels underwent significant changes driven largely by biological and geological processes.

### Oxygen Levels

Oxygen concentrations reached some of the highest levels in Earth’s history during the late Carboniferous, estimated to be around 30–35% of the atmosphere, compared to today’s ~21%. This surge was largely due to the massive proliferation of plant life, especially large vascular plants in extensive swampy forests. These plants absorbed CO₂ and released oxygen through photosynthesis, leading to a net accumulation of oxygen in the atmosphere.

### Carbon Dioxide Levels

Concurrently, CO₂ levels declined sharply during the same period. This was a result of both biological activity (carbon being sequestered into plant biomass) and geological processes (formation of extensive peat and coal deposits). As these organic materials were buried and compressed into coal, large amounts of carbon were effectively locked away from the atmosphere.

### Resulting Impacts

	These oxygen-rich conditions supported the evolution of giant insects and amphibians, which benefitted from more efficient respiration in a high-O₂ environment.
	
	The drop in CO₂ contributed to a cooling of the global climate, culminating in glaciations during the late Carboniferous.
	

This fossil fuel formation epoch essentially acted as a large-scale carbon sink event. However, in modern times, the burning of these fossil fuels has reversed the process, returning vast amounts of CO₂ to the atmosphere and contributing to anthropogenic [[concepts/climate-change|climate change]].

2. Carbon Cycle Feedbacks – Explore how the burial of organic carbon in the Paleozoic relates to modern carbon cycle [[concepts/feedback|feedback]] [[concepts/loops|loops]] and climate models.

At 30 - 35% oxygen levels I assume forests were extremely flammable?  Also those levels are toxic to modern animals isn’t it? Did those creatures have specific [[concepts/adaptations|adaptations]] to survive?

Yes, during the late Carboniferous and early Permian, when atmospheric oxygen reached 30–35%, forests were indeed extremely flammable, and this had significant ecological and evolutionary consequences.

### 1\. High Oxygen = High Flammability

Oxygen is the key oxidiser in combustion, and higher levels make organic matter ignite more easily and burn more intensely. The Carboniferous landscape—dense with ferns, lycopsids, and other vascular plants—would have been highly vulnerable to fire, especially during drier periods. Evidence for this includes:

	Abundant fossilised charcoal (fusain), indicating frequent [[concepts/wildfires|wildfires]].
	
	Fire-adapted traits in some plant species, such as thick bark and rapid regrowth.
	

### 2\. Oxygen Toxicity and Animal Adaptations

For many modern organisms, oxygen levels above ~25% can lead to oxidative [[concepts/stress|stress]], damaging cells and tissues. In humans, prolonged [[concepts/exposure|exposure]] to oxygen-rich environments (hyperoxia) can result in lung damage and central nervous system toxicity. However, ancient organisms adapted to this high-oxygen world:

	Respiratory Efficiency: Insects like Meganeura (giant dragonflies with 70 cm wingspans) had simple diffusion-based respiratory systems. The increased O₂ allowed sufficient diffusion to support larger body sizes.
	
	Protective Mechanisms: Some hypotheses suggest ancient animals may have evolved enhanced antioxidant systems or other physiological adaptations to mitigate oxidative damage.
	
	Body Size [[concepts/scaling|Scaling]]: The high O₂ enabled gigantism in arthropods and amphibians. However, as O₂ levels declined later, so did the maximum sizes of these organisms.
	

### Ecological and Evolutionary Feedbacks

Fire regimes shaped plant community structures and influenced atmospheric CO₂ by altering the balance between biomass accumulation and decay. As plant types shifted and oxygen declined post-Permian, large insect species disappeared—likely due to both lower oxygen and changing predation or competition dynamics.

Why were those oxygen levels never happened again? Why was this a unique period?

The extraordinarily high oxygen levels of the late Carboniferous and early Permian—peaking around 30–35%—were the result of a unique convergence of biological evolution, climate conditions, and geological processes. Here’s why this period was singular in Earth’s history:

* * *

### 1\. Massive Burial of Organic Carbon

A critical factor was the unprecedented burial of organic material:

	Vast swampy forests dominated by lycopods, ferns, and seed ferns produced immense amounts of biomass.
	
	Dead plant material accumulated in waterlogged, anoxic (oxygen-poor) environments, especially in equatorial peat bogs.
	
	Decomposition was slow and incomplete, preventing carbon from returning to the atmosphere as CO₂.
	

This burial of carbon as peat and coal removed CO₂ from the atmosphere while freeing oxygen (produced by photosynthesis) to accumulate.

* * *

### 2\. Lack of Wood-Decomposing Organisms

	Fungi capable of efficiently degrading lignin, a key component of wood, had not yet evolved.
	
	Without decomposers to recycle carbon back into the atmosphere, more biomass was buried than respired, leading to net oxygen accumulation.
	

* * *

### 3\. Geological Stability and Continental Configuration

	Supercontinent Pangaea’s formation created large, low-lying equatorial basins ideal for peat accumulation.
	
	Relatively low tectonic and volcanic activity during parts of this era reduced CO₂ emissions from geological sources.
	

* * *

### Why Oxygen Didn’t Rise Again

Post-Permian, oxygen levels never returned to those heights because:

	Decomposer evolution: By the late Permian and early Mesozoic, fungi and microbes had evolved that could effectively break down lignin, closing the carbon loop and reducing net O₂ accumulation.
	
	Fewer anoxic peatlands: Climate shifts and tectonic changes reduced the extent of the massive swamp forests.
	
	Increased wildfires: As O₂ levels rose, frequent fires acted as a natural brake, consuming biomass and returning CO₂ to the atmosphere, preventing runaway oxygen accumulation.
	
	Changes in plant types: Gymnosperms and later angiosperms didn’t form peat in the same extensive manner as their Carboniferous predecessors.
	

* * *

### Summary

This oxygen spike was a geologically brief but biologically profound phenomenon, enabled by an evolutionary bottleneck (undecomposed wood), ideal swampy ecosystems, and long-term burial of carbon. It’s unlikely to recur without a similar set of conditions—especially absent the constraint on decomposer activity.

* * *

We had giant insects able to live as a result of high oxygen levels - but they still survive today in smaller forms. Were there any unique life forms back then that cannot exist today even in miniature form as a result of the higher oxygen back then and the susbsequent drop?

Yes, several life forms of the Carboniferous–Permian period were not just larger, but fundamentally dependent on high oxygen concentrations for their basic biological function, and have no true analogues today—not even in miniature. Here’s a breakdown of those uniquely extinct forms and why they likely couldn’t exist in today’s lower-oxygen atmosphere:

* * *

### 1\. Giant Arthropods with Passive Respiration

Species like Meganeura (giant dragonflies) and Arthropleura (a millipede-like creature up to 2.6 metres long) are iconic examples. While their modern relatives exist (e.g. dragonflies, millipedes), their size was entirely enabled by the high atmospheric O₂ because:

	Insects and millipedes rely on tracheal diffusion—a passive system that doesn’t pump air.
	
	This system becomes inefficient for gas exchange beyond a certain body size under modern 21% oxygen levels.
	
	Attempts to raise modern insects in high-O₂ lab environments have shown temporary size increases, but not sustained survival or function.
	

Conclusion: These species’ entire respiratory viability at large sizes depended on elevated O₂. Their forms can’t be downscaled without becoming biologically unviable.

* * *

### 2\. Amphibians with Minimal Lungs

Some Carboniferous amphibians, like Crassigyrinus, had primitive or reduced lungs and relied heavily on cutaneous respiration (absorbing oxygen through the skin). In today’s air, which has:

	Lower O₂ levels,
	
	Higher rates of desiccation,
	
	And increased pollutants,
	these adaptations would be insufficient to sustain large-bodied or fully aquatic forms outside of very oxygen-rich water.
	

Modern analogues like salamanders are much smaller and require very specific, moist, high-altitude or aquatic environments.

* * *

### 3\. Obligate High-O₂ Forest Ecosystems

Certain now-extinct plant and insect interactions would also be impossible to maintain today:

	Dense lycopod forests created microenvironments rich in oxygen and shade.
	
	Some specialist detritivores or insect symbionts were dependent on both this ecosystem and its flammability–recovery cycle.
	
	With modern atmospheric conditions, forest fire dynamics, plant chemistry, and decay rates have changed substantially, disrupting those niches.
	

Conclusion: These were ecosystem-bound lifeforms—not just species, but systems—dependent on atmospheric composition and combustion thresholds.

* * *

### Why Can’t They Return Today, Even Small?

1. Respiratory Mechanics: Passive systems don’t scale linearly in oxygen-poor air.
	
2. Ecosystem Collapse: Their food webs and fire-adapted biomes no longer exist.
	
3. Physiological Bottlenecks: Evolution can’t “rewind” to a form that only worked in a now-absent atmosphere.
	

* * *

### Further Exploration:

	Paleophysiology studies like Dudley (1998) on oxygen and insect gigantism.
	
	Research on experimental high-O₂ rearing of insects for insight into physiological limits.
	

These uniquely large and niche-dependent creatures weren’t just big—they were oxygen-fueled experiments of evolution that couldn’t survive a world like ours, even in miniature.

I imagine minerals oxidised differently in those high oxygen level environments - were there any unique minerals only formed during that period?

The elevated atmospheric oxygen levels during the Carboniferous period (approximately 359 to 299 million years ago) significantly influenced Earth’s geochemical processes, including mineral formation. While this period did not yield entirely unique minerals exclusive to its timeframe, the high oxygen concentrations facilitated the formation and [[concepts/preservation|preservation]] of certain minerals that are rare or less stable under today’s atmospheric conditions.

* * *

### Minerals Influenced by High Oxygen Levels

1. Vivianite
	Vivianite is a hydrated iron(II) phosphate mineral that often forms in reducing environments but oxidizes readily upon exposure to oxygen, changing from colorless to deep blue or green. During the Carboniferous, the high oxygen levels could have accelerated the oxidation of vivianite, leading to the formation of its oxidized counterpart, metavivianite. This transformation is significant in understanding the redox conditions of ancient environments.
	
2. Iron Oxides (Hematite and Magnetite)
	The abundance of atmospheric oxygen facilitated the widespread oxidation of iron-bearing minerals, leading to extensive deposits of iron oxides like hematite and magnetite. These minerals are indicative of oxidative weathering processes and are often used as proxies to infer past atmospheric oxygen levels.
	
3. Banded Iron Formations (BIFs)
	Although most BIFs date to earlier periods, the oxidative conditions of the Carboniferous could have contributed to localized iron precipitation events, forming iron-rich sedimentary layers. These formations result from the oxidation of dissolved iron in seawater, precipitating as iron oxides.
	

* * *

### Mineral Evolution and Atmospheric Oxygen

The concept of mineral evolution posits that the diversity and distribution of minerals on Earth have been influenced by biological activity and atmospheric changes. The rise in atmospheric oxygen, particularly during events like the Great Oxidation Event, led to the formation of new minerals through oxidative processes. While the Carboniferous period postdates the Great Oxidation Event, its high oxygen levels continued to [[concepts/motivation|drive]] the diversification of minerals, especially oxidized forms of existing minerals.

* * *

### Further Exploration

For those interested in delving deeper into the interplay between atmospheric conditions and mineral formation, consider exploring:

	Paleo-redox [[concepts/indicators|Indicators]]: Studying minerals like uraninite and pyrite, which are sensitive to oxygen levels, can provide insights into ancient atmospheric conditions.
	
	Coal Geochemistry: Analyzing the mineral content of Carboniferous coal deposits can reveal information about the depositional environment and diagenetic processes influenced by oxygen levels.
	
	Mineral Evolution Database: A resource detailing the chronological appearance of minerals and their relation to Earth’s changing environments.
	

* * *

In summary, while the Carboniferous period’s high oxygen levels did not produce entirely new minerals exclusive to that era, they significantly influenced the formation, stability, and distribution of certain minerals, leaving a lasting imprint on Earth’s geochemical record.
