# Mathematical Problems

Mathematical problems form the computational foundation of cryptographic [[concepts/security|security]] systems. These problems are carefully selected for their [[concepts/solution-difficulty|computational difficulty]]—easy to verify when solved, but extremely hard to solve without specific knowledge or keys. The security of cryptographic systems depends on the assumption that no efficient [[concepts/algorithm|algorithm]] exists to solve these underlying mathematical problems within a practical timeframe.

## Classical Cryptographic Problems

Traditional cryptographic systems rely on problems from [[concepts/number-theory|number theory]] and discrete [[concepts/mathematics|mathematics]]. RSA encryption, for example, depends on the difficulty of factoring large composite numbers into their [[concepts/prime-lens|prime]] factors. Elliptic curve [[concepts/cryptography|cryptography]] [[concepts/number-systems|bases]] its security on the elliptic curve discrete logarithm problem, where computing discrete logarithms on elliptic curves is considered computationally infeasible with classical [[concepts/algorithms|algorithms]].

## Post-Quantum Alternatives

The [[concepts/emergent-behavior|emergence]] of [[concepts/quantum-computing|quantum computing]] poses a theoretical threat to classical cryptographic approaches, as quantum algorithms could potentially solve these traditional problems much faster than [[concepts/classical-computers|classical computers]]. [[concepts/encryption-algorithms|Lattice-based cryptography]] has emerged as a leading post-quantum [[concepts/solution|solution]]. Lattice problems, such as the Shortest Vector Problem and [[concepts/learning|Learning]] With Errors problem, appear to remain difficult even against quantum adversaries. This makes [[concepts/lattice-cryptography|lattice cryptography]] a practical candidate for maintaining cryptographic security in a potential [[entities/quantum-computing|quantum computing]] era.

## Problem Selection and Validation

The selection of mathematical problems for cryptographic use requires rigorous analysis and community scrutiny. A suitable cryptographic problem must remain unsolved despite years of research by the mathematical and cryptographic communities, with no known polynomial-time algorithms for solving it. The difficulty of these problems is continuously evaluated as computational technology and mathematical techniques advance.
