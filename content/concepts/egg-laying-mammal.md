# Egg Laying Mammal

Egg-laying mammals, or monotremes, are among the most unusual mammals on Earth. The platypus and echidnas represent the only living members of this ancient group, which diverged from other mammals over 160 million years ago. Unlike all other mammals, monotremes reproduce by laying eggs rather than bearing live young, a trait they share with their reptilian ancestors.

## Biology and Anatomy

The platypus possesses a remarkable array of primitive and specialized features. It retains a single reproductive and excretory opening called a cloaca, similar to birds and reptiles. Despite laying eggs, female platypuses produce milk to nourish their young, though they lack nipples and instead secrete milk through pores in the skin. The platypus also possesses venomous spurs on its hind limbs, making it one of the few venomous mammals, and generates bioelectrical fields that aid in sensing prey underwater.

## Evolutionary Significance

Monotremes provide crucial insights into early [[concepts/mammalian-evolution|mammalian evolution]]. Their genome contains unusual combinations of reptilian and mammalian genetic traits, including genes for both egg-laying and lactation. The presence of ten sex chromosomes—compared to the typical two found in other mammals—further [[concepts/highlights|highlights]] their evolutionary distinctiveness. Study of monotremes helps scientists understand how key mammalian features such as milk production evolved from reptilian precursors.
