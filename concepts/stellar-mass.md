# Stellar Mass

Stellar mass refers to the total mass of a star, typically expressed in solar masses (M☉), where one solar mass equals approximately 2 × 10³⁰ kilograms. Stars exhibit a wide range of masses, from approximately 0.08 solar masses for the smallest red dwarfs to over 100 solar masses for the most massive stars observed. Stellar mass is a fundamental parameter that directly determines a star's luminosity, core temperature, nuclear fusion rate, lifespan, and evolutionary trajectory.

## Mass-Luminosity Relationship

For main-sequence stars, there is a strong correlation between mass and luminosity, described by the mass-luminosity relation. More massive stars burn their nuclear fuel at a significantly higher rate, resulting in greater brightness but shorter lifespans. This relationship implies that high-mass stars evolve rapidly through their life cycles, often ending in supernova explosions, while low-mass stars burn their fuel slowly and persist for billions or trillions of years.

## Sub-solar Mass Objects and Gravitational Waves

While traditional stellar evolution models suggest a lower mass limit for stars formed via gravitational collapse, the detection of sub-solar mass objects challenges this boundary. Recent gravitational wave evidence has identified compact objects with masses below the typical stellar formation threshold. These detections are critical for investigating the existence of primordial black holes and other exotic compact objects that may have formed in the early universe rather than through standard stellar processes.
