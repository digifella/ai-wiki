# Egg Cooking

Egg cooking involves applying heat to transform the proteins and fats within the egg, resulting in diverse textures and flavors. The process relies on the differential denaturation temperatures of egg components: egg white proteins typically denature between 60–65°C, while yolk proteins require higher temperatures of approximately 65–70°C. This thermal gap allows for precise control over the final consistency, enabling cooks to achieve states ranging from soft, runny yolks with set whites to fully firm structures.

## Temperature Control and Texture

Precise temperature management is critical for determining the structural integrity of the cooked egg. By maintaining heat below the yolk's denaturation threshold, the white can coagulate while the yolk remains liquid or custard-like. Conversely, sustained heating above 70°C causes both components to fully solidify, resulting in a firm texture. Techniques such as poaching, boiling, and sous-vide cooking utilize specific time-temperature combinations to exploit these thermal properties, allowing for reproducible outcomes ranging from jammy to hard-set yolks.

## Common Methods

Scrambled eggs rely on continuous agitation during heating to create small, soft curds by preventing large protein networks from forming. In contrast, fried or poached eggs involve minimal movement, allowing the white to set around the intact yolk. Baking eggs in dishes like quiches or frittatas uses ambient oven heat to gently coagulate the proteins throughout the mixture, often incorporating other ingredients that influence the final moisture content and texture. Each method manipulates the rate of heat transfer and physical stress on the protein structures to achieve distinct culinary results.
