## Pigeon Navigation: Superparamagnetic Macrophages in Liver and Spleen Explain Earth's Magnetic Field Sensing

### Summary
The video explores the long-standing mystery of how [[concepts/homing-pigeons|homing pigeons]] navigate, particularly their ability to find their way home even over vast distances and without visual cues. Historically, pigeons have been relied upon for communication for thousands of years, from ancient Egyptians to military forces and even for sending personal messages. Despite this long relationship, the exact mechanism behind their "internal compass" remained elusive. The presenter introduces the scientific quest to understand this ability, noting that previous theories about bird magnetoreception, such as light-dependent chemical magnetoreception in the eye (cryptochromes) or iron particles in the beak connected to the trigeminal nerve, lacked definitive evidence or failed to explain navigation in complete darkness.

A recent study from the University of Bonn and the Max [[entities/planck|Planck]] Institute of Animal Behavior may have finally uncovered a crucial mechanism. The research revealed that homing pigeons possess surprisingly strong magnetic properties in their liver and spleen, compared to other body parts and other animals. Through electron microscopy, scientists identified specific white blood cells called macrophages within the liver that contain iron-rich ferritin, exhibiting a property known as superparamagnetism. This means these tiny magnetic nanoparticles align with [[concepts/protective-magnetic-field|Earth's magnetic field]], and their orientation shifts with temperature changes, potentially creating a "magnetic flip" that the birds can sense.

Crucially, these [[concepts/superparamagnetic-macrophages|superparamagnetic macrophages]] were found to be located within two micrometers of nerve fibers in the liver, suggesting a direct [[concepts/connection|connection]] to the pigeon's brain. To test this, researchers conducted an elegant [[concepts/scientific-experiment|experiment]] where they divided pigeons into two groups. One group received a drug that temporarily wiped out macrophages in their liver, while the other was a control. On a clear, sunny day, both groups navigated home easily, as pigeons primarily use the sun for navigation in good visibility. However, on an overcast day when the sun was not visible, the pigeons with impaired liver macrophages became completely lost, displaying random spatial orientation, while the control group found their way back in about 70 minutes.

This groundbreaking study provides definitive [[concepts/proof|proof]] that these liver macrophages are essential for homing pigeons' navigation, particularly when visual cues are unavailable. It suggests a dual navigation system where the sun is preferred in daylight, but the magnetic field becomes absolutely necessary in overcast or nighttime conditions. The discovery significantly broadens our understanding of animal navigation, with scientists now suspecting that similar quantum immune cell [[concepts/causes|mechanisms]] might exist in other migrating animals like whales, sharks, bats, and even blind mole-rats. This profound insight into how life interacts with Earth's physical forces also raises intriguing questions about whether humans might retain any vestige of such magnetic sensing capabilities, often referred to as a "gut feeling."
