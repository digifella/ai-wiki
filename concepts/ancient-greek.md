# Ancient Greek

[[concepts/ancient-greek-mathematics|Ancient Greek mathematics]] emerged as a distinctive intellectual tradition between the 6th and 4th centuries BCE, representing a fundamental shift in how mathematical knowledge was conceived and practiced. Rather than viewing mathematics primarily as a practical tool for [[concepts/computation|computation]] and record-keeping, Greek mathematicians developed abstract [[concepts/theoretical-frameworks|theoretical frameworks]] and established formal logical [[concepts/proof|proof]] as the standard for mathematical truth. This approach transformed geometry from a collection of empirical rules into a deductive [[concepts/science|science]], where conclusions were derived from axioms and previously proven theorems.

The tradition is often characterized by its focus on geometry and [[concepts/number-theory|number theory]], with figures such as Pythagoras, Euclid, and Archimedes playing pivotal roles. Euclid’s *Elements*, compiled around 300 BCE, became the most influential mathematical text in history, systematizing the geometric knowledge of the time and establishing the method of proof that would dominate Western mathematics for over two millennia. The Greeks also made significant advances in understanding [[concepts/irrational_number|irrational numbers]] and the concept of infinity, although their reliance on geometric methods sometimes limited the development of algebraic notation.

While earlier civilizations like the Babylonians and Egyptians possessed sophisticated computational techniques for [[concepts/astronomy|astronomy]] and [[entities/national-academies|engineering]], the Greek contribution lay in the [[concepts/abstraction-layer|abstraction]] and [[concepts/abstraction|generalization]] of these practices. They sought universal truths rather than specific solutions, laying the groundwork for the rigorous standards of [[concepts/reasoning-skills|logical reasoning]] that define modern mathematics. This intellectual legacy persisted through the Hellenistic period and was later preserved and expanded upon by scholars in the Islamic [[entities/earth|world]] and medieval [[entities/europe|Europe]].
