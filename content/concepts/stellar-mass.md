# Stellar Mass

Stellar mass refers to the mass of stars, typically measured in solar masses (M☉), where one solar mass equals approximately 2 × 10³⁰ kilograms. Stars exist across a wide range of masses, from about 0.08 solar masses for the smallest red dwarfs to over 100 solar masses for the most massive stars known. Stellar mass is a fundamental property that determines a star's luminosity, lifetime, and ultimate fate.

## Sub-Solar Mass Objects

Objects with masses below that of the Sun have become increasingly important in astronomical research. These sub-solar mass objects include brown dwarfs, planetary-mass objects, and potentially [[concepts/primordial-black-hole|primordial black holes]]—hypothetical [[concepts/black-holes|black holes]] formed in the [[concepts/early-universe|early universe]] rather than from stellar collapse. The detection and characterization of such objects presents unique observational challenges, as their low masses make them inherently difficult to observe through conventional electromagnetic means.

## Gravitational Wave Detection

Recent advances in gravitational wave [[concepts/astronomy|astronomy]] have opened new avenues for detecting sub-solar mass objects. Gravitational waves produced by the collision or merger of low-mass compact objects can reveal their presence even when direct electromagnetic observation is infeasible. Evidence from gravitational wave detectors has suggested possible detections of sub-solar mass compact objects, which could include primordial black holes or other exotic remnants from the early universe. These observations provide independent confirmation of object masses and properties that complement traditional astronomical techniques.
