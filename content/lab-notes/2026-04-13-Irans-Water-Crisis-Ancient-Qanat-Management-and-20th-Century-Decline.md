## Iran's Water Crisis: Ancient Qanat Management and 20th Century Decline
**Clip title:** Iran's Alarming Water Crisis
**Author / channel:** Asianometry
**URL:** https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=aaEhNTpvEN8

### Summary
Iran [[concepts/faces|faces]] a severe and long-standing water crisis, stemming from its inherently [[concepts/arid-geography|arid geography]] and low average annual precipitation, ranking 158th globally. Historically, much of the country's central lands were uninhabitable without sophisticated water management. The nation's water problem is compounded by the fact that 75% of its limited rainfall occurs on only 25% of its land, primarily during winter months, leaving vast areas dry and dependent on [[concepts/engineered-solutions|engineered solutions]].

For millennia, ancient Persians demonstrated remarkable ingenuity in water management with the "Qanat" system. These gently sloping, underground tunnels efficiently channeled snowmelt from mountains to plains using [[concepts/gravitational-pull|gravity]], minimizing evaporation and allowing for water [[entities/storage|storage]]. This sustainable approach enabled the flourishing of the [[concepts/persian-empire|Achaemenid Empire]] across a vast, dry territory. The Qanat system continued to expand for centuries, with the Qajar Dynasty even building over 500 [[concepts/qanats|Qanats]] to supply its new capital, Tehran, and constructing over 270,000 kilometers of these underground waterways across the country.

However, the 20th century brought rapid [[concepts/population-growth|population growth]] and modernization efforts, particularly after World War I, which saw the settlement of nomadic populations. This increased demand pushed Iran towards hydraulic mega-projects like dams and electric pumps for groundwater, leading to the decline of the Qanat system. Government [land reforms](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Land_reforms) in the 1960s further disrupted traditional landlord-maintained Qanats, which subsequently fell into disrepair. By 2000, Qanats, which once supplied 75% of Iran's water in 1945, accounted for less than 10% of the national water supply.

Post-1979 Islamic Revolution, Iran embarked on an aggressive [industrialization](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Industrialization) of its water infrastructure, becoming one of the world's biggest dam builders with over 588 dams and more under construction. Driven by a national security imperative for [food self-sufficiency](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Food_self-sufficiency), 93% of Iran's water is now allocated to [[concepts/agriculture|agriculture]], with [[concepts/policies|policies]] that heavily subsidize water and fertilizer, leading to highly inefficient irrigation practices. This strategy, coupled with a population that has quadrupled since 1960, has resulted in shrinking lakes like Urmia and Hamun, [groundwater depletion](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Groundwater_depletion), increased [soil salinity](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Soil_salinity), destructive [salt and dust storms](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Salt_and_dust_storms), and [land subsidence](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Land_subsidence) that damages infrastructure and permanently reduces aquifer capacity. The decentralized water [[concepts/governance|governance]] system, split across multiple ministries, further hinders a coherent, integrated approach to solving the crisis.

In conclusion, Iran's water crisis is a complex interplay of increasing demand, inefficient usage, and fragmented management. While [[concepts/population-growth|population growth]] is a contributing factor, the core issue lies in unsustainable policies that prioritized supply-side solutions without adequately addressing demand reduction or the long-term environmental consequences. The severe ecological and economic impacts, including agricultural decline and widespread [land degradation](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Land_degradation), underscore the urgent need for comprehensive, integrated water management reforms that balance societal needs with [environmental sustainability](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Environmental_sustainability), even if such reforms prove challenging for a populace accustomed to heavily subsidized water.
