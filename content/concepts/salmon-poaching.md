# Salmon Poaching

Salmon poaching is a classical French cooking technique in which salmon fillets or steaks are gently cooked in a flavored liquid, typically cider, combined with a [[concepts/cream-sauce|cream-based sauce]]. The method originates from Normandy, a region in northern France known for both its salmon fisheries and cider production. This technique represents a traditional approach to preparing salmon that emphasizes delicate, moist results through the controlled application of low to [[entities/medium|medium]] heat.

## Preparation and Method

The salmon is poached by simmering it in cider, which imparts a subtle sweetness and acidity to the fish. The cooking liquid is typically combined with cream, aromatics such as shallots, and sometimes white wine or fish stock to create a cohesive sauce. The poaching process typically takes 10-15 minutes, depending on the thickness of the salmon, and results in tender flesh that absorbs the flavors of the cooking medium. The remaining poaching liquid is often reduced and finished with cream to create a sauce served alongside the cooked salmon.

## Characteristics

This method produces salmon with a delicate [[concepts/texture|texture]] and subtle flavor profile, as the gentle heat and moist cooking environment prevent the fish from drying out. The combination of cider's natural sweetness with cream creates a balanced sauce that complements rather than overpowers the salmon's natural taste. The technique is particularly suited to salmon fillets of uniform thickness, which cook evenly in the liquid.
