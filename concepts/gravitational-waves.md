# Gravitational Waves

Gravitational waves are ripples in the curvature of spacetime that propagate outward from their source at the speed of light. Predicted by Albert Einstein in 1916 as a consequence of his general theory of relativity, these waves are generated by the acceleration of massive objects, particularly during violent cosmic events such as the merger of black holes or neutron stars. They carry unique information about their origins and the nature of gravity, providing a new method for observing the universe that complements traditional electromagnetic astronomy.

The first direct detection of gravitational waves occurred in 2015 by the Laser Interferometer Gravitational-Wave Observatory (LIGO), confirming a major prediction of general relativity. Subsequent observations have identified signals from binary black hole mergers and neutron star collisions. These detections have opened the field of gravitational-wave astronomy, allowing scientists to study compact objects and test the limits of gravitational theory in strong-field regimes.

Recent research has focused on analyzing these signals to identify the nature of the merging objects, including the search for sub-solar mass primordial black holes. Primordial black holes are hypothetical black holes that formed in the early universe, distinct from those formed by stellar collapse. Detecting mergers involving sub-solar mass objects would provide strong evidence for their existence, as such masses cannot be produced by standard stellar evolution processes.

Current and future detectors, such as LIGO, Virgo, KAGRA, and the planned space-based LISA mission, aim to improve sensitivity and frequency coverage. This expanded observational capability allows for more precise measurements of source parameters and the potential discovery of new classes of gravitational wave sources. The analysis of these signals continues to refine models of black hole formation and the distribution of mass in the universe.
