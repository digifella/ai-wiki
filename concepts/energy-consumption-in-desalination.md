# Energy Consumption In Desalination

[[concepts/energy-consumption|Energy consumption]] is a defining characteristic of [[concepts/desalination-technology|desalination technology]] and represents one of its primary [[concepts/operational-costs|operational costs]] and environmental considerations. Desalination processes require substantial inputs of electrical power for mechanical separation and thermal energy for evaporative methods. The specific energy demand varies significantly depending on the desalination method employed, the salinity of the source water, and the desired product water quality.

## Energy Requirements by Technology

Reverse osmosis (RO) is the most energy-efficient desalination method currently in widespread use, relying primarily on high-[[concepts/pressure|pressure]] pumps to force saline water through semi-permeable membranes. Modern RO systems typically require between 3 and 4 kilowatt-hours per cubic meter of freshwater produced, approaching the theoretical minimum energy limit for the separation of salt from water. This efficiency makes RO the dominant technology for large-scale municipal and industrial applications where electricity is the primary [[concepts/energy-source|energy source]].

Thermal desalination processes, such as [[concepts/msf-technology|multi-stage flash]] (MSF) distillation and multi-effect distillation (MED), utilize heat to evaporate water and condense it into freshwater. These methods generally require significantly more energy than RO, often demanding both thermal energy and electricity for auxiliary systems. While MSF is energy-intensive, MED can be more efficient when coupled with industrial waste heat or cogeneration plants, allowing for the utilization of low-grade thermal energy that would otherwise be wasted.

## Influencing Factors and Efficiency

The salinity of the source water directly impacts energy demand; higher concentrations of dissolved solids require greater pressure in RO systems or more thermal energy in distillation processes to achieve the same output. Pretreatment requirements also contribute to the overall energy footprint, as removing suspended solids, organic matter, and [[concepts/computational-scaling|scaling]] ions necessitates additional pumping and chemical dosing. Advances in membrane materials and energy recovery devices have progressively lowered the [[concepts/energy-demand|energy intensity of desalination]], but the process remains more energy-demanding than conventional water treatment methods for freshwater sources.
