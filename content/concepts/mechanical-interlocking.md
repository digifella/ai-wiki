# Mechanical Interlocking

Mechanical interlocking is a fundamental engineering principle in which two or more components lock together through direct physical engagement rather than adhesives, [[concepts/buttons|fasteners]], or external forces. The locked state is maintained by the geometric interaction of the parts themselves, where the shape and positioning of one component prevents another from moving or separating. This approach to joining or securing objects has [[concepts/software|applications]] across numerous industries and everyday products.

## Zippers as a Primary Example

The zipper exemplifies mechanical interlocking at scale. A zipper consists of two parallel rows of small metal or plastic teeth that interlock when a slider moves along them, joining two fabric edges together. The teeth on each side are precisely engineered to nest together, creating a [[concepts/secure|secure]] closure that can be repeatedly opened and closed. This design emerged from earlier clasp-locking mechanisms and was refined into the modern zipper system that became standardized across the clothing and luggage industries.

## Principles and Applications

Mechanical interlocking relies on precise manufacturing and tight tolerances to function effectively. The physical dimensions and spacing of engaging components must be carefully controlled to ensure reliable locking without jamming or accidental [[concepts/disengagement|disengagement]]. Beyond zippers, mechanical interlocking appears in puzzle joints, interlocking bricks, chain links, and various mechanical assemblies where components must remain securely coupled under operational [[concepts/stress|stress]].
