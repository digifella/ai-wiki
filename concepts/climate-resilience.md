# Climate Resilience

Climate resilience describes the capacity of Earth's physical systems—including the atmosphere, hydrosphere, biosphere, and geosphere—to withstand, adapt to, and recover from climate disturbances and variability. These interconnected systems maintain their essential functions when subjected to shocks such as extreme weather events, prolonged droughts, or rapid temperature shifts. The concept encompasses both the inherent stability of natural environments and their ability to reorganize following significant perturbations.

## Ecological and Systemic Dimensions

In ecological contexts, resilience refers to the ability of ecosystems to absorb change and continue functioning despite stressors. This involves biodiversity, which provides functional redundancy, allowing species to compensate for one another when environmental conditions change. Healthy ecosystems with high structural complexity generally exhibit greater resilience, as they possess more pathways to maintain energy flow and nutrient cycling during disruptions.

## Human and Health Implications

For human societies, climate resilience extends beyond environmental stability to include the robustness of infrastructure, economies, and public health systems. In the domain of health and wellbeing, resilience is defined by the ability of communities to prevent, prepare for, and respond to climate-related health risks, such as heat stress, vector-borne diseases, and food insecurity. Building this capacity requires integrated strategies that address social determinants of health, improve early warning systems, and ensure equitable access to resources during and after climate events.
