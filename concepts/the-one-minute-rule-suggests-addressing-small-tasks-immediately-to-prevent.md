# The One Minute Rule Suggests Addressing Small Tasks Immediately To Prevent

The [[entities/one-minute-rule|one-minute rule]] is a [[concepts/productivity|productivity]] principle that recommends completing tasks that take one minute or less immediately upon identification, rather than postponing them. The underlying premise is that deferring small tasks creates unnecessary [[concepts/friction|friction]] in workflow and allows minor items to accumulate into a burdensome backlog. By addressing these tasks in the moment, practitioners aim to maintain [[concepts/clarity-slider|clarity]] and momentum in their work environment.

## Origins and Application

The rule emerged from broader [[concepts/daily-routines|daily routine]] optimization and time management literature, particularly in organizational contexts where administrative tasks frequently interrupt primary work. The practice gained prominence through productivity frameworks that emphasize [[concepts/decision-making|decision-making]] efficiency. [[entities/employees|Workers]] applying the rule process items such as responding to brief messages, filing documents, or clearing desk clutter immediately rather than creating a separate task list for later handling.

## Benefits and Trade-offs

Advocates argue that immediate completion prevents small tasks from consuming disproportionate mental energy through repeated deferral decisions, a phenomenon related to decision fatigue. The approach can reduce context-[[concepts/vendor-lock-in|switching costs]] by bundling decisions with execution. However, the rule assumes that immediate task completion genuinely requires only one minute and that interrupting current work carries acceptable costs. In deep work contexts requiring sustained concentration, frequent task interruptions may reduce overall productivity despite clearing individual items quickly.

## Practical Considerations

Effectiveness of the one-minute rule depends on accurate time estimation and the [[concepts/nature-of-work|nature of work]] being performed. Knowledge workers with flexible schedules may find it more applicable than those with rigid workflow requirements or time-sensitive projects. The rule works best when combined with other organizational systems that capture tasks exceeding the one-minute threshold for later processing.
