# Lippmann Photography

Lippmann photography is a color photography process invented by French physicist Gabriel Lippmann in 1891. Rather than relying on chemical dyes or pigments to reproduce color, the technique captures color through optical interference—the interaction of light waves with themselves. The process involves placing a fine-grain photographic emulsion in direct contact with a mercury mirror. When light enters the plate from the front, it reflects off the mirror and interferes with the incoming light waves, creating microscopic stationary interference patterns within the emulsion.

These interference patterns record the wavelength and phase of the incident light, effectively storing the spectral information of the original scene. Because the color is generated by the physical structure of the recorded layers rather than by absorbing or reflecting specific wavelengths via pigments, the resulting images exhibit structural color. This mechanism allows the photographs to display colors that are physically present in the light field, distinct from colors created by chemical subtraction or addition.

The distinction between true color and illusion is central to understanding the significance of this method. In conventional photography, color is an illusion created by the selective absorption of light by dyes. In Lippmann photography, the color is a direct physical manifestation of the light’s properties at the moment of exposure. This makes the process a unique intersection of physics and art, demonstrating how structural color can be permanently fixed in a medium without the use of traditional colorants.

Although the process is largely obsolete for practical photography due to its complexity and the requirement for large-format plates, it remains historically significant for its contribution to the understanding of wave optics and color reproduction. It serves as a foundational example of how interference can be harnessed to capture and reproduce the full spectrum of visible light, influencing later developments in holography and structural color research.
