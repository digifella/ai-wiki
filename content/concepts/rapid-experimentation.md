# Rapid Experimentation

Rapid experimentation refers to a methodological approach in AI and [[concepts/coding|software development]] that leverages [[concepts/automated-feedback-loops|automated feedback]] loops to accelerate agent or system improvement. Rather than relying on manual [[concepts/testing|testing]] and human-directed [[concepts/iteration|iteration]] cycles, this approach employs continuous [[concepts/automated-software-testing|automated testing]] at high frequency, enabling [[concepts/agents|agents]] to explore [[concepts/solution|solution]] spaces and refine their behavior with minimal human intervention between cycles.

The core mechanism involves establishing closed-loop systems where agents execute tasks, performance is measured against [[concepts/defined-metrics|defined metrics]], and results feed directly into the next iteration of optimization. This [[concepts/automation|automation]] removes traditional bottlenecks associated with human review and decision-making, allowing for orders of magnitude more experiments to be conducted within the same time period. Researchers and practitioners can thereby observe patterns and convergence trajectories that would be impractical to discover through manual iteration alone.

This approach has implications for how development timelines are structured and how human oversight functions. Where traditional development might complete dozens of iterations in a given timeframe, automated rapid experimentation can execute hundreds or thousands. This shift changes the [[entities/nature|nature]] of human involvement from directing each step toward setting initial conditions, constraints, and success criteria, then analyzing higher-level patterns in the results.

The effectiveness of rapid experimentation depends critically on the quality of the [[concepts/feedback|feedback]] signal and the relevance of the metrics guiding optimization. When metrics accurately reflect desired outcomes, frequent iteration can produce meaningful improvement. When metrics are misaligned with actual goals, rapid experimentation can efficiently move in undesired directions. The approach thus requires careful measurement design alongside the technical infrastructure for automated loops.
