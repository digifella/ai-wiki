# Lens Compression

Lens compression is an optical effect where objects at varying distances appear closer together than they physically are. This phenomenon occurs when using longer focal lengths, which have a narrower field of view that alters the perceived spatial relationships within a scene. The effect is not caused by the lens itself changing the distance between objects, but rather by the camera's position relative to the subject and the angle of view, which flattens depth relationships in the final image.

The degree of compression becomes more pronounced with increasingly longer focal lengths. For instance, a 200mm lens produces a more noticeable flattening of depth compared to an 85mm lens. This characteristic is often utilized in photography and cinematography to isolate subjects from their background or to create specific compositional effects where distant elements appear layered closely together.

In the context of panoramic photography, this principle is applied through shooting and stitching techniques. By capturing multiple images and combining them, photographers can create wide-angle compositions that mimic the spatial characteristics of long lens compression. This allows for the creation of expansive views that maintain the flattened depth perspective typically associated with telephoto optics, despite the wide field of view.
