# Theoretical Computer Science

Theoretical computer [[concepts/science|science]] (TCS) is the mathematical study of computation itself. It uses formal methods and mathematical proofs to understand the fundamental [[concepts/capabilities|capabilities]] and limitations of computers and algorithms. Rather than focusing on practical [[concepts/adoption|implementation]], TCS examines what problems can be solved computationally, how efficiently they can be solved, and whether certain problems are inherently unsolvable by any algorithm.

## Core Areas

The field encompasses several interconnected domains. Computability [[concepts/theory|theory]] investigates which problems are decidable—that is, which can be solved by an algorithm in finite time. [[concepts/complexity-classes|Computational complexity]] theory classifies problems by the resources (time and [[concepts/memory|memory]]) required to solve them, leading to concepts like the [[concepts/a-video-titled-p-vs-np|P versus NP]] problem. Formal language theory and automata theory study abstract computational [[concepts/models|models]] and the languages they can recognize, providing foundations for understanding programming languages and parsing.

## Methods and Significance

Theoretical computer science relies heavily on mathematical [[concepts/proof|proof]] and formal [[concepts/reasoning|reasoning]] rather than experimentation. Researchers develop theorems about [[concepts/algorithm-optimization|algorithmic efficiency]], prove lower bounds on problem difficulty, and establish connections between different computational models. These theoretical insights underpin practical advances in [[concepts/cryptography|cryptography]], optimization, [[concepts/ai-technologies|artificial intelligence]], and [[concepts/software|software]] [[concepts/design|design]], making TCS essential to both computer science education and research.
