# Water Scarcity

Water scarcity occurs when the available supply of fresh water is insufficient to meet the demands of human populations and natural ecosystems. This condition is driven by multiple interconnected factors, including uneven geographic [[concepts/distribution|distribution]] of water resources, overextraction from aquifers and rivers, pollution of freshwater sources, and the growing demands created by [[concepts/population-growth|population growth]] and economic development. While Earth contains abundant water overall, approximately 97% exists as salt water, leaving only a small fraction of fresh water readily accessible for human use.

## Causes and Regional Impacts

Population growth intensifies water stress in already water-limited regions, particularly in arid and semi-arid zones across the Middle East, North Africa, South Asia, and parts of Central Asia. Climate variability and changing [[concepts/precipitation-patterns|precipitation patterns]] further complicate water availability. Industrial [[concepts/agriculture|agriculture]], which accounts for the majority of freshwater withdrawals globally, competes directly with municipal and environmental needs. Some regions, such as Iran, face particularly acute crises where groundwater depletion threatens both agricultural viability and urban water security.

## Technological and Management Responses

[[concepts/desalination-technology|Desalination technology]] has emerged as a significant tool for coastal regions to supplement freshwater supplies, though large-scale [[concepts/desalination-plants|desalination plants]] require substantial energy inputs and create concentrated brine discharge. Other approaches include improved irrigation efficiency, wastewater treatment and recycling, transboundary water agreements, and demand management through conservation. However, technological solutions alone cannot address the fundamental challenge of balancing growing human needs with environmental water requirements and the limits of natural water cycling.
