# Qanats

Qanats are underground aqueduct systems designed to transport water across arid and semi-[[concepts/desert-landscapes|arid landscapes]] for agricultural irrigation and domestic use. The system consists of a series of vertical access shafts connected by gently sloping underground tunnels that move water from a water source—typically an aquifer or spring—to the surface where it can be distributed for irrigation. The slight downward gradient of the [[concepts/tunnel|tunnel]] allows [[concepts/gravitational-pull|gravity]] to move water reliably across considerable distances with minimal evaporation losses, a critical advantage in dry climates where surface water channels would lose substantial volumes to sun and wind.

## History and Geography

Qanat technology originated in ancient [[concepts/iran|Persia]] and has been in continuous use for over two millennia. Systems spread throughout the Middle East, North Africa, Central Asia, and into Spain during medieval times. Iran remains home to the world's largest concentration of functioning qanats, with thousands of systems still in operation. The technology represents an engineering adaptation specifically suited to [[concepts/water-scarcity|water scarcity]], allowing societies in arid regions to sustain [[concepts/agriculture|agriculture]] in otherwise inhospitable environments.

## Modern Status

Many [[concepts/qanat-management|qanat systems]] have fallen into decline since the twentieth century due to the development of motorized pumping technology, changing irrigation practices, and shifts in [[concepts/water-management|water resource management]]. In some regions, over-extraction of groundwater and changing [[concepts/precipitation-patterns|precipitation patterns]] have lowered water tables, affecting qanat viability. However, renewed interest in sustainable water management has prompted efforts to maintain and restore functioning qanats as both [[concepts/cultural-heritage|cultural heritage]] and practical water infrastructure.
