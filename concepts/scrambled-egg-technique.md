# Scrambled Egg Technique

Scrambled eggs are a fundamental cooking technique that achieves tender, creamy results through careful control of three primary variables: [[concepts/salt-timing|salt timing]], liquid ratios, and equipment selection. Each factor directly influences the final [[concepts/texture-slider|texture]] and [[concepts/moisture-content|moisture content]] of the dish. Inconsistent application of these principles commonly produces either rubbery, overcooked eggs or watery, underdone results.

## Salt Timing

The timing of salt addition significantly affects [[concepts/protein-structure|protein structure]] and moisture [[concepts/storing|retention]]. Adding salt too early can draw moisture from the eggs through osmosis, resulting in a weeping [[concepts/texture|texture]] during cooking. For optimal results, salt should be added either immediately before cooking begins or after the eggs have started to set, once the proteins have begun to denature and are less susceptible to premature moisture loss.

## Liquid and Heat Management

The ratio of liquid to eggs influences both texture and [[concepts/cooking-time|cooking time]]. Whole eggs contain roughly 75% water; adding milk, cream, or additional liquid increases moisture content but requires careful [[concepts/temperature-management|heat management]] to prevent overcooking. Lower, more consistent heat allows proteins to coagulate gradually, creating a creamier texture than high heat, which [[concepts/causes|causes]] rapid [[concepts/whirlpool-technique|protein bonding]] and moisture expulsion. Constant [[concepts/stirring-technique|stirring]] redistributes heat evenly and prevents large curds from forming.

## Equipment Considerations

The choice of [[concepts/cooking-equipment|cookware]] affects heat distribution and moisture evaporation. Non-stick surfaces allow for lower heat settings and easier stirring without sticking, while stainless steel conducts heat more intensely. Pan size also matters: a smaller pan concentrates heat and reduces evaporation, while a larger pan disperses heat more gradually. Heavy-bottomed pans provide more even, controllable heating than thin cookware.
