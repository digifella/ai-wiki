# Poaching Method

Poaching is a moist-heat cooking technique in which food is gently simmered in liquid at temperatures below boiling, typically between 160–180°F (70–82°C). The surrounding liquid, known as the poaching medium, conducts heat gradually to cook the food evenly while maintaining its delicate structure. This method is particularly valued for producing tender results while preserving the natural flavour and texture of the ingredient, making it suitable for proteins and fruits that can become tough or dry with more aggressive cooking methods.

## Poaching Media

The choice of poaching liquid varies widely depending on the food and desired flavour profile. Common poaching mediums include water, stock, broth, wine, cider, milk, or combinations thereof. For fish and seafood, light stocks and court-bouillon (a seasoned broth with vegetables and herbs) are traditional choices. Poultry may be poached in chicken stock, while fruits are often poached in sugar syrups or wine. The poaching medium itself contributes flavour to the food while also becoming infused with the ingredient's essence, creating a concentrated liquid that can be reduced into a sauce or used as a base for other preparations.

## Applications

Poaching is commonly applied to fish, eggs, chicken, and fruits. The gentle heat prevents the proteins from tightening excessively, resulting in moist, tender textures. Regional preparations such as Normandy-style poached salmon, cooked in cider and finished with cream sauce, demonstrate how the poaching medium directly shapes the final dish's character. The method's reliance on careful temperature control makes it more time-intensive than some cooking techniques, but the superior results justify this attention for delicate ingredients.
