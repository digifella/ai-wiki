# Hinduism

Hinduism is one of the [[entities/earth|world]]'s major religions, with approximately one billion adherents primarily in [[entities/india|India]] and Nepal, alongside significant diaspora communities globally. Unlike religions founded by a single figure or text, Hinduism developed organically over more than three thousand years through the integration of Vedic, indigenous, and philosophical traditions. This extended historical development produced a religious system of considerable internal diversity, encompassing multiple schools of thought, worship practices, and philosophical frameworks that coexist without requiring doctrinal uniformity.

## Core Texts and Philosophy

The religion's foundational texts include the Vedas, Upanishads, Bhagavad Gita, and various Puranas, which collectively address metaphysics, [[concepts/ethics|ethics]], and practical spirituality. Central concepts include dharma (duty or righteousness), karma (action and consequence), and moksha (liberation from the cycle of rebirth). Hindu [[concepts/philosophy|philosophy]] encompasses both theistic and non-theistic schools, with some traditions emphasizing devotion to deities and others focusing on abstract principles or meditation practices.

## Worship and Practice

Hindu practice varies widely across regions and communities. Many Hindus worship specific deities such as Brahma, Vishnu, Shiva, or the goddess Devi, while others understand these as manifestations of a single ultimate reality called Brahman. Common practices include puja (ritual worship), meditation, yoga, pilgrimage, and observance of festivals such as Diwali and Holi. The caste system, historically central to Hindu social organization, remains socially significant despite legal prohibition of caste-based discrimination in modern India.

## Contemporary Context

Modern Hinduism continues to evolve as practitioners navigate globalization, migration, and social change. Hindu philosophical concepts and practices, particularly yoga and meditation, have gained widespread [[concepts/adoption|adoption]] beyond traditional Hindu communities. The religion remains deeply embedded in Indian cultural and political identity, while also maintaining distinct institutional and philosophical presence in diaspora communities worldwide.
