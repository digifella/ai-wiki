# Invasive Species

**Invasive species** are non-native organisms whose introduction [[concepts/causes|causes]] or is likely to cause economic or environmental harm or harm to human [[concepts/health|health]]. They disrupt local ecosystems by outcompeting [[concepts/native-flora|native flora]] and fauna, altering habitat structures, and changing nutrient cycles.

## Characteristics & Impact
- **Rapid proliferation**: Lack of natural predators allows [[concepts/exponential-growth|exponential growth]].
- **Resource competition**: Displacement of native species through superior [[concepts/metabolic-uptake|resource acquisition]] ([[concepts/light|light]], water, nutrients).
- **Habitat alteration**: Modification of soil chemistry, fire regimes, or hydrology.
- **Hybridization**: Genetic dilution of native populations.

## Management Strategies
- **Eradication**: Physical removal or chemical control (herbicid
- **Biological Control**: Exploiting unique biological vulnerabilities of the invasive population. For example, recent efforts in [[concepts/australia|Australia]] regarding the [[concepts/cane-toad|Cane Toad]] (*Rhinella marina*) involve leveraging intraspecific cannibalism to control populations. See [[lab-notes/2026-10-11-Australian-Cane-Toad-Control-Leveraging-Intraspecific-Ca|Australian Cane Toad Control: Leveraging Intraspecific Cannibalism]] for details on this specific intervention.

## Case Study: Cane Toad in Australia
- **Origin**: Introduced to Queensland in 1935 from Hawaii (originally from Puerto Rico) to combat [[concepts/sugar|sugar]] cane beetles.
- **Outcome**: Disastrous spread due to lack of natural predators and high reproductive rates.
- **Current Control**: Scientific efforts focus on exploiting the toad's unique biology, specifically intraspecific cannibalism, to mitigate their impact on native [[concepts/biodiversity|biodiversity]].
