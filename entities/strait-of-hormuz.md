# Strait Of Hormuz

The Strait of Hormuz is a narrow waterway situated between Iran and Oman, serving as the primary maritime link between the Persian Gulf and the Gulf of Oman, which opens into the Arabian Sea. At its narrowest point, the strait spans approximately 54 nautical miles (100 kilometers). Its geographic position makes it one of the world's most critical maritime chokepoints, facilitating the movement of global energy resources. Approximately one-third of all globally traded seaborne crude oil passes through this corridor, alongside significant volumes of liquefied natural gas.

## Economic and Strategic Significance

The strait’s importance stems from its role in global energy supply chains. Major exporters in the Persian Gulf, including Saudi Arabia, the United Arab Emirates, Kuwait, Qatar, and Iraq, rely on this route to transport hydrocarbons to international markets. Disruptions to traffic in the strait can lead to immediate volatility in global oil and gas prices, as alternative routes are either insufficient in capacity or prohibitively expensive. Consequently, the stability of the Strait of Hormuz is closely monitored by global economic analysts and military strategists alike.

## Geopolitical Risks and Conflict Impact

Tensions between Iran and Western nations, particularly the United States, have historically raised concerns about the potential closure of the strait. In the event of a conflict involving Iran, the region could face blockades, mine-laying, or attacks on shipping infrastructure. Such scenarios would likely cause a sharp spike in oil market prices due to supply fears, even if actual physical disruptions are limited. Economic models suggest that a prolonged closure could result in price increases exceeding 100%, triggering significant global economic repercussions.
