# Gamma Rays

[[entities/gamma|Gamma]] [[concepts/rays|rays]] are the highest-energy form of [[concepts/light|electromagnetic radiation]], produced by radioactive decay, nuclear reactions, and extremely energetic cosmic phenomena. They have wavelengths smaller than one trillionth of a meter and can penetrate most materials, making them both scientifically valuable and hazardous to biological tissue. Detection and analysis of gamma rays from space requires specialized satellite and ground-based observatories, as Earth's atmosphere blocks most cosmic gamma radiation.

## Dark Matter Detection

Recent observations of gamma ray emissions from the [[concepts/milky-way|Milky Way]] have provided new avenues for [[concepts/dark-matter|dark matter]] research. [[concepts/wimp-particles|Weakly Interacting Massive Particles]] ([[concepts/wimps|WIMPs]]), a leading dark matter candidate, are theorized to annihilate when they encounter their [[concepts/antimatter|antimatter]] counterparts, producing gamma rays as a byproduct. By analyzing the spectrum and spatial [[concepts/distribution|distribution]] of gamma rays in galactic regions with high predicted dark matter concentrations, researchers can search for signatures consistent with [[concepts/particle-annihilation|WIMP annihilation]] that would distinguish this signal from gamma rays produced by known astrophysical sources.

## Astrophysical Sources

Gamma rays from space originate from various natural phenomena, including supernova remnants, [[concepts/neutron-stars|neutron stars]], [[concepts/black-holes|black hole]] accretion, and cosmic ray interactions with interstellar gas. Ground-based observatories have identified some of the universe's most powerful [[concepts/high-energy-particles|cosmic ray sources]] through their gamma ray emissions. Distinguishing between these conventional sources and potential dark matter signals requires careful analysis of the gamma ray spectrum's characteristics and spatial patterns across the galaxy.
