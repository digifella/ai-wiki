# Rock Rusting

Rock rusting is a geological process involving the oxidation of iron-bearing minerals within rocks upon exposure to atmospheric oxygen. This form of chemical weathering typically occurs at or near the Earth's surface, where primary minerals such as iron silicates and iron sulfides react with oxygen. The reaction transforms these primary minerals into secondary iron oxide minerals, primarily hematite and goethite. The resulting iron oxide deposits impart a characteristic rust-red coloration to the affected rock formations.

This phenomenon serves as a visible indicator of long-term atmospheric changes and weathering history. It became particularly significant during periods of high atmospheric oxygen levels, such as the Great Oxidation Event, when the availability of free oxygen accelerated the oxidation of surface iron. The process is not limited to ancient geological eras but continues to operate in modern environments where iron-rich rocks are exposed to air and moisture.

The most prominent example of this process is found in the interior of Australia. The vast red landscapes of the Australian outback are largely the result of rock rusting over millions of years. As ancient rocks containing iron minerals were exposed to the atmosphere, the oxidation process created extensive layers of iron oxides, giving the region its distinctive color and influencing soil composition across the continent.
