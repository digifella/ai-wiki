# Goldbach Conjecture

Goldbach's conjecture is one of the oldest and most famous [[concepts/unsolved-problems|unsolved problems]] in [[concepts/number-theory|number theory]] and [[concepts/mathematics|mathematics]]. First proposed by the Prussian mathematician Christian Goldbach in 1742 in a letter to Euler, it makes a deceptively simple claim: every even integer greater than 2 can be expressed as the sum of two [[concepts/prime-numbers|prime numbers]]. Despite centuries of effort by mathematicians, the conjecture remains unproven, though it has been verified computationally for extraordinarily large numbers.

## Statement and Variants

The conjecture exists in two main forms. The strong conjecture, as stated above, concerns even integers greater than 2. A weaker variant, known as the ternary or weak Goldbach conjecture, asserts that every odd integer greater than 5 can be expressed as the sum of three primes. The weak conjecture was proven in 2013 by Harald Helfgott, but the strong conjecture—the original and more celebrated version—remains open.

## Mathematical Significance

The conjecture's appeal lies partly in its elegant simplicity and partly in its deep [[concepts/connection|connection]] to the [[concepts/distribution|distribution]] of prime numbers. While no general proof exists, computational verification has tested the conjecture for even numbers up to extraordinarily large values (in the range of 4 × 10¹⁸), finding no counterexamples. Various partial results and related theorems have been established, narrowing the possible scope of any counterexample, but a complete proof remains elusive and continues to challenge mathematicians.
