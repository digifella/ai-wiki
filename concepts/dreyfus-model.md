# Dreyfus Model

The [[entities/dreyfus-and-dreyfus|Dreyfus Model]] is a [[concepts/framework-for-understanding|framework for understanding]] [[concepts/skill|skill]] acquisition developed by Stuart and Hubert Dreyfus in the 1980s. It describes five stages of competence that practitioners progress through as they develop [[concepts/expertise|expertise]]: novice, advanced beginner, competent, proficient, and expert. The model emphasizes that skill development involves fundamental shifts in how learners perceive problems and apply knowledge, rather than simple linear accumulation of [[concepts/factual-knowledge|facts]] and procedures.

## Stages of Competence

Novices follow rigid rules and lack [[concepts/contextual-understanding|contextual understanding]], relying entirely on explicit [[concepts/instructions|instructions]] to perform tasks. Advanced beginners begin to recognize situational aspects and can handle simple, concrete tasks but still struggle with prioritization. Competent practitioners can plan and solve problems systematically, relying on conscious [[concepts/decision-making|decision-making]] and [[concepts/critical-thinking|analytical thinking]] to manage complex situations.

Proficient individuals perceive situations holistically rather than as a collection of parts, allowing them to recognize patterns and adjust their approach based on context. Experts operate from an intuitive deep understanding, where decision-making becomes automatic and subconscious. They no longer rely on rules or principles but instead draw upon a vast reservoir of [[concepts/experience|tacit knowledge]] to navigate novel and ambiguous [[concepts/scenarios|scenarios]] effectively.
