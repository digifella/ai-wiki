# Attachment Theory

Attachment theory is a psychological framework developed primarily by John Bowlby in the 1950s that explains how early relationships between infants and caregivers shape emotional and social development. The theory proposes that children form emotional bonds with primary caregivers as an evolutionary adaptation for survival, with proximity-seeking behavior serving protective functions. Bowlby argued that the quality of these early attachments significantly influences personality development, emotional regulation, and later relationship patterns throughout life.

## Core Mechanisms

The theory identifies several key attachment patterns. Secure attachment develops when caregivers respond consistently and sensitively to an infant's needs, promoting confidence and healthy exploration. Insecure attachment patterns—including anxious-ambivalent, avoidant, and disorganized types—emerge when caregiving is inconsistent, neglectful, or unpredictable. These patterns reflect an infant's adaptive responses to their caregiving environment and tend to persist into adulthood, affecting romantic relationships, parenting behavior, and social functioning.

## Later Developments

Mary Ainsworth expanded attachment theory through systematic observation and introduced the "Strange Situation" procedure to measure attachment security in laboratory settings. Subsequent researchers including Mary Main and Donna Hesse developed attachment classifications for adults and identified how childhood attachment patterns can transmit across generations. Contemporary attachment research examines the neural basis of attachment, cultural variations in attachment expression, and applications in therapeutic interventions for trauma and relationship difficulties.
