# Muon G-2 Experiment

The Muon g-2 experiment is a precision [[concepts/physics|physics]] experiment conducted at Fermilab that measures the [[concepts/anomalous-magnetic-moment|anomalous magnetic moment]] of the muon, a fundamental particle similar to an electron but approximately 200 times heavier. The experiment seeks to determine how the muon's magnetic properties deviate from theoretical predictions based on the [[concepts/standard-model-of-particle-physics|Standard Model of particle physics]]. By comparing experimental measurements with theoretical calculations, physicists can identify potential discrepancies that might indicate previously undiscovered particles or forces.

## Methodology and Significance

The experiment works by storing muons in a magnetic field and observing how they precess, or spin, as they move through the field. The rate of this precession is directly related to the muon's magnetic moment. Fermilab's version of the experiment builds on decades of prior work, including earlier measurements at Brookhaven National Laboratory. The precision required is extraordinarily high, as the experiment must measure values accurate to several [[concepts/decimal-places|decimal places]] to reveal any meaningful deviations from theoretical expectations.

## Recent Developments

Results from the Muon g-2 experiment have suggested potential hints of new physics beyond the Standard Model, though the significance of these anomalies remains subject to ongoing theoretical and experimental scrutiny. Updated results were expected in 2025 as the collaboration continued to refine measurements and improve systematic uncertainties. These findings have important implications for understanding fundamental physics and the possible existence of yet-undiscovered particles.
