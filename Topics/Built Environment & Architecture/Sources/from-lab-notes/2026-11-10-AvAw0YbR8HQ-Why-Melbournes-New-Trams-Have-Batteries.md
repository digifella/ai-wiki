## Why Melbourne's New Trams Have Batteries
**Clip title:** Why Melbourne's New Trams Have Batteries
**Author / channel:** AussieTransit
**URL:** https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=AvAw0YbR8HQ
**Credibility:** Commentary (tier 1 of 5). YouTube video from AussieTransit: not edited or peer reviewed, so its claims are the speaker's own. Summary written by Gemini.

### Summary
The video discusses Melbourne’s new G-class trams, highlighting their distinctive battery-powered capabilities and the reasoning behind their implementation. Unlike traditional electric trams powered solely by overhead wires, the G-class carries lithium-ion batteries on its roof. These batteries assist with acceleration, providing a burst of power when the tram pulls away from stops and reducing the immediate electrical demand placed on the overhead grid.

The video explains how these batteries help manage the high current demands of larger, heavier modern trams, which require significantly more electrical power than older models like the Z and A classes. By utilizing stored energy from the batteries and regenerative braking—where kinetic energy from braking is converted back into electrical energy—the G-class reduces the need for expensive upgrades to the city’s electrical substations and cables.

In conclusion, the G-class trams represent a modernization of Melbourne’s public transport network, combining increased capacity, lower floors for better accessibility, and advanced energy management systems. The ongoing rollout, supported by infrastructure projects like the Maidstone depot and route upgrades, aims to replace older models and improve overall network efficiency by 2029.

### Video Description & Links
#### Description
Melbourne's new G Class trams still run under overhead wires, yet each carries two lithium-ion batteries on its roof. This video explains why: how the 600-volt DC supply, its substations and cables set limits, why a larger tram draws a burst of current when it pulls away, how the batteries help it accelerate while the pantograph stays on the wire, how they charge, and how regenerative braking recovers some of the energy. It also looks at what else has to change: raised platforms, the Droop Street stops on Route 82, the new Maidstone depot and the timetable of the hundred-tram programme.

Notes
The contract awarded to Alstom in 2022 was worth approximately 700 million euros and includes fifteen years of maintenance; the video's rough A$1 billion is our conversion at the European Central Bank reference rate of 21 April 2022 (1 euro = 1.4653 Australian dollars, about A$1.03 billion), not a figure published in dollars. The A$424.5 million is the Victorian Budget 2026-27 total for the wider Tram infrastructure upgrades package (the Maidstone depot, upgrades at Southbank Depot and land for new power substations): it is not the depot's price and is not added to the A$1.85 billion programme. Mid-2029 is the estimated completion of the Next Generation Trams project in the same budget papers (the last quarter of 2028-29): a project date, not a date for tram number 100. The maximum motor demands (550 amps for the older Z3 and A Class trams, 1,200 amps for the E Class) come from a Yarra Trams standard and describe how much current the motors can need at once, not what they use over a journey. The battery explanations (assist while the pantograph stays on the wire, trickle charging, regenerative braking) follow an interview with the Department of Transport and Planning's engineers and the department's fact sheet; the drawings are schematics, not to scale, and the G Class is an illustration. The maps use OpenStreetMap data: the Droop Street stops are the mapped tram stops, and the track renewal is drawn along the streets the works notice closes (Droop, Hopkins and Leeds streets); the notice gives 1.4 kilometres of track but no map of its extent.
