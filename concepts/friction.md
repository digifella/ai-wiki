# Friction

Friction is a resistive force that opposes the relative motion or the tendency of such motion between two surfaces in contact. Acting parallel to the interface, it always operates in a direction opposite to the motion or potential motion between the objects. This force arises from microscopic irregularities on the surfaces and electromagnetic interactions between the atoms at the points of contact. It plays a fundamental role in both natural phenomena and engineered systems, influencing everything from vehicle braking and machinery operation to geological stability.

The phenomenon is generally categorized into static friction, which prevents the initiation of motion, and kinetic friction, which acts against ongoing sliding motion. Static friction is typically greater than kinetic friction, meaning more force is required to start moving an object than to keep it moving. The magnitude of the frictional force is proportional to the normal force pressing the surfaces together, a relationship defined by the coefficient of friction, which varies depending on the materials involved and the condition of their surfaces.

In addition to sliding friction, other forms include rolling friction, which occurs when an object rolls over a surface, and fluid friction, which resists the motion of objects through liquids or gases. Understanding these forces is critical in fields ranging from mechanical engineering, where minimizing wear and energy loss is essential, to geophysics, where friction determines the stability of slopes and the mechanics of tectonic plate movement.
