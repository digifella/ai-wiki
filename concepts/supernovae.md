# Supernovae

Supernovae are extremely energetic stellar explosions that occur when certain stars reach the end of their lives. During a supernova event, a star releases enormous amounts of energy across the electromagnetic spectrum, temporarily becoming as bright as billions of normal stars. The explosion can emit more light in a few weeks than the Sun will produce over its entire 10-billion-year lifespan. This extreme brightness makes supernovae visible from vast distances across the universe, allowing astronomers to detect them even in distant galaxies.

## Types and Mechanisms

There are two primary categories of supernovae, distinguished by their progenitor stars and physical mechanisms. Type Ia supernovae occur in binary systems where a white dwarf accretes matter from a companion star until it reaches the Chandrasekhar limit, triggering a thermonuclear runaway that completely disrupts the star. These events serve as standard candles in cosmology due to their consistent peak luminosity, enabling the measurement of cosmic distances and the discovery of the universe's accelerated expansion.

Core-collapse supernovae, classified as Type II, Ib, or Ic, result from the gravitational collapse of massive stars (typically more than eight times the mass of the Sun) at the end of their nuclear fusion cycles. When the stellar core can no longer support itself against gravity, it collapses into a neutron star or black hole, while the outer layers are ejected in a violent shockwave. These explosions are crucial for nucleosynthesis, dispersing heavy elements such as iron, gold, and uranium into the interstellar medium, which subsequently enriches future generations of stars and planetary systems.
