## Making One Enantiomer: 2026 Nobel Prize in Chemistry Explained
**Clip title:** Making One Enantiomer: 2026 Nobel Prize in Chemistry Explained
**Author / channel:** Chemistry in a Nutshell
**URL:** https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=bkZTr_Q1rQo

### Summary
The video discusses the concept of chirality in chemistry, focusing on molecules that are non-superimposable mirror images of each other, known as enantiomers. The speaker uses the analogy of hands and screws to explain how enantiomers work. While many biological molecules exist as mixtures of enantiomers, life itself is homochiral, utilizing only one specific version of molecules like amino acids and DNA. 

The video highlights the 2026 Nobel Prize in Chemistry awarded to Henri B. Kagan and Kenso Soai for their groundbreaking research. Kagan discovered a way to create more of one enantiomer using a chiral catalyst, and Soai developed a reaction that forms only one enantiomer from achiral starting materials. This research is crucial for pharmaceutical production, allowing for safer, purer drugs, and sheds light on the origins of life's homochirality.

#### Description
The 2026 Nobel Prize in Chemistry has just been awarded to Henri Kagan and Kenso Soai for their ground-breaking discoveries in asymmetric synthesis, non-linear effects, and autocatalysis, solving the origin of life’s asymmetry. Every protein in your body is "left-handed." But in a lab flask, nature stubbornly gives you a 50/50 mixture. This isn't just a curiosity: get the "hand" wrong in pharmaceutical manufacturing, and you risk disaster (like the Thalidomide tragedy).

For decades, chemists couldn't force a reaction to make just one pure mirror image without the other.
