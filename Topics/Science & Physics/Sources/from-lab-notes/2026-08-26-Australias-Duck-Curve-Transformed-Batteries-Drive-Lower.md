## Australia's Duck Curve Transformed: Batteries Drive Lower Electricity Prices
**Clip title:** Australia Killed the Duck Curve (With Batteries)
**Author / channel:** Engineering with Rosie
**URL:** https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=jltRq_KlGH4

### Summary
This video, presented by mechanical engineer [[entities/rosie-barnes|Rosie Barnes]], explains why [[concepts/electricity-prices|electricity prices]] in Australia are finally dropping for most households and small businesses, attributing the change to the increasing integration of [[concepts/renewable-energy|renewable energy]] and [[concepts/battery-storage|battery storage]] into the grid. The core concept revolves around transforming the "[[concepts/duck-curve|duck curve]]" of electricity demand and generation into a more stable "platypus curve."

Barnes first details Australia's unique electricity pricing system, where generators bid from cheapest to most expensive every five minutes. The grid then dispatches power from as many generators as needed to meet demand. Critically, *all* generators receive the price set by the *last and most expensive* generator dispatched in that five-minute window. This system meant that while abundant midday solar provided near-zero cost power (creating the "belly" of the duck curve, often leading to negative prices as coal plants bid low to stay online), the evening peak, when solar disappears and demand spikes (the "head" of the duck), was traditionally met by expensive gas peaking plants. Consequently, even with cheap solar, gas prices often dictated the overall market price during these critical hours, preventing consumer bills from reflecting the true cost reduction of renewables.

The significant change that has flattened this steep evening price spike, thus turning the duck curve into a platypus curve, is the widespread adoption of grid-scale batteries. These batteries charge during the day using cheap, surplus solar power, effectively soaking up the excess and flattening the duck's belly. Crucially, they then discharge during the evening peak, undercutting the previously dominant gas peaking plants. Data from late 2024 and 2025 shows a dramatic decrease in evening peak prices from nearly $500/MWh to around $100/MWh, with batteries now setting the marginal price during the evening peak almost 40% of the time.

This structural shift is now directly impacting consumer bills. From July 1st, electricity prices are set to fall by 3-10% for most Australians. Furthermore, new initiatives like the "Solar Sharer Offer" provide households (even those without rooftop solar) three hours of free power during the midday solar surplus. This encourages consumers to shift high-demand activities like running dishwashers or charging electric vehicles to these free periods, further balancing the grid. Barnes concludes that this represents a successful and gradual energy transition, where technological advancements like battery storage create economic shifts that ultimately benefit consumers.
