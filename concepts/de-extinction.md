# De Extinction

De extinction refers to scientific efforts to restore extinct species through reproductive cloning and genetic engineering techniques. The concept proposes using preserved DNA from extinct animals to create viable offspring, potentially reintroducing lost species to ecosystems they once inhabited. Proposed approaches include cloning from preserved genetic material and reconstructing genomes through genetic engineering based on closely related extant species.

Current de extinction strategies employ two primary methods. The first involves somatic cell nuclear transfer, where the nucleus of a somatic cell from the extinct species is inserted into an enucleated egg cell of a closely related living species. The second method utilizes advanced genetic engineering to edit the genomes of living relatives, gradually introducing traits of the extinct species until a viable proxy is created. These techniques aim to reverse biodiversity loss and restore ecological functions previously managed by the extinct organisms.

A significant twenty-year Japanese study on mammalian serial cloning has provided critical insights into the biological limitations of these methods. The research concluded that the process of serial cloning leads to a genetic dead end, as accumulated genetic errors and telomere shortening compromise the viability and health of cloned offspring over successive generations. This finding highlights substantial technical hurdles in using cloning alone for de extinction, suggesting that genetic engineering may be necessary to correct such defects for long-term success.
