# Cognitive Biases

Cognitive [[concepts/biases|biases]] are systematic patterns in human [[concepts/human-cognition|thinking]] that cause people to deviate consistently from rational judgment. Rather than processing information objectively, humans tend to filter, interpret, and [[concepts/recall|recall]] information in ways that are shaped by their existing beliefs, emotions, motivations, and social contexts. These biases are not character flaws but reflect how [[concepts/human-intelligence|human cognition]] necessarily simplifies complex information to enable faster [[concepts/decision-making|decision-making]].

## Common Types

Well-documented cognitive biases include confirmation bias (seeking information that confirms existing beliefs), availability heuristic (overweighting easily recalled examples), anchoring bias (relying too heavily on initial information), and hindsight bias (perceiving past events as more predictable than they were). Social biases such as in-group bias and attribution errors affect how people interpret others' behavior and group membership. These patterns emerge across cultures and contexts, suggesting they reflect fundamental aspects of how human minds process reality.

## Implications

The existence of cognitive biases has significant implications for [[concepts/philosophy|philosophy]], epistemology, and practical decision-making. It complicates questions about the [[entities/nature|nature]] of knowledge and rational judgment, since even careful thinking remains subject to systematic distortions. In practical domains from medicine to law to business, awareness of cognitive biases has prompted the development of decision-support systems and institutional checks designed to counteract these patterns.
