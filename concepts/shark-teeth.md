# Shark Teeth

Shark teeth are specialized dental structures fundamentally different from those of mammals in both their attachment and replacement mechanisms. Rather than being anchored permanently in bone sockets, shark teeth attach to the jaw through connective tissue fibers, allowing them to be shed and replaced continuously throughout an animal's lifetime. This modular design has proven highly successful across hundreds of millions of years of shark evolution, enabling rapid adaptation to different prey and feeding strategies.

## Diversity of Forms

The variety of shark tooth morphologies reflects adaptations to different ecological niches and feeding behaviors. Tooth shapes range from the serrated blades of great whites, suited for cutting flesh, to the flattened crushing plates of nurse sharks, which process hard-shelled prey. Some extinct shark species possessed more unusual dental structures, including tooth-like rostral saws, anvil-shaped teeth, and elaborate spike formations that have since been lost through evolution. These varied forms demonstrate how dental architecture directly correlates with predatory specialization.

## Replacement and Growth

Shark teeth develop in multiple rows within the jaw, with new teeth continuously replacing worn or lost ones. This conveyor-belt system of replacement allows sharks to maintain functional dentition throughout their lives without the biological constraints that limit tooth renewal in most vertebrates. The rate of replacement varies among species and can accelerate in response to damage or heavy use, ensuring that feeding efficiency remains uncompromised regardless of wear.
