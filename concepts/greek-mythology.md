# Greek Mythology

Greek mythology comprises the collection of myths, deities, and [[concepts/storytelling|narrative]] traditions that emerged from ancient Greece, spanning from the Bronze Age through the Classical period and beyond. These stories served multiple functions in Greek society, including religious observance, [[concepts/cumulative-culture|cultural transmission]], and entertainment. The myths were preserved through oral tradition before being recorded in written form by poets and historians such as Homer and Hesiod during the archaic and classical periods. Later Greco-Roman writers including Ovid further documented and adapted these narratives for subsequent audiences.

## Major Deities and Cosmology

Greek mythology centers on a pantheon of twelve Olympian deities, including Zeus, Hera, Poseidon, and Athena, who were believed to inhabit Mount Olympus. These gods possessed human characteristics—including flaws and emotions—while maintaining immortality and supernatural powers. The mythology also encompasses a complex [[concepts/cosmology|cosmology]] involving Titans, giants, and various supernatural beings, as well as an underworld ruled by Hades. Creation myths describe how the gods came to power and established their dominion over the mortal [[entities/earth|world]].

## Narrative Traditions and Legacy

Key narrative cycles include the Trojan War, the adventures of Heracles, and the journey of Odysseus, which were transmitted through [[entities/epic-systems|epic]] poetry, drama, and [[concepts/visual-arts|visual arts]]. These stories explored themes of heroism, fate, hubris, and the relationship between mortals and immortals. Greek mythology influenced subsequent Western literature and [[concepts/philosophy|philosophy]], and its deities and narratives were adopted and adapted by the Romans, ensuring their continued prominence in Western culture through the medieval and modern periods.

## Divine and Mortal Power

The tension between divine and mortal power structures much of Greek mythology. While gods possess superhuman abilities, they remain subject to fate and bound by cosmic laws they did not establish. Mortals, conversely, achieve power through cunning, [[concepts/courage|courage]], or divine favor rather than inherent [[concepts/excellence|superiority]]. This framework—where power derives from multiple sources and remains perpetually negotiable—maps onto modern discussions of institutional authority, individual agency, and structural constraint.


## Contemporary Relevance

Greek mythology's treatment of power continues to inform contemporary ethical and political discourse. The hubris of those who overreach, the consequences of unchecked authority, and the possibility of challenging established hierarchies remain recognizable through mythological narratives. From literature to political [[concepts/theory|theory]], these ancient frameworks provide conceptual language for examining how power operates, circulates, and shifts across different domains of human [[concepts/experience|experience]].
