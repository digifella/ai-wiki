# Seasoning Timing

Salt is a fundamental seasoning tool for home cooks, and its effective use depends on understanding different types and their physical properties. The most common culinary salts—table salt, kosher salt, and sea salt—differ in crystal size and density, which affects how they measure and dissolve. Table salt has fine, compact crystals and is denser, meaning a teaspoon contains more salt by weight than the same volume of kosher salt. Sea salt and kosher salt have larger, irregular crystals with more air space between them, resulting in lower density and requiring larger volumes to achieve equivalent saltiness.

## Measuring and Application

Because of these density differences, direct volume substitution between salt types is imprecise. A recipe calling for one teaspoon of table salt will taste noticeably saltier than one teaspoon of kosher salt. Home cooks should either weigh salt in grams for consistency or adjust quantities when switching types. Kosher salt's larger crystals make it easier to pinch and control by hand, which is why many cooks prefer it for finishing dishes and seasoning to taste during cooking.

## Timing in Cooking

When salt is applied matters for texture and flavor development. Salt added early in cooking dissolves completely and distributes evenly throughout a dish, seasoning it thoroughly. Salt applied at the end of cooking—particularly larger-crystal varieties—can provide bursts of salinity and textural contrast. Understanding these distinctions helps home cooks develop seasoning practices suited to different dishes and cooking techniques.
