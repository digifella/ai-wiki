# De Extinction

De extinction refers to the scientific effort to restore extinct species through reproductive [[concepts/cloning|cloning]] and genetic engineering techniques. The most well-known theoretical application involves using preserved DNA from extinct animals to create viable offspring, potentially reintroducing lost species to ecosystems they once inhabited. Various approaches have been proposed, including cloning from preserved genetic material and reconstructing genomes through genetic engineering.

## Genetic Limitations of Serial Cloning

A twenty-year Japanese study on mammalian [[concepts/serial-cloning|serial cloning]]—the process of repeatedly cloning organisms derived from cloned parents—concluded that the technique leads to a genetic dead end. The research demonstrated that successive generations of clones accumulate genetic degradation, making the approach impractical for de extinction purposes. This finding suggests that simply cloning from cloned individuals introduces compounding genetic errors that ultimately render populations non-viable.

## Current State and Challenges

De extinction remains largely theoretical, with significant technical and biological hurdles remaining. Beyond the genetic complications identified in the Japanese study, de extinction projects face challenges in obtaining sufficiently intact DNA from preserved specimens, recreating complex ecosystems needed to sustain restored species, and addressing ethical questions about introducing engineered organisms into modern environments. Current research focuses on understanding these limitations rather than implementing full-scale de extinction [[concepts/software|programs]].
