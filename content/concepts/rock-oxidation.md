# Rock Oxidation

Rock oxidation is a geochemical process in which rocks and minerals undergo chemical alteration through [[concepts/exposure|exposure]] to oxygen, typically in the presence of water. This weathering mechanism involves the transfer of electrons from mineral components to oxygen, resulting in the formation of new mineral phases, commonly oxides and hydroxides. The process occurs at Earth's surface and subsurface and contributes significantly to landscape evolution and soil formation over geological timescales.

## Geological Context

Rock oxidation has varied in intensity throughout Earth's history in correlation with atmospheric oxygen concentrations. During periods of elevated atmospheric oxygen, oxidation rates increased, affecting the chemistry of surface rocks and the composition of sediments being deposited. The timing and extent of oxidation in different geological intervals has influenced the [[concepts/preservation|preservation]] and alteration of organic material, including the formation environments of coal and petroleum deposits. These fossil fuel deposits reflect the redox conditions of their source environments, with oxidation state playing a role in determining whether organic matter accumulated under oxygen-rich or oxygen-poor conditions.

## Modern Significance

Understanding rock oxidation remains relevant to current geological and environmental studies. The process influences mineral weathering rates, affects groundwater chemistry, and contributes to the long-term carbon cycling at Earth's surface. Rock oxidation also has practical implications for mining, construction, and the stability of exposed geological materials in engineered environments.
