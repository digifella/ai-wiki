# Seasoning Techniques

Seasoning is the practice of enhancing food flavor through the addition of salt, spices, herbs, and other flavor compounds. Effective seasoning requires understanding how different salt types and their crystalline structures affect flavor distribution and intensity. Table salt, kosher salt, and sea salt have different densities and grain sizes, which means they don't measure equally by volume and dissolve at different rates during cooking. This variation directly impacts how evenly seasoning distributes throughout a dish and how pronounced the saltiness becomes.

## Salt Varieties and Application

The physical properties of salt significantly influence culinary outcomes. Kosher salt typically has larger, flakier crystals that are easy to pinch and distribute evenly by hand, making it a preferred choice for seasoning meat and vegetables. Table salt is finely ground and often contains anti-caking agents, resulting in a higher density by volume; consequently, substituting table salt for kosher salt in recipes can lead to over-salting if measured by volume. Sea salt varies widely in grain size and mineral content, with fine sea salt behaving similarly to table salt and coarse varieties offering a distinct textural crunch.

## Timing and Method

The timing of salt application alters the chemical interaction between the seasoning and the food. Salting vegetables before cooking draws out moisture through osmosis, which can aid in browning and caramelization. Adding salt to liquids during the early stages of cooking allows the ions to penetrate deeper into the food matrix, seasoning the interior rather than just the surface. Conversely, finishing a dish with salt just before serving highlights the immediate flavor profile and provides a sharper, more distinct salty taste on the palate.

## Integration with Other Ingredients

Seasoning is not limited to salt; it involves the strategic layering of spices and herbs to build complexity. Dry spices should often be toasted or bloomed in fat to release their essential oils, enhancing their aromatic potential. Herbs are generally categorized by their durability, with hardy herbs like rosemary and thyme added early in the cooking process to withstand heat, while delicate herbs like basil and cilantro are added at the end to preserve their fresh flavor and color. Balancing these elements requires an understanding of how heat, time, and moisture affect the volatility and solubility of flavor compounds.
