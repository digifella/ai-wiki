# Rgb Light

RGB light refers to the additive color model based on red, green, and blue wavelengths, which forms the foundation of how digital displays generate color by emitting light. The human eye contains three types of cone cells sensitive to different wavelengths: long (red, ~700 nm), medium (green, ~546 nm), and short (blue, ~435 nm). The brain combines signals from these receptors to perceive the full spectrum of visible colors. By varying the intensity of these three primary wavelengths, digital displays—including screens, projectors, and LEDs—can produce the range of colors visible to the human eye through metamerism, where different spectral power distributions are perceived as identical colors.

This mechanism contrasts with structural color, which relies on the physical interaction of light with microstructures to create color through interference, diffraction, or scattering. While RGB technology simulates color perception by stimulating specific cone responses, it does not replicate the spectral purity of natural structural colors. This distinction highlights the difference between true spectral coloration and the perceptual illusion created by additive mixing, a concept historically linked to Lippmann photography’s ability to record true spectral data rather than just intensity.

The reliance on additive mixing means that RGB displays cannot reproduce the entire gamut of natural colors, particularly those with high spectral saturation found in structural coloration. Consequently, the "true color" of an object as defined by its physical properties differs from the "perceived color" generated by RGB systems. Understanding this gap is critical in fields such as color science and display technology, where the goal is often to maximize the approximation of natural spectral responses within the constraints of the RGB additive model.
