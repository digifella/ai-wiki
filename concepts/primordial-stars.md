# Primordial Stars

Primordial stars, theoretically designated as Population III stars, represent the first generation of stellar objects to form in the universe. They are predicted to have emerged within the first few hundred million years after the Big Bang, arising from the primordial gas cloud composed almost entirely of hydrogen and helium. Unlike subsequent stellar generations, these early stars formed from pristine material with negligible heavy element content, a composition that fundamentally distinguished their physical properties and evolutionary paths from modern stars.

Theoretical models suggest that Population III stars were substantially more massive and luminous than contemporary stars, potentially reaching hundreds of times the mass of the Sun. Due to their lack of metallicity, they burned their nuclear fuel at an accelerated rate, leading to short lifespans of only a few million years. Their formation and subsequent supernova explosions were critical to the chemical enrichment of the early universe, seeding subsequent generations of stars and galaxies with heavier elements necessary for planet formation and life.

Recent astronomical observations have provided indirect evidence supporting the existence of these early stars. Data from the James Webb Space Telescope (JWST) has identified signatures consistent with Population III characteristics in the distant galaxy GN-z11. These findings align with cosmological models predicting that the earliest structures in the universe were dominated by massive, metal-free stars, marking a pivotal epoch in cosmic history known as the cosmic dawn.
