# Early Galaxy Formation

Early galaxy formation refers to the processes by which galaxies assembled during the universe's first billion years, a period critical to understanding cosmic structure and evolution. Theoretical models developed over recent decades predicted that galaxies would grow gradually through mergers and accretion, with the most massive galaxies forming relatively late in cosmic history. These predictions were grounded in our understanding of gravitational collapse, dark matter dynamics, and the distribution of baryonic matter in the early universe.

## Current Observational Challenges

Observations from the James Webb Space Telescope (JWST) have revealed galaxies in the early universe that appear significantly more massive and mature than existing models predict. These galaxies, observed at redshifts corresponding to only a few hundred million years after the Big Bang, challenge the timeline and mechanisms of galaxy assembly. The discrepancy suggests either that galaxies formed more rapidly than previously theorized, or that existing formation models require substantial revision to account for more efficient star formation or mass assembly in the early universe.

## Implications for Galaxy Formation Theory

These observations have prompted renewed investigation into the physical processes governing early galaxy formation. Possible explanations include revised understanding of star formation efficiency, the role of supermassive black holes in early galaxy evolution, or modifications to dark matter models and their effects on structure formation. Resolving these tensions between theory and observation remains an active area of research with implications for broader models of cosmic structure and the universe's evolutionary history.
