# Jazz Harmony

Jazz [[concepts/musical-harmony|harmony]] is the system of chord construction and progression that underpins jazz music. Unlike classical harmony, which follows strict rules of [[concepts/tone|voice]] leading and functional [[concepts/tonal-music|tonality]], jazz harmony is more flexible and experimental. It emphasizes extended chords (those with added ninths, elevenths, and thirteenths), altered dominant chords, and chromatic [[concepts/exercise|movement]]. These expanded harmonic palettes allow musicians to create richer, more colorful sounds and greater expressive [[concepts/range|range]].

## Chord Types and Extensions

The foundation of jazz harmony rests on seventh chords and their extensions. Major seventh, minor seventh, and dominant seventh chords form the basic vocabulary, but jazz musicians regularly add color tones that extend beyond the traditional triad. A C major seventh chord (C-E-G-B) might become a Cmaj9 (adding D) or Cmaj7#11 (adding F#), fundamentally changing its [[entities/sonic|sonic]] character. Altered chords—where the fifth or ninth is raised or lowered—are also common, particularly in dominant chord contexts.

## Scale Relationships and Substitution

Jazz harmony's relationship with [[concepts/musical-scales|scales]] differs from classical practice. Rather than adhering strictly to one scale, jazz musicians employ multiple scales and modes to navigate chord changes. A single chord might be played against different scales depending on the desired color and context. Tritone substitution, where a dominant seventh chord is replaced by another dominant seventh a tritone away, is a characteristic jazz practice that allows for smooth voice leading while maintaining harmonic function.
