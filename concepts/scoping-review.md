# Scoping Review

A scoping review is a systematic methodological approach used to map the breadth of existing literature on a specific topic, concept, or field. Unlike systematic reviews, which aim to answer specific research questions through rigorous quality assessment of primary studies, scoping reviews focus on identifying the types of evidence available, key concepts, and emerging themes within a domain. This process involves a broad search strategy to capture diverse sources, including grey literature, to provide a comprehensive overview of what is known and what remains unclear.

This methodology is particularly valuable for investigating complex or broad topics where evidence may be heterogeneous in nature or methodology. It serves to clarify working definitions, categorize types of evidence, and identify gaps in the existing research. By mapping the landscape of available literature, scoping reviews help researchers and policymakers understand the scope of current knowledge and determine whether a full systematic review is feasible or necessary.

The process typically follows established frameworks, such as those outlined by Arksey and O’Malley or the Joanna Briggs Institute, which guide the stages of the review from formulating the research question to charting the data and collating, summarizing, and reporting the results. These reviews are widely used in health and wellbeing domains to inform future research agendas, develop policy guidelines, and ensure that interventions are based on a thorough understanding of the available evidence base.
