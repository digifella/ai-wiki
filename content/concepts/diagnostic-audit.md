# Diagnostic Audit

A diagnostic audit is a systematic evaluation process used to identify deficiencies, inconsistencies, and areas for improvement in a given [[concepts/output|output]], decision, or strategy. Rather than accepting work [[concepts/assistive-technology|at]] face value, a diagnostic audit applies structured scrutiny to uncover hidden problems—whether factual errors, logical gaps, unsupported claims, or practical limitations. The process is particularly valuable in business contexts where the [[concepts/cost|cost]] of flawed analysis or poor recommendations can be substantial.

## Core Process

A diagnostic audit typically involves three stages. First, the auditor conducts a thorough examination of the subject matter, reviewing all available evidence, assumptions, and [[concepts/reasoning|reasoning]]. Second, the auditor identifies specific deficiencies by cross-referencing claims against [[concepts/verifiable-facts|verifiable facts]], [[concepts/testing|testing]] [[concepts/logical-consistency|logical consistency]], and assessing whether recommendations align with stated objectives. Third, the auditor proposes concrete alternatives or corrections that address the identified weaknesses, along with rationale for the improvements.

## Application and Value

In practice, diagnostic audits are applied to strategic plans, financial analyses, policy recommendations, [[concepts/flow-management|research findings]], and major business decisions. They serve as a [[concepts/quality-control|quality control]] mechanism, reducing the risk of implementing flawed strategies or overlooking critical vulnerabilities. The rigor of a diagnostic audit depends on the auditor's expertise, access to relevant information, and the time allocated to the review process. Organizations typically employ diagnostic audits when the stakes are high or when decisions [[entities/will|will]] have significant consequences for operations, finances, or stakeholder outcomes.
