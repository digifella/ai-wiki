# Empirical Validation

Empirical validation is the process of [[concepts/testing|testing]] theoretical predictions against observations or experiments in the natural world. [[concepts/feynman|Richard Feynman]] articulated a influential three-step framework for this process: first, [[entities/make|make]] a guess (formulate a hypothesis or theoretical prediction); second, [[concepts/compute|compute]] the consequences of that guess using logic and [[concepts/mathematics|mathematics]]; third, [[concepts/feynmans-three-step-scientific-method|compare]] the computed results with actual experimental or [[concepts/empirical-evidence|observational data]] from [[entities/nature|nature]]. This cycle forms the core of the [[concepts/scientific-method|scientific method]] as Feynman understood it.

## The Feynman Framework

Feynman emphasized that a [[concepts/theory|theory]] has value only insofar as it can be tested against reality. The "guess" need not be original or intuitive—it can draw from existing [[concepts/ideas|ideas]], analogies, or mathematical forms—but it must be sufficiently concrete to allow calculation of specific, measurable predictions. The computation step requires rigorous logical and mathematical work to derive what the theory predicts. Only in the third step, comparison with nature, does a theory prove its worth or reveal its limitations. A theory that fails this comparison, no matter how elegant or internally consistent, must be rejected or revised.

## Distinction from Mere Equivalence

This validation approach also underscores [[concepts/knowing-versus-understanding|Feynman's distinction]] between [[concepts/mathematical-equivalence|equivalent theories]] and genuine scientific progress. Two theories may make identical predictions and thus be empirically equivalent, yet differ fundamentally in how they explain phenomena or what they reveal about nature's [[concepts/structure|structure]]. Empirical validation alone cannot distinguish between such theories; genuine progress involves deepening understanding of underlying mechanisms, not merely matching observational data.
