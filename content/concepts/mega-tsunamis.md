# Mega Tsunamis

Mega tsunamis are unusually large-scale [[concepts/tsunami|tsunami]] events capable of producing waves exceeding 100 meters in height. Unlike typical tsunamis generated by underwater [[concepts/earthquakes|earthquakes]], mega tsunamis are typically triggered by catastrophic phenomena that displace enormous volumes of water rapidly. These events are rare but can cause severe coastal devastation.

## Volcanic Flank Collapse

One of the most significant documented examples of a mega tsunami occurred in 1888 near [[entities/ritter-island|Ritter Island]] in Indonesia. During a catastrophic [[concepts/volcanic-eruption|volcanic eruption]], a portion of [[entities/cuba|the island]]'s flank collapsed into the sea, displacing a massive volume of water and generating a tsunami wave estimated [[concepts/assistive-technology|at]] approximately 100 meters tall. This event demonstrated the destructive potential of mega tsunamis and the role that geological instability can play in their formation.

## Other Potential Sources

While volcanic flank collapses represent a well-documented source, mega tsunamis can [[entities/theoretically-media|theoretically]] be generated by other large-scale phenomena, including submarine landslides and meteorite impacts. The rarity of mega tsunami events makes detailed historical records limited, though geological evidence suggests such events have occurred at various points in Earth's history. Understanding the mechanisms and triggers of mega tsunamis remains an area of geological research relevant to hazard assessment in tectonically active regions.
