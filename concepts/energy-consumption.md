# Energy Consumption

Energy consumption represents a critical operational and environmental challenge in desalination processes used to meet agricultural water demands. Desalination—the removal of dissolved salts and minerals from seawater or brackish water—requires substantial energy inputs across all established technologies. The energy intensity of desalination directly affects both the economic viability of projects and their associated greenhouse gas emissions, making energy efficiency a central consideration for sustainable agricultural water supply.

## Energy Requirements by Technology

Different desalination methods demand varying amounts of energy. Reverse osmosis (RO), the most widely deployed technology, typically requires 3–7 kilowatt-hours of electricity per cubic meter of fresh water produced, depending on feed water salinity and system efficiency. Thermal desalination methods, such as multi-effect distillation and reverse osmosis coupled with thermal processes, consume both thermal and electrical energy, often in the range of 10–15 kilowatt-hours per cubic meter. The choice of technology significantly influences operational costs and environmental footprint.

## Economic and Environmental Implications

Energy costs typically represent 40–50% of total desalination operating expenses, making energy consumption a primary driver of water production cost. In regions where desalination supports agricultural irrigation, these energy demands can be substantial relative to overall farm operational budgets. The source of energy—whether fossil fuels, nuclear power, or renewable sources—determines the greenhouse gas emissions profile of desalinated water. Coupling desalination with renewable energy sources such as solar or wind power can substantially reduce carbon emissions, though infrastructure investment and intermittency considerations present practical challenges.
