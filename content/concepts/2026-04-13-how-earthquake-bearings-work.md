# 2026 04 13 How Earthquake Bearings Work

Earthquake bearings are mechanical and structural devices designed to allow buildings and infrastructure to move independently during seismic events. They function by decoupling the motion of a structure's superstructure from its foundation, absorbing and distributing the energy generated by ground movement. This isolation prevents the direct transmission of seismic forces through load-bearing columns and walls, which would otherwise cause cracking, collapse, or catastrophic failure.

## Types and Mechanisms

Common earthquake bearing systems include elastomeric bearings, which use layers of rubber and steel to flex and absorb lateral movement, and friction pendulum systems, which allow structures to slide on curved surfaces with controlled friction. Tuned mass dampers represent another approach, using the inertia of heavy weights suspended within a building to counteract seismic oscillations. Each system is engineered to accommodate specific frequency ranges and displacement magnitudes expected in particular geological regions.

## Application and Design Considerations

Earthquake bearings are most commonly implemented in critical infrastructure such as bridges, hospitals, power plants, and high-rise buildings in seismically active zones. Engineers must calculate bearing specifications based on local seismic hazard assessments, soil conditions, and the expected magnitude and duration of ground motion. The bearings must balance flexibility during earthquakes with sufficient stiffness to support normal building loads and resist wind forces under non-seismic conditions.
