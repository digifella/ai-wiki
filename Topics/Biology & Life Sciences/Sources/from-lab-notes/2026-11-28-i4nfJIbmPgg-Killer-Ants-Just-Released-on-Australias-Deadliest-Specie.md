### Summary
The video tells the story of how Australian scientists recruited native meat ants and cannibal tadpoles to fight the country's worst invader, cane toads. In the 1930s, cane toads were brought to Australia to combat the grey-backed cane beetle, which was destroying sugarcane. However, the toads proved to be the wrong tool for the job, as they quickly multiplied and spread across northern Australia, causing a disaster for local wildlife and ecosystems. The toads are toxic, and their population has exploded, with an estimated 200 million cane toads in the north.

To combat this, researchers have turned to native meat ants and cannibal tadpoles. Scientists have discovered that Australian cane toad tadpoles are enthusiastic cannibals, eating the next generation of cane toads. The team of scientists at Macquarie University has noticed that traps baited with young hatchlings pulled in Australian tadpoles 29.5 times more often than South American ones. Additionally, meat ants, known to scientists as Iridomyrmex reburrus, have been found to target baby cane toads, killing 98% of them within the first few minutes of the bait being put out.

The video concludes that while the use of cannibal tadpoles and meat ants shows promise in controlling the cane toad population, there are still challenges and risks involved. Scientists are working on ways to introduce these methods in a controlled manner, such as through gene editing and targeted baiting, to ensure they do not harm native wildlife. The ongoing efforts highlight the complex and often surprising ways nature can be used to combat invasive species.
