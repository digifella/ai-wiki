## Air India Crash: Human Responsibility and Evolving AI Cognition in Aviation

### Summary
The video, titled "Where Responsibility Begins…And Ends", is the first episode in a multi-part series exploring the complex relationship between [[concepts/human-cognition|human cognition]], [[concepts/ai-technologies|artificial intelligence]], and responsibility, using the history of aviation as a guiding framework. The [[entities/speaker|speaker]], a pilot, begins by referencing a past [[entities/air-india|Air India]] crash, noting that public discourse instinctively sought to assign blame to a human (the pilots, the airline, etc.), rather than to technology, despite extensive technical analysis. This observation highlights the central theme: humans tend to concentrate responsibility in other humans, even as increasingly capable machines perform significant cognitive tasks.

The video traces the historical evolution of "delegated [[concepts/cognition|cognition]]" in aviation through several key stages. Initially, machines learned to **calculate**, exemplified by the Norden Bombsight during WWII, which performed complex ballistic computations and even briefly controlled the aircraft. Next, machines began to **"know"** by continuously updating their understanding of the [[entities/earth|world]], as seen with inertial navigation systems in the 1950s that allowed aircraft and spacecraft to determine their position autonomously. The Apollo [[concepts/recommendations|Guidance]] Computer then marked a period where the machine became an indispensable **partner** in complex missions, sharing the [[concepts/cognitive-load|cognitive load]] with astronauts and mission control. Subsequently, technologies like weather radar, TCAS, GPS, and electronic flight bags enabled machines to **perceive**, extending human senses and providing a richer, real-time understanding of the operational environment. Finally, modern fly-by-wire aircraft demonstrate machines beginning to **recommend**, interpreting pilot intent and executing actions within safe parameters, essentially expressing a "preference" for how to achieve desired outcomes. Throughout all these stages, a consistent pattern emerged: cognition became progressively distributed across human-machine systems, yet ultimate responsibility remained concentrated in the human operator.

This historical journey in aviation offers profound insights into humanity's current reckoning with [[concepts/ai-technologies|artificial intelligence]]. The [[entities/speaker|speaker]] argues that aviation arrived at the question of separating cognition and responsibility long before "AI" became a mainstream concept. As [[concepts/ai-models|AI systems]] become more sophisticated, capable of [[concepts/reasoning|reasoning]] across domains, forming internal [[concepts/world-models|world models]], and even offering [[concepts/recommendations|recommendations]], they challenge the traditional understanding of human responsibility. The fundamental question becomes: if a machine's comprehension and [[concepts/decision-making|decision-making]] capabilities genuinely exceed our own, who is ultimately accountable when something goes wrong? Aviation has consistently upheld human responsibility, but the accelerating capabilities of AI may force society to reconsider where responsibility truly begins and ends, especially when human oversight might no longer fully grasp the intricacies of the machine's operations.
