# Dissonance

Dissonance in music refers to combinations of sounds that are perceived as tense, unstable, or unresolved. The term originates from Latin *dissonare* (to sound apart) and describes intervals or chords that create an expectation for resolution to a more stable harmonic state. Dissonance is fundamentally relational—its effect depends on cultural context, historical period, and the listener's familiarity with musical conventions.

## Harmonic Function

In Western harmonic [[concepts/theory|theory]], dissonance contrasts with consonance, which describes sounds perceived as stable and resolved. Common dissonant intervals include the tritone, minor second, and major seventh, though the classification of intervals has shifted across centuries. Renaissance composers treated the tritone as highly dissonant and avoided it systematically, while 20th-century composers incorporated dissonance as a primary structural element. The resolution of dissonance to consonance traditionally creates a sense of closure and is central to functional [[concepts/musical-harmony|harmony]].

## Perceptual and Cultural Factors

The experience of dissonance varies across cultures and individuals. Listeners acculturated to Western [[concepts/tonal-music|tonal music]] may perceive certain interval combinations as requiring resolution, while listeners from different musical traditions may interpret the same sounds differently. Psychological research suggests that dissonance perception involves both acoustic properties—such as beating frequencies between pitches—and learned musical expectations. Extended [[concepts/exposure|exposure]] to dissonant music can shift listener tolerance and preference.
