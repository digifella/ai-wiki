# Diagnostic Audit

A diagnostic audit is a systematic evaluation process that subjects organizational outputs, decisions, or strategic [[concepts/recommendations|recommendations]] to rigorous scrutiny. Rather than accepting analysis or advice at face value, a diagnostic audit applies structured critique to identify deficiencies, inconsistencies, logical gaps, and unsupported claims. The approach is grounded in the principle that even well-intentioned analysis can contain errors, [[concepts/biases|biases]], or practical limitations that may not be immediately apparent.

## Purpose and Application

Diagnostic audits serve to protect organizations from costly strategic mistakes that can result from flawed [[concepts/reasoning|reasoning]] or incomplete analysis. They are particularly valuable in high-stakes business contexts—such as major capital investments, market entry decisions, or organizational restructuring—where the consequences of poor recommendations are significant. By introducing a layer of independent critical review, diagnostic audits help organizations stress-test their [[concepts/human-cognition|thinking]] before committing to action.

## Process and Methodology

The diagnostic audit process typically involves examining the underlying assumptions, data sources, and reasoning chains within a recommendation or analysis. This includes verifying factual claims, testing the validity of conclusions drawn from evidence, and identifying any logical fallacies or unstated premises. The audit may also assess whether alternative interpretations of the evidence have been adequately considered and whether practical constraints or implementation risks have been sufficiently addressed.

A successful diagnostic audit goes beyond identifying problems to propose constructive improvements. This might involve suggesting alternative approaches, highlighting information gaps that need to be filled, or recommending [[concepts/adjustments|adjustments]] to implementation strategy based on identified weaknesses.
