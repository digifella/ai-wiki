# Qanats

Qanats are underground channel systems engineered to transport water across arid and semi-arid regions for irrigation and domestic consumption. The system works by tapping into groundwater sources—typically aquifers or springs at higher elevations—and conveying water through gently sloping tunnels to lower-lying agricultural lands or settlements. This design allows gravity to move water reliably over considerable distances while minimizing evaporation losses that would occur in open-surface canals.

## Structure and Construction

A typical qanat consists of a series of vertical shafts connected by a gently sloping underground tunnel. The first shaft, located at the source, is the deepest and serves to access the water table. Subsequent shafts are spaced at regular intervals along the tunnel's path, primarily to facilitate excavation, ventilation, and ongoing maintenance. The tunnel itself is constructed with a slight downward gradient, ensuring a steady flow of water without causing erosion or structural collapse.

## Historical and Geographic Context

Originating in ancient Persia (modern-day Iran), qanats spread across the Middle East, North Africa, and parts of Asia and Europe. They represent one of the earliest forms of sustainable water management, allowing civilizations to thrive in environments where surface water was scarce. While many traditional qanats have fallen into disuse due to the advent of modern pumping technology, some remain operational, serving as important cultural heritage sites and examples of passive hydraulic engineering.
