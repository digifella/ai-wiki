# Egg Laying Mammal

Egg-laying mammals, scientifically known as monotremes, represent the most ancient living lineage of mammals still in existence. Only five species survive today: the platypus and four species of echidnas, all native to Australia and nearby regions. These animals diverged from other mammalian groups approximately 160 million years ago during the Jurassic period, long before the evolution of live birth that characterizes all other modern mammals. Their geographic isolation has allowed them to persist with reproductive strategies and physiological characteristics that reflect their early evolutionary origin.

## Reproduction and Biology

Monotremes are distinguished by their unique reproductive approach among mammals: they lay leathery eggs rather than bearing live young. After eggs hatch, mothers nurse their young with milk, despite lacking nipples—instead, milk is secreted through pores in the skin and collected by offspring. This combination of traits, unusual among mammals, reflects their intermediate evolutionary position between reptiles and more derived mammalian groups.

## Evolutionary Significance

The study of monotremes provides crucial insights into early mammalian evolution and the acquisition of mammalian characteristics. Their genome contains features that bridge reptilian and mammalian traits, making them valuable subjects for understanding how key mammalian innovations—such as hair, specialized teeth, and milk production—developed over evolutionary time. Comparative analysis of monotreme biology continues to refine scientific understanding of mammalian diversification and adaptation.
