# Ecological Succession

**Ecological succession** is the process by which the structure of a biological community evolves over time. It involves a predictable sequence of colonization, growth, and replacement of species, leading toward a stable Climax Community or dynamic equilibrium.

## Types of Succession

- **Primary Succession**: Occurs in essentially lifeless areas where the soil is incapable of sustaining life (e.g., [[concepts/lava-flows|lava flows]], newly formed sand dunes, rocks left from a retreating glacier). Pioneer species, such as Lichen and Moss, break down rock to form soil.
- **Secondary Succession**: Occurs in areas where a community that previously existed has been removed; soil remains intact (e.g., after fire, flood, or agricultural abandonment). Recovery is generally faster than primary succession.

## Key Mechanisms

- **Facilitation**: Early species modify the environment in ways that make it more suitable for later species (e.g., [[concepts/nitrogen-fixation|nitrogen fixation]] by legumes).
- **Inhibition**: Early species hinder the establishment of later species (e.g., through allelopathy or resource monopolization).
- **Tolerance**: Later species are able to tolerate low resource levels and outcompete earlier species as resources become scarce.

## Case Study: Gorse-Based Restoration

Traditional [[concepts/preservation|restoration]] often involves the immediate eradication of invasive species. However, alternative approaches utilize invasive plants as nurse crops to accelerate soil recovery and structural complexity.

- **[[concepts/banks-peninsula|Banks Peninsula]] Rewilding: [[entities/hugh-wilson|Hugh Wilson]]'s Gorse-Based [[concepts/ecosystem-restoration|Ecosystem Restoration]]**
	- Hugh Wilson, a botanist in New Zealand, utilized [[concepts/gorse]] (*Ulex europaeus*), typically classified as a noxious weed, to rewild [[concepts/degraded-farmland|degraded farmland]] on [[entities/banks-peninsula|Banks Peninsula]].
	- Instead of immediate removal, Wilson allowed gorse to establish, leveraging its nitrogen-fixing capabilities and dense thorny structure to protect emerging native seedlings from herbivory.
	- This approach challenges conventional [[concepts/invasive-species]] management by treating the invader as a transitional tool in Secondary Succession, facilitating the return of native [[concepts/biodiversity|biodiversity]] through facilitated succession rather than direct intervention.
