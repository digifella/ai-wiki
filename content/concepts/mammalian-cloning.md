# Mammalian Cloning

[[concepts/de-extinction|Mammalian cloning]] is the process of creating a genetically identical copy of an existing mammal. The most widely known method is somatic cell nuclear transfer (SCNT), in which the nucleus of an adult cell is extracted and inserted into an enucleated egg cell. This reconstructed embryo is then stimulated to develop, typically by implantation into a surrogate mother. The resulting offspring is genetically identical to the donor of the original nucleus rather than to the egg provider or surrogate.

## Research and Long-Term Effects

Early mammalian cloning successes, such as the sheep Dolly in 1996, demonstrated the theoretical feasibility of the technique. However, subsequent long-term studies have revealed concerning patterns. A 20-year Japanese study tracking serial [[concepts/cloning|cloning]]—creating clones of clones across multiple generations—found evidence of progressive genetic deterioration. Cloned organisms showed accumulating chromosomal abnormalities and reduced genetic viability with each generation, suggesting that repeated cloning may not be a sustainable reproductive strategy for maintaining healthy populations.

## Current Status

While mammalian cloning remains technically possible, the technique continues to face significant challenges including low [[concepts/success-rates|success rates]], high miscarriage rates, and developmental abnormalities in surviving offspring. Research continues in controlled laboratory settings, primarily for studying cellular biology and genetic mechanisms. The practical application of cloning in [[concepts/agriculture|agriculture]] or conservation remains limited due to these complications and ongoing [[concepts/ethical-considerations|ethical considerations]] surrounding the technique.
