# Solid Core

The Earth's inner core, situated at depths exceeding 5,100 kilometers beneath the outer core, is conventionally modeled as a solid sphere composed primarily of iron and nickel. This structure is maintained under extreme pressure and temperature conditions that theoretically stabilize a metallic lattice. However, recent seismic data has challenged the traditional view of the inner core as a homogeneous solid, indicating the presence of complex structural variations that may imply novel states of matter.

## Seismic Anomalies

Observations of seismic waves propagating through the inner core have revealed discrepancies in velocity and attenuation that do not align with standard isotropic models. These anomalies suggest that the inner core may possess anisotropic properties, where physical characteristics vary depending on the direction of measurement. Such findings have led to hypotheses regarding the alignment of iron crystals or the existence of distinct layers within the core that differ from the expected uniform solid state.

## Implications for Matter States

The identification of these structural complexities has prompted research into potential new phases of matter under planetary core conditions. Theoretical models propose that the extreme environment may support intermediate states between solid and liquid, or distinct crystalline arrangements not previously accounted for in geophysical literature. Continued analysis of seismic tomography data aims to refine these models and determine whether the inner core represents a single coherent solid or a more dynamic, heterogeneous system.
