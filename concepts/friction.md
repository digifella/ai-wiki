# Friction

Friction is a resistive force that opposes the relative motion or tendency of such motion between two surfaces in [[entities/contact|contact]]. [[concepts/acting|Acting]] parallel to the interface, it always operates in a direction opposite to the motion or potential motion between the objects. This force arises from microscopic irregularities on the surfaces and electromagnetic interactions between the atoms at the points of contact. It plays a fundamental role in both natural phenomena and engineered systems, influencing everything from vehicle braking and machinery operation to the ability to walk or move objects across floors.

The phenomenon is generally categorized into static and kinetic friction. Static friction acts on stationary objects, preventing them from starting to move when an external force is applied. It adjusts its magnitude to match the applied force up to a maximum threshold, beyond which the object begins to slide. Once motion commences, kinetic friction takes over, acting to slow down the moving object. Kinetic friction is typically lower than the maximum static friction, which explains why it is often harder to start moving a heavy object than to keep it moving.

The magnitude of frictional force depends on the nature of the surfaces in contact and the normal force pressing them together, rather than the apparent area of contact. This relationship is often described by the coefficient of friction, a dimensionless scalar value that represents the ratio of the frictional force to the normal force. Related concepts such as the [[concepts/angle-of-repose|angle of repose]] illustrate the limits of static friction in [[concepts/granular-materials|granular materials]], where the slope of a pile reaches a [[concepts/critical-point|critical point]] where gravitational forces overcome the frictional resistance holding the particles in place.
