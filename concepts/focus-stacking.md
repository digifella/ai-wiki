# Focus Stacking

[[concepts/blur-correction|Focus stacking]] is a computational photography technique that combines multiple images taken at different focal distances to produce a single photograph with greater overall [[concepts/sharpness|sharpness]] than any individual shot. This method addresses the fundamental optical constraint where increasing depth of field by stopping down the [[concepts/aperture|aperture]] often leads to diffraction, which degrades image quality. By capturing the scene in multiple exposures, photographers can maintain optimal aperture settings for sharpness while ensuring the entire subject remains in focus.

The process typically begins with the photographer taking a series of shots, each focused on a slightly different plane of the subject. These images are then aligned and merged using specialized software that identifies the sharpest areas in each frame. The software creates a composite image by selecting the in-focus pixels from each source image, effectively extending the depth of field beyond the physical limits of the lens.

While originally popular in [[concepts/macro-photography|macro photography]] for its ability to capture fine detail, focus stacking has become a standard practice in [[concepts/landscape-photography|landscape photography]]. It allows for scenes with foreground elements and distant horizons to be rendered with uniform [[concepts/clarity-slider|clarity]]. The technique is particularly valuable in low-[[concepts/light|light]] conditions or when using wide-angle lenses, where achieving sufficient depth of field through aperture adjustment alone would compromise exposure or introduce optical softness.
