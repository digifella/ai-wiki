# Egg Laying Mammal

Egg-laying mammals, scientifically classified as monotremes, represent the most ancient surviving lineage of mammals. Currently, only five extant species exist: the platypus and four species of echidnas. These animals are native exclusively to Australia and nearby regions, including New Guinea. Their evolutionary history diverged from other mammalian groups approximately 160 million years ago during the Jurassic period, predating the evolution of live birth that characterizes all other modern mammalian orders.

Monotremes possess a unique combination of reptilian and mammalian traits. Like reptiles, they lay leathery eggs rather than giving birth to live young. However, they also exhibit key mammalian characteristics, such as the presence of hair and the production of milk to nourish their young. Unlike placental mammals and marsupials, monotremes lack nipples; instead, milk is secreted through pores in the skin, which the young lap up from specialized patches on the mother's abdomen.

Recent discoveries continue to refine our understanding of monotreme biology and evolution. Genetic studies have revealed that monotremes share a common ancestor with therian mammals (placental and marsupial mammals) but retained ancestral features lost in other lineages. The platypus, for instance, has a venomous spur on its hind legs, a trait not found in other living mammals, while echidnas have developed specialized adaptations for digging and foraging. These findings highlight the distinct evolutionary path taken by monotremes since their divergence from the main mammalian tree.
