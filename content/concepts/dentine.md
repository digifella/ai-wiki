# Dentine

Dentine is a calcified tissue that forms the bulk of teeth in vertebrates, lying beneath the harder outer enamel layer. It is composed primarily of mineral crystals embedded in an organic matrix, giving it properties intermediate between bone and enamel. Unlike enamel, dentine can regenerate to a limited degree throughout life and contains microscopic tubules that extend from the outer surface to the inner pulp chamber, allowing for sensory function and nutrient transport.

## Historical significance in shark evolution

Dentine played a crucial role in the evolutionary diversity of [[concepts/shark-teeth|shark teeth]] during the Paleozoic era, often called the [[concepts/golden-age-of-sharks|Golden Age of Sharks]]. Different shark lineages developed specialized dentine structures that supported an array of tooth morphologies—from serrated cutting edges to crushing plates and even elaborate defensive spikes. The composition and arrangement of dentine allowed sharks to rapidly evolve novel feeding [[concepts/adaptations|adaptations]] suited to various ecological niches, contributing to their ecological dominance during this period.

## Modern biology

In modern sharks and other vertebrates, dentine remains essential to tooth function and durability. The material's semi-rigid [[entities/nature|nature]] allows teeth to absorb impact [[concepts/stress|stress]] without shattering, while still maintaining a hard cutting or gripping surface when combined with enamel. This property has made dentine-based teeth remarkably successful across millions of years of vertebrate evolution.
