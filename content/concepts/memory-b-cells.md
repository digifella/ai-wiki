# Memory B Cells

[[concepts/memory|Memory]] B cells are long-lived lymphocytes that develop following [[concepts/exposure|exposure]] to an antigen and remain in circulation or reside in lymphoid tissues for extended periods. These cells are part of the adaptive immune system's capacity to generate faster, stronger [[concepts/responses|responses]] upon re-exposure to previously encountered pathogens. Unlike short-lived plasma cells that produce antibodies immediately after infection, memory B cells persist in a quiescent state, ready for rapid reactivation.

## Formation and Characteristics

Memory B cells arise through selection during the germinal center reaction, where B cells that effectively bind antigen are retained while others are eliminated. These cells typically have undergone somatic hypermutation and class switching, allowing them to produce higher-affinity antibodies and different immunoglobulin types compared to the initial [[concepts/immune-system-function|immune response]]. They can survive for years or even a lifetime, contributing to durable immunity after [[concepts/vaccination|vaccination]] or natural infection.

## Function in Secondary Immune Response

Upon re-encounter with the same antigen, memory B cells differentiate rapidly into plasma cells that secrete antibodies, or into new memory B cells that further amplify the protective response. This secondary response is typically faster and produces antibodies with higher affinity and greater quantity than the primary response. This mechanism underlies the protective effects of vaccination and is responsible for the [[concepts/long-term-immunity|long-term immunity]] that follows recovery from infectious disease.
