# Speed Of Light

The speed of light in a vacuum, denoted by the symbol $c$, is a fundamental physical constant that defines the maximum speed at which all energy, matter, and information in the universe can travel. Its exact value is defined as 299,792,458 meters per second. This constant plays a central role in the theory of special relativity, where it serves as the invariant speed limit for all observers, regardless of their relative motion or the motion of the light source.

## Relativistic Significance

In the framework of special relativity, $c$ is not merely the speed of photons but a fundamental property of spacetime geometry. It links space and time into a single continuum known as spacetime, establishing the relationship between mass and energy through the equation $E=mc^2$. As an object with mass approaches the speed of light, its relativistic mass increases toward infinity, requiring infinite energy to accelerate it further to $c$. Consequently, no massive particle can reach or exceed this speed, preserving causality across the universe.

## Cosmological Horizon

The finite speed of light establishes a causal horizon for the observable universe. Because light takes time to travel, observing distant objects is equivalent to looking back in time. The particle horizon defines the boundary of the observable universe, beyond which light has not had sufficient time to reach us since the Big Bang. This limitation means that the universe we can observe is only a portion of the total cosmos, and events occurring beyond this horizon are currently inaccessible to direct observation.
