## Ethernet Cable Selection: Categories, Network Speed, and Cost-Effectiveness
**URL:** https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=r5GPceyJKt8

### Summary
This video provides a comprehensive guide to choosing the right Ethernet cable for a [[concepts/wired-network-setup|wired network setup]], clarifying the distinctions between various categories ([[concepts/cat-5e|Cat 5e]] to [[concepts/cat-8|Cat 8]]). The main topic revolves around understanding [[concepts/cable-specifications|cable specifications]] relative to internet service provider (ISP) speeds and [[concepts/internal-networking|internal networking]] needs to make an informed purchasing decision. The host emphasizes that selecting a cable beyond one's current internet [[concepts/speed|speed]] or internal network requirements often results in unnecessary cost without any performance benefit.

A critical first step is to determine your internet [[concepts/speed|speed]], which can be done via speedtest.net by testing a wired [[concepts/connection|connection]] directly to your router. Most modern fiber internet plans offer Gigabit Ethernet (1 Gbps or 1000 Mbps), while rural or cheaper tiers might be lower (100-800 Mbps). The "CAT" in cable names stands for "Category," indicating performance standards. Cables older than Cat 5 are largely obsolete. For most home users with 1 Gbps internet, a Cat 5e cable is the most cost-effective and sufficient choice, as it is rated for 1 Gbps data transfer over distances up to 100 meters (328 ft). The video debunks the myth that longer cables inherently mean slower speeds, as long as they are within their rated distance.

For users needing more robust internal network transfers or facing potential electrical interference, [[concepts/cat-6|Cat 6]] cables are a good upgrade. Cat 6 offers shielding (foil or braiding) to reduce noise and crosstalk, and supports speeds up to 10 Gbps over shorter distances (around 50 meters or 165 ft), while still reliably handling 1 Gbps over 100 meters. [[concepts/cat-6a|Cat 6a]] doubles the bandwidth of Cat 6 but is often overkill for typical home use. [[concepts/cat-7|Cat 7]] and 7a cables are generally not recommended for home networks because they use a different connector type (GG45) incompatible with standard RJ45 ports found on most consumer devices, negating any potential speed advantages.

Finally, Cat 8 cables represent the highest current standard, supporting speeds up to 40 Gbps over short distances (30 meters or 150 ft) and always featuring shielding. While expensive and thicker, making them harder to manage, Cat 8 is an excellent option for new home construction aiming for long-term future-proofing, especially as technologies like AI demand increasingly higher data transfer rates. Other factors like solid versus stranded core wires (solid is better for performance but less flexible, stranded is suitable for patch cables) and gold-plated connectors (which prevent corrosion but do not increase speed) are also discussed, with the conclusion that these are mostly relevant for specific environments (e.g., outdoor for [[concepts/major-gold-deposit|gold]] plating) rather than general speed enhancement.

In conclusion, the primary takeaway is that most home users [[entities/will|will]] find a **Cat 5e or Cat 6 cable** perfectly adequate for their current and near-future needs. Cat 5e is the cheapest and widely compatible [[concepts/solution|solution]] for gigabit speeds, while Cat 6 offers added shielding for better performance in electrically noisy environments or for high-speed local file transfers. Investing in higher category cables like Cat 8 is only truly beneficial and cost-effective for new constructions or highly specialized, demanding network environments where future bandwidth needs are paramount.
