# Intraspecific Cannibalism

**Intraspecific Cannibalism** refers to the act of an individual organism consuming all or part of another individual of the same species. While often viewed as pathological, it is a documented survival strategy in various taxa under conditions of [[concepts/limited-resources|resource scarcity]], population [[concepts/density|density]] [[concepts/stress|stress]], or specific [[concepts/evolutionary-pressures|evolutionary pressures]].

## Ecological Mechanisms
- **Population [[concepts/regulation|Regulation]]:** Cannibalism can act as a density-dependent control mechanism, reducing [[concepts/population-growth|population growth]] when resources are limited.
- **[[concepts/metabolic-uptake|Resource Acquisition]]:** In environments with low prey availability, conspecifics become an accessible [[concepts/energy-source|energy source]].
- **Life Stage Dynamics:** Often observed in predator-prey dynamics where larger individuals consume smaller conspecifics (e.g., larvae eating eggs or juveniles).

## Case Study: Australian Cane Toad Control
Recent biological control efforts in Australia have explored leveraging the [[concepts/cane-toad]]'s susceptibility to intraspecific cannibalism to mitigate its invasive spread.

- **Context:** The [[concepts/cane-toad]] (*Rhinella marina*) was introduced to Queensland in 1935 from Hawaii (originally from Puerto Rico) to control [[concepts/sugar|sugar]] cane beetles, leading to a disastrous ecological impact.
- **Control Strategy:** Scientific efforts are currently investigating methods to exploit the toad's unique biology, specifically its tendency toward cannibalism, to reduce invasive populations.
- **Observation:** Reports indicate that cane toad populations are vanishing quickly in certain areas, with intraspecific predation identified as a terrifying but effective natural regulator.
