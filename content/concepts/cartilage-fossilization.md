# Cartilage Fossilization

Cartilage fossilization is the [[concepts/preservation|preservation]] of cartilaginous material in the fossil record, a process that is inherently challenging due to cartilage's soft tissue composition and rapid decomposition after death. Unlike bone, which is mineralized and more resistant to decay, cartilage is composed primarily of collagen and proteoglycans, making it rarely preserved under standard [[concepts/fossilization|fossilization]] conditions. When cartilage does fossilize, it typically occurs in exceptional circumstances involving rapid burial and anoxic (oxygen-poor) environments that inhibit bacterial decomposition.

## Significance for Shark Evolution

The fossilization of cartilage has been particularly important for understanding shark evolutionary history, as sharks possess entirely cartilaginous skeletons rather than bony ones. The fossil record of sharks is therefore dependent on the preservation of these soft structures, along with more commonly fossilized teeth and dermal [[concepts/musical-scales|scales]]. This limitation has shaped our understanding of shark diversity and adaptation throughout geological time, particularly during periods of rapid shark evolution when unusual morphologies—such as distinctive tooth arrangements, body shapes, and defensive structures—emerged and subsequently disappeared from the fossil record.
