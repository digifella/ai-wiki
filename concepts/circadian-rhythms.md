# Circadian Rhythms

Circadian rhythms are endogenous biological cycles that repeat approximately every 24 hours, regulating sleep-wake patterns and numerous other physiological processes. The term is derived from the Latin words *circa* (around) and *dies* (day). These internal rhythms are generated by the body’s biological clock and are synchronized with external environmental cues, most notably the light-dark cycle, through a process known as entrainment.

## The Master Clock and Entrainment

The suprachiasmatic [[concepts/atomic-nuclei|nucleus]] (SCN), a small region within the brain's hypothalamus, functions as the master clock that controls and coordinates these rhythms. Specialized photoreceptor cells in the retina detect [[concepts/light|light]] intensity and transmit signals to the SCN, which then regulates the production of melatonin by the pineal gland. This mechanism ensures that physiological functions align with the external day-night cycle, optimizing alertness during daylight hours and promoting [[concepts/sleep|sleep]] at night.

## Physiological Regulation

Beyond sleep, circadian rhythms influence a wide array of bodily functions, including hormone [[concepts/deployment|release]], body temperature, [[concepts/yeast-fermentation|metabolism]], and immune system activity. Key hormones such as cortisol and growth hormone follow distinct daily patterns, peaking at specific times to support energy levels and tissue repair. Disruptions to these rhythms, often caused by irregular sleep schedules, shift work, or excessive [[concepts/exposure|exposure]] to artificial light at night, can lead to sleep disorders, metabolic issues, and impaired cognitive function.
