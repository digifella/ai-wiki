# Vertical Takeoff And Landing

[[concepts/vertical-takeoff-and-landing-vtol|Vertical takeoff and landing]] (VTOL) refers to an aircraft's ability to ascend, descend, and hover without requiring a runway. This capability distinguishes VTOL aircraft from conventional fixed-wing aircraft, which depend on extended runway infrastructure for operations. VTOL technology has [[concepts/software|applications]] in military operations, emergency response, and civilian transport, particularly in environments where runway construction or maintenance is impractical.

## Design Approaches

Several mechanical designs enable VTOL flight. Rotorcraft such as helicopters use rotating blades to generate lift and control directional [[concepts/exercise|movement]]. [[concepts/tiltrotor-technology|Tiltrotor]] aircraft, exemplified by the V-22 Osprey, combine helicopter and fixed-wing capabilities by rotating their engines between vertical and horizontal orientations. This dual-mode approach allows tiltrotors to hover like helicopters while achieving the speed and efficiency of conventional aircraft during forward flight. Other VTOL concepts include vectored [[concepts/engine-thrust|thrust]] designs, which redirect engine exhaust for vertical lift, and multirotor systems that employ multiple independent propellers.

## Operational Advantages and Constraints

VTOL aircraft reduce dependency on airfield infrastructure and enable operations in austere locations, ships, and urban environments. However, VTOL designs typically involve mechanical complexity, higher maintenance demands, and reduced payload capacity compared to equivalent runway-dependent aircraft. Fuel efficiency generally decreases in vertical flight modes, and noise generation can be significant, particularly for rotorcraft operations in populated areas.
