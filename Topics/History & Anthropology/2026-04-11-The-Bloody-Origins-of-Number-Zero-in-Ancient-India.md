## The Bloody Origins of Number Zero in Ancient India
**Clip title:** The Bloody Origins of the [[concepts/number-zero|Number Zero]]
**Author / channel:** Nightshift – Kurzgesagt After Dark
**URL:** https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=RSIsGomGZcc

### Summary
The video provides a comprehensive historical overview of ancient India's influence on [[concepts/global-trade|global trade]], culture, and [science](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Science), beginning with [[concepts/emperor-ashokas-reign|Emperor Ashoka's reign]] in the [[concepts/mauryan-empire|Mauryan Empire]]. Following the brutal conquest of Kalinga in 261 BCE, which resulted in immense suffering and devastation, Ashoka experienced profound remorse. This led to his conversion to Buddhism and a radical shift in his imperial policy, emphasizing non-violence, [[concepts/religious-tolerance|religious tolerance]], and social welfare through carved edicts on pillars and public works such as improved roads and wells.

This transformative period saw the rise of Buddhist monasteries which, supported by Ashoka's patronage and the burgeoning [trade routes](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Trade_routes), evolved into significant economic and spiritual centers. These monasteries acted as early banks, managing land and mills, lending money, and serving as safe havens for merchants traveling along trade routes connecting inland markets to ports bound for Southeast Asia. This symbiotic relationship, where faith funded trade and trade carried faith, allowed for the gradual spread of Buddhist [[concepts/philosophy|philosophy]] and Indian goods across the region, even after the Mauryan Empire's eventual decline.

India further solidified its position as a global economic powerhouse during the "Golden Age of Trade" with the Roman Empire. Roman control over Red Sea ports established a direct maritime corridor to the Indian world, greatly increasing ship traffic. Leveraging predictable monsoon winds, Indian sailors transported vast quantities of valuable commodities like ebony, sandalwood, cotton, and pepper to Rome. Pepper, in particular, became so popular it reshaped Roman cuisine and accounted for a significant portion of the Roman Empire's budget, cementing India's role as Rome's largest trading partner for over three centuries.

Beyond material wealth, India became a "Ground Zero" for intellectual and [[concepts/cultural-exchange|cultural exchange]] under the Gupta Empire. Centres of learning like Nalanda flourished, fostering advancements in [[concepts/art|art]] and science. A pivotal contribution was the invention and [[concepts/slms|definition]] of zero and the [decimal positional numeral system](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Decimal_positional_numeral_system) by Brahmagupta around 628 CE, which revolutionized [[concepts/mathematics|mathematics]] by allowing any number to be expressed with just ten symbols. This Indian knowledge, along with Buddhist traditions, traveled both eastward to [[entities/china|China]] and Southeast Asia (influencing architecture like Borobudur and Angkor Wat) and westward to the Arab world, where Arab scholars at Baghdad's House of Wisdom translated and disseminated these texts.

The journey of Indian numbers culminated in their [[concepts/adoption|adoption]] in [[entities/europe|Europe]], fundamentally transforming European commerce and laying the groundwork for modern science. Mathematicians like Fibonacci, encouraged by figures like Frederick II, introduced these efficient numerals to replace cumbersome Roman systems, enabling more complex calculations essential for banking, trade, and ultimately, global exploration. Despite the subsequent decline and colonization of its homeland, India's most profound and enduring legacy remains this [[concepts/universal-language|universal language]] of mathematics, which continues to be an indispensable tool for understanding and shaping our reality.
