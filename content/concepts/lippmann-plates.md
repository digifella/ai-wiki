# Lippmann Plates

Lippmann plates are photographic plates used in [[concepts/lippmann-photography|Lippmann photography]], a color photography technique developed by Gabriel Lippmann in the 1890s. The technique works by recording the interference pattern created when [[concepts/light|light]] reflects off a mirror placed behind the photographic emulsion. This interference pattern, rather than a dye or pigment, encodes the color information in the image.

When light strikes a Lippmann plate during [[concepts/exposure|exposure]], it creates standing waves within the emulsion layer. These waves form a three-dimensional interference pattern that selectively reflects specific wavelengths of light when the plate is later illuminated. The reflected light reproduces the original colors of the subject with remarkable [[concepts/accuracy|accuracy]], making Lippmann photography one of the earliest methods of color photography.

The colors produced by Lippmann plates are examples of [[concepts/structural-color|structural color]]—color that arises from the physical [[concepts/structure|structure]] of a material rather than from pigments or dyes. This distinction is significant because it demonstrates that color perception depends not only on chemical composition but also on how light interacts with microscopic physical structures. Despite the technical elegance of the method, Lippmann photography did not become commercially viable due to the long exposure times required and the difficulty of viewing the resulting plates, which could only be seen under specific lighting conditions.
