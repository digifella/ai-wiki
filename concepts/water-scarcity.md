# Water Scarcity

Water scarcity occurs when the available supply of fresh water is insufficient to meet the demands of human populations and natural ecosystems. Although Earth contains approximately 1.386 billion cubic kilometers of water, about 97% exists as saltwater in oceans. Of the remaining freshwater, roughly 69% is frozen in ice sheets and glaciers, while much of the rest is stored in groundwater aquifers. This leaves less than 1% of Earth's total water readily accessible as surface freshwater in lakes, rivers, and precipitation.

## Drivers and Global Distribution

Water scarcity is driven by a combination of physical limitations and human activity. Physical water scarcity arises when natural resources are depleted faster than they can be replenished, often due to arid climates or over-extraction of aquifers. Physical water scarcity is prevalent in regions such as North Africa, the Middle East, and parts of Central Asia. Economic water scarcity, conversely, occurs where water resources are physically present but inaccessible due to a lack of infrastructure, financial resources, or institutional capacity. This form of scarcity affects billions of people in sub-Saharan Africa and parts of South Asia.

Population growth, agricultural expansion, and industrial development significantly exacerbate the demand for fresh water. Agriculture accounts for approximately 70% of global freshwater withdrawals, making it the primary driver of scarcity in many regions. Climate change further intensifies these pressures by altering precipitation patterns, increasing the frequency of droughts, and accelerating the melting of glaciers that serve as long-term freshwater reservoirs. Consequently, water stress is increasingly becoming a critical constraint on economic development and food security worldwide.

## Mitigation and Adaptation

Addressing water scarcity requires a multifaceted approach involving conservation, infrastructure development, and technological innovation. Water conservation strategies include improving irrigation efficiency, reducing industrial waste, and implementing pricing mechanisms that reflect the true cost of water resources. Infrastructure projects, such as dams and reservoirs, help store water during periods of abundance for use during dry seasons, though they can have significant ecological impacts.

Technological solutions play an increasingly vital role in expanding the available water supply. Desalination, the process of removing salt from seawater, has become a critical source of fresh water for arid coastal nations, particularly in the Middle East. However, desalination is energy-intensive and produces brine waste that must be managed carefully to avoid marine ecosystem damage. Other emerging technologies include wastewater recycling and advanced groundwater management techniques, which aim to close the loop on water usage and ensure sustainable availability for future generations.
