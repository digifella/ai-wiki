# Stellar Mass

Stellar mass refers to the total mass of a star, typically expressed in solar masses (M☉), where one solar mass equals approximately 2 × 10³⁰ kilograms. Stars exhibit a wide range of masses, from approximately 0.08 solar masses for the smallest red dwarfs to over 100 solar masses for the most massive stars observed. Stellar mass is a fundamental parameter that directly determines a star's luminosity, core temperature, [[concepts/fusion-power|nuclear fusion]] rate, lifespan, and evolutionary trajectory.

## Mass-Luminosity Relationship

The relationship between stellar mass and luminosity is one of the most important empirical laws in [[concepts/astrophysics|astrophysics]]. More massive stars burn their fuel at significantly higher rates and thus shine much brighter than lower-mass stars. This relationship follows approximately an exponential power law, such that luminosity [[concepts/musical-scales|scales]] roughly with the cube to fourth power of mass. Consequently, massive stars exhaust their fuel rapidly and have much shorter lifespans—sometimes only millions of years—whereas low-mass stars can persist for tens of billions of years.

## Detection and Measurement

Stellar masses are determined through various observational methods, including analysis of [[concepts/binary-stars|binary star systems]] where gravitational interactions reveal mass through orbital dynamics, spectroscopic observations of stellar properties, and more recently through [[concepts/gravitational-waves|gravitational wave detection]]. The latter method has proven particularly valuable for detecting [[concepts/compact-stellar-objects|compact remnants]] and sub-solar mass objects such as [[concepts/primordial-black-hole|primordial black holes]], which would be extremely difficult to observe through conventional astronomical techniques. These gravitational wave detections have opened new avenues for understanding the mass distribution of stellar-mass objects across the universe.
