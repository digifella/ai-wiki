# Wimps

WIMPs (Weakly Interacting Massive Particles) are hypothetical particles proposed as leading candidates for dark matter, the invisible substance estimated to comprise approximately 85% of the matter in the universe. Unlike ordinary baryonic matter, WIMPs interact primarily through gravity and the weak nuclear force, making them extraordinarily difficult to detect through conventional means. Their name derives from their defining properties: they possess significant mass while interacting only weakly with normal matter.

Direct detection experiments attempt to observe WIMPs by measuring the recoil of atomic nuclei when struck by a passing dark matter particle. These underground detectors, such as those using liquid xenon or germanium crystals, are shielded from cosmic rays to minimize background noise. The goal is to identify the specific energy signatures and interaction rates predicted by supersymmetry and other theoretical models that extend the Standard Model of particle physics.

Indirect detection methods focus on the products of WIMP annihilation or decay. If WIMPs are their own antiparticles, they may annihilate upon collision, producing gamma rays, neutrinos, or antimatter particles. Astronomers search for excess gamma-ray emissions from dense dark matter regions, such as the Galactic Center or dwarf spheroidal galaxies, to find evidence of these high-energy signals. Current observations from telescopes like Fermi-LAT have placed stringent limits on the WIMP annihilation cross-section, narrowing the viable parameter space for their mass and interaction strength.
