# Coal Formation

Coal is a combustible sedimentary rock composed primarily of carbon, along with variable quantities of hydrogen, sulfur, oxygen, and nitrogen. It originates from the accumulation of plant debris in ancient swampy environments, where anaerobic conditions prevented the complete decomposition of organic matter. This preservation of biomass is the foundational step in the creation of fossil fuels, allowing vast quantities of carbon to be stored within the Earth's crust over geological timescales.

The transformation of this organic material into coal occurs through a process known as coalification, which involves increasing heat and pressure over millions of years. This metamorphic process drives off volatile components and concentrates the carbon content, progressing through stages from peat to lignite, bituminous coal, and finally anthracite. The specific rank of the coal depends on the degree of metamorphism experienced by the original plant matter.

Geological evidence suggests that significant coal and oil deposits were formed during periods of high atmospheric oxygen. This era is linked to the oxidation of rocks, particularly in regions such as the red centre of Australia. The oxidation processes facilitated the preservation of organic carbon by preventing its immediate re-oxidation and return to the atmosphere, thereby enabling the large-scale accumulation of fossil fuel reserves observed in the geological record.
