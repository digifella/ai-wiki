# Shark Teeth

Shark teeth are specialized dental structures that differ fundamentally from mammalian dentition in both attachment and replacement mechanisms. Unlike teeth anchored in bone sockets, shark teeth attach to the jaw via connective tissue fibers, facilitating a continuous cycle of shedding and replacement throughout the animal's lifetime. This modular design has supported successful adaptation across hundreds of millions of years, allowing sharks to rapidly evolve diverse feeding strategies.

The morphological diversity of shark teeth reflects their evolutionary history, particularly during the Mesozoic Era often referred to as the Golden Age of Shark Evolution. During this period, various lineages developed unique adaptations to exploit specific ecological niches. These included specialized forms such as serrated blades for slicing flesh, flattened plates for crushing hard-shelled prey, and needle-like structures for gripping slippery fish.

Some extinct shark groups exhibited even more extreme dental modifications. Fossil evidence indicates the presence of teeth with buzzsaw-like serrations, anvil-shaped crushing surfaces, and spike-like projections. These bizarre adaptations demonstrate the plasticity of shark dentition, which has allowed the group to maintain ecological dominance despite significant environmental changes and competition from other marine predators.
