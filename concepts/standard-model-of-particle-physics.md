# Standard Model Of Particle Physics

The [[concepts/standard-model|Standard Model]] is the [[concepts/theory|theoretical framework]] describing the [[concepts/elementary-particles|fundamental particles]] and forces of nature, excluding [[concepts/gravitational-pull|gravity]]. Developed through decades of experimental and theoretical work beginning in the mid-20th century, it represents our best current understanding of [[concepts/granular-physics|particle physics]] at the smallest [[concepts/musical-scales|scales]]. The model successfully explains the behavior of [[concepts/point-like-particles|elementary particles]] and their interactions across three of the four known fundamental forces.

## Fundamental Components

The Standard Model organizes matter into two categories of particles: quarks and leptons. Quarks combine to form composite particles such as [[concepts/protons|protons]] and neutrons, while leptons include electrons and neutrinos. These matter particles interact via gauge bosons, which mediate the electromagnetic, weak, and strong nuclear forces. The [[entities/higgs-boson|Higgs boson]], discovered in 2012, is responsible for giving mass to other elementary particles through the Higgs mechanism.

## Experimental Verification and Anomalies

[[concepts/accuracy|Precision]] tests of the Standard Model rely on high-energy particle accelerators and sensitive detectors to measure particle properties with extreme accuracy. One notable area of investigation is the magnetic moment of the muon, a heavier cousin of the electron. The [[concepts/muon-g-2-experiment|Muon g-2 experiment]] at Fermilab measures this property to test the [[concepts/logical-consistency|consistency]] of the Standard Model predictions. Discrepancies between theoretical calculations and experimental results in such measurements may indicate the presence of new physics beyond the current framework.

## Future Directions

Upcoming results from the Muon g-2 experiment in 2025 are expected to provide further constraints on the Standard Model. Continued refinement of theoretical calculations and experimental techniques aims to resolve existing tensions between [[concepts/user-attention-prediction|prediction]] and observation. These efforts are crucial for determining whether the Standard Model is a complete description of nature or if it requires extension to account for phenomena such as [[concepts/dark-matter|dark matter]] and neutrino mass.
