# Ice Nucleation

Ice nucleation is the process by which water vapor in the atmosphere condenses and freezes into ice crystals. This process is fundamental to cloud formation and precipitation. Rather than freezing spontaneously, water droplets typically require a surface or particle to initiate [[concepts/crystal-formation|crystallization]]—a surface is called an ice nucleus or nucleant. Without such particles, water can remain liquid even [[concepts/assistive-technology|at]] temperatures well below 0°C, a state known as supercooling.

## Biological Ice Nucleators

Certain microorganisms, particularly fungi, produce proteins capable of serving as ice nucleation sites. These ice-nucleating proteins (INPs) have a molecular [[concepts/structure|structure]] that facilitates the formation of ice crystals at relatively warm temperatures, compared to inorganic particles. Fungal INPs are thought to play a significant role in atmospheric ice nucleation, influencing cloud microphysics and [[concepts/precipitation-patterns|precipitation patterns]]. The presence of such biological nucleators in the atmosphere suggests that life processes contribute directly to weather and hydrological cycles.

## Atmospheric Significance

Ice nucleation by fungal proteins affects how clouds form and evolve, with implications for rainfall patterns and cloud properties. The abundance and [[concepts/distribution|distribution]] of biological ice nucleators in the atmosphere remain active areas of research, as does their role relative to other nucleating particles such as mineral dust and sea salt aerosols. Understanding these mechanisms is relevant to cloud [[concepts/physics|physics]], atmospheric chemistry, and potentially to predictions of weather and climate processes.
