# Interlocking Mechanisms

Interlocking mechanisms are the fundamental operating principle of zippers, enabling two parallel rows of separated teeth or coils to engage and disengage in a controlled, reversible manner. As a slider moves along the fastener, it precisely guides elements from opposite sides into contact, creating a mechanical interlock that holds two fabric panels together. The effectiveness of this system depends entirely on the geometry of the interlocking elements—their shape, spacing, and angular alignment—which must be manufactured to extremely tight tolerances to function reliably across thousands of open-close cycles.

## Design and Function

The slider performs the critical task of separating and aligning teeth or coils as it travels. When moving downward, the slider's internal geometry compresses and guides upper and lower elements toward each other, causing them to interlock. When moving upward, the reverse occurs: the slider creates sufficient separation for the interlocking elements to disengage without jamming. This reversibility distinguishes zippers from many other fastening systems and requires precision engineering of the slider's internal surfaces, particularly the upper and lower converging channels that control element movement.

## Historical Development

The modern interlocking zipper was developed through iterative improvements to earlier fastener designs. YKK, founded in 1934, became the dominant manufacturer by systematizing zipper production and establishing consistent quality standards. The company's innovations in metallurgy, molding, and assembly processes made reliable interlocking mechanisms available at scale. Different zipper types—coil, metal, and plastic—employ variations of the basic interlocking principle, each suited to different applications and load requirements.
