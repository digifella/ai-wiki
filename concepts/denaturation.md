# Denaturation

Denaturation is the process by which proteins lose their native three-dimensional structure, resulting in the disruption of their biological function. Proteins rely on precise folding to maintain their shape, which is essential for their roles as enzymes, structural components, or regulatory molecules. When denaturation occurs, the delicate arrangement of amino acid chains unravels, altering the protein's conformation and preventing it from interacting with other molecules as intended.

In the context of food and nutrition, denaturation is frequently triggered by heat, pH changes, chemicals, or mechanical stress. Heat causes the hydrogen bonds and other non-covalent interactions holding the protein together to break, leading to unfolding. Acidic environments, such as those found in citrus juices or vinegar, can alter the charge of amino acid side chains, disrupting ionic bonds and causing the protein to precipitate or coagulate.

This structural change often leads to visible physical transformations in food. For example, the clear albumin in egg whites turns opaque and solidifies when heated, while milk curdles when acid is added. While denaturation typically renders the original biological function of the protein inactive, it can also improve digestibility by exposing peptide bonds to digestive enzymes, making nutrients more accessible to the body.
