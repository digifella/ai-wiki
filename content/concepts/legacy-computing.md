# Legacy Computing

[[concepts/historical-computing|Legacy computing]] refers to the continued use, study, and operation of computing systems from earlier decades, often alongside or instead of contemporary [[concepts/hardware|hardware]]. These systems—ranging from mainframes to microcomputers—remain functional and sometimes actively maintained for specific [[concepts/software|applications]], historical [[concepts/preservation|preservation]], or educational purposes. The term encompasses both the physical hardware and the software ecosystems that supported these machines.

## Historical Context

[[concepts/vintage-computing|Legacy systems]] became particularly prevalent following the rapid advancement of computing technology in the late 20th century. Hardware from the 1960s through 1980s, such as the PDP-11 series, remains notable for its durability and the substantial software investments made in these platforms. Many organizations continued [[concepts/running|running]] legacy systems well into subsequent decades due to the high costs and complexity of migration, creating a de facto category of "legacy" hardware that outlasted its original commercial lifecycle.

## Contemporary Interest

Modern interest in [[concepts/retro-computing|legacy computing]] encompasses multiple motivations. Hobbyists and enthusiasts maintain and restore vintage hardware for historical documentation and nostalgia. Researchers have explored the theoretical and practical limits of legacy systems, including demonstrations of running computationally intensive modern algorithms—such as [[concepts/transformer-architectures|transformer neural networks]]—on machines with severe [[concepts/memory|memory]] and processing constraints. These experiments reveal both the engineering ingenuity of earlier designs and the relative efficiency requirements of contemporary software.
