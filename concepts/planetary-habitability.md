# Planetary Habitability

Planetary habitability is the measure of a celestial body's capacity to sustain life. Scientists evaluate this potential by assessing physical and chemical conditions necessary for life as understood on Earth. Key criteria include the presence of liquid water, an appropriate atmospheric composition, suitable temperature ranges, and adequate protection from harmful radiation. While Earth remains the only confirmed example of a habitable world, these established criteria are applied to evaluate other planets and exoplanets for their potential to support biological activity.

The concept extends beyond simple environmental metrics to include geological and magnetic factors that maintain long-term stability. A planet must retain its atmosphere against stellar winds, often requiring a magnetic field, and possess a carbon cycle to regulate climate over geological timescales. These factors determine whether a world can remain habitable for sufficient time for life to emerge and evolve, rather than merely being temporarily suitable.

Research into planetary habitability frequently focuses on bodies within our solar system, particularly Mars. Evidence from Mars research, such as ancient riverbeds and mineral deposits formed in water, suggests that the planet was once more hospitable. Current missions investigate subsurface ice and organic molecules to determine if past or present microbial life could have existed. This local study informs the search for biosignatures on exoplanets, helping astronomers prioritize targets for future observation.
