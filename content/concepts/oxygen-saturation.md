# Oxygen Saturation

During the [[concepts/geologic-epochs|Carboniferous and Permian periods]] (approximately 360 to 250 million years ago), atmospheric oxygen levels experienced significant fluctuations as vast quantities of organic material accumulated and eventually formed fossil fuel deposits. The Carboniferous period, in particular, witnessed some of the highest oxygen concentrations in Earth's history, with atmospheric oxygen levels reaching approximately 35% compared to today's 21%. This elevated oxygen availability was driven by extensive plant growth in tropical swamps and forests, which through photosynthesis removed carbon dioxide from the atmosphere and produced oxygen [[concepts/assistive-technology|at]] unprecedented rates.

As these organic-rich ecosystems accumulated in swampy environments—particularly in the formation of coal deposits—they effectively sequestered carbon that would otherwise have remained in the atmosphere as carbon dioxide. This process temporarily lowered atmospheric CO₂ levels, contributing to cooler global climates during portions of this era. However, as the Permian period progressed, oxygen levels gradually declined from their Carboniferous peaks, while CO₂ levels began to rise again as the conditions favoring massive plant accumulation shifted and decomposition of organic matter increased.

## Modern Significance

The formation of fossil fuels during these periods represented a major planetary carbon cycle disruption, locking away atmospheric carbon for hundreds of millions of years. The subsequent burning of these fossil fuels in the modern [[concepts/industrial-revolution|industrial era]] has reversed this process, returning sequestered carbon to the atmosphere as CO₂ while consuming atmospheric oxygen, though at rates too gradual to significantly impact current oxygen saturation levels.
