# Antimatter

Antimatter consists of particles that are identical to [[concepts/baryonic-matter|ordinary matter]] particles in mass but opposite in electric charge and other quantum properties. For every type of matter particle, a corresponding antiparticle exists: electrons have positrons, [[concepts/protons|protons]] have antiprotons, and neutrons have antineutrons. When a particle and its antiparticle meet, they annihilate each other in a reaction that converts their combined mass entirely into energy, as described by Einstein's mass-energy equivalence E=mc².

## Production and Detection

Antimatter is produced naturally in small quantities through cosmic ray interactions in Earth's atmosphere and in high-energy environments throughout the universe. Scientists also create antimatter in particle accelerators, most notably at facilities like CERN's [[entities/lhc|Large Hadron Collider]], where particles are smashed together at extreme energies. Detection of antimatter relies on identifying the characteristic radiation and energy signatures produced when antiparticles interact with ordinary matter.

## Cosmological Significance

The origin and fate of antimatter remains one of [[concepts/cosmology|cosmology]]'s fundamental puzzles. Current theories predict that the [[concepts/big-bang|Big Bang]] should have created equal amounts of matter and antimatter, which would have annihilated each other completely, leaving only energy. Yet our universe is dominated by ordinary matter, suggesting that some asymmetry or process preferentially preserved matter over antimatter in the [[concepts/early-universe|early universe]]. Understanding this matter-antimatter imbalance is crucial to explaining why the universe exists in its current form.
