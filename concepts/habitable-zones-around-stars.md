# Habitable Zones Around Stars

A habitable zone, often referred to as the "Goldilocks zone," is the orbital region around a star where surface temperatures on a planet allow [[concepts/liquid-water|liquid water]] to exist. This zone is defined by the balance between stellar radiation and planetary cooling, ensuring that water remains in a liquid state rather than freezing into ice or evaporating into vapor. The presence of liquid water is considered a critical prerequisite for life as it is known, making these zones primary targets in the search for [[concepts/extraterrestrial-life|extraterrestrial life]].

The position and width of the habitable zone are determined primarily by the luminosity and spectral type of the host star. More luminous [[concepts/stellar-objects|stars]], such as F-type or A-type stars, have habitable zones located farther from the stellar surface, while dimmer stars, like M-type red dwarfs, have zones much closer to the star. Consequently, planets orbiting red dwarfs must be tidally locked or have specific atmospheric conditions to maintain habitable temperatures, whereas planets around brighter stars may require wider orbits to avoid runaway greenhouse effects.

The concept is dynamic and depends on additional factors beyond simple distance, including a planet's atmospheric [[concepts/writing|composition]], albedo, and internal heat sources. A thick [[concepts/earths-atmosphere|atmosphere]] rich in [[concepts/greenhouse-gases|greenhouse gases]] can extend the outer boundary of the habitable zone by trapping heat, allowing liquid water to exist further from the star than radiative equilibrium calculations alone would suggest. Conversely, high albedo from ice or cloud cover can reflect sufficient radiation to shrink the habitable zone inward. Therefore, the habitable zone represents a theoretical range rather than a guarantee of [[concepts/habitability|habitability]] for any specific planet within it.
