# Biological Immortality

Biological immortality refers to the theoretical absence of senescence—the biological process of aging—in living organisms. An organism possessing biological immortality would not experience age-related decline and could theoretically live indefinitely, though it would remain vulnerable to death from injury, disease, predation, or environmental factors. This contrasts with the popular concept of immortality, which typically implies invulnerability to all forms of death.

## Natural Examples

Several organisms demonstrate negligible senescence in natural conditions. Certain species of jellyfish, particularly *Turritopsis dohrnii*, can revert to an earlier life stage in a process called transdifferentiation. Some tortoises show minimal signs of aging throughout their extended lifespans. Certain bacteria and single-celled organisms that reproduce through fission rather than sexual reproduction also exhibit negligible senescence, as each division produces genetically identical offspring without the accumulated cellular damage characteristic of aging in multicellular organisms.

## Research Challenges

Scientific investigation into biological immortality has pursued multiple avenues, including genetic analysis, cellular mechanisms, and reproductive biology. However, research has encountered significant obstacles. A 20-year Japanese study on mammalian serial [[concepts/cloning|cloning]] concluded that repeated cloning leads to a genetic dead end, suggesting that replicating biological immortality through cloning is not viable. The complexity of aging—involving genetic, cellular, and systemic factors—means that achieving biological immortality in complex organisms remains theoretical rather than practically achievable.
