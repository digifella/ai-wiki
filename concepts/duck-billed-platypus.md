# Duck Billed Platypus

The duck-billed platypus is a monotreme—one of only five species of egg-laying mammals—native to eastern Australia and Tasmania. It is distinguished by its distinctive leathery bill resembling a duck's bill, venomous spurs on males, and dense waterproof fur. The platypus is primarily nocturnal and semi-aquatic, spending much of its time in freshwater rivers and streams where it hunts for invertebrates using electroreception sensors embedded in its bill. These specialized sensory organs detect electrical fields generated by muscle contractions in prey, allowing the platypus to locate food in murky water where vision is of limited use.

## Biology and Reproduction

The platypus possesses an unusually low body temperature for a mammal, ranging from 32 to 34 degrees Celsius. Rather than bearing live young, females lay between one and three small leathery eggs in burrows and incubate them for approximately ten days. After hatching, mothers nurse their young with milk secreted through pores in the skin, as they lack nipples. Males produce a venomous secretion from glands in their hind legs during breeding season, likely used in competition with other males.

## Evolutionary Significance

The platypus represents a unique evolutionary lineage that diverged from other mammals over 160 million years ago. Its combination of reptilian features—such as egg-laying and a single reproductive opening called a cloaca—alongside mammalian characteristics like fur and milk production makes it a valuable subject for understanding early mammalian evolution. Genetic studies reveal that the platypus genome contains genes more similar to birds and reptiles than to placental mammals, providing insights into the ancestral traits of the mammalian class.
