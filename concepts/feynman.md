# Feynman

Richard Feynman (1918–1988) was an American theoretical physicist whose work fundamentally advanced twentieth-century physics, particularly in the fields of quantum mechanics and quantum electrodynamics (QED). Born in Far Rockaway, New York, he earned his doctorate from Princeton University in 1942 under the supervision of John Archibald Wheeler. During World War II, Feynman was recruited to the Manhattan Project at Los Alamos, where he contributed to the development of the atomic bomb and later became known for his work on safety protocols and the investigation of technical failures.

## Quantum Electrodynamics and Path Integrals

Feynman’s most significant scientific contribution was the formulation of quantum electrodynamics, a theory describing how light and matter interact. He developed the path integral formulation of quantum mechanics, which provides a framework for calculating the probability amplitude of a system by summing over all possible paths. This approach, along with the development of Feynman diagrams, revolutionized particle physics by offering a visual and mathematical tool to simplify complex calculations involving subatomic particles. His work on QED earned him the Nobel Prize in Physics in 1965, which he shared with Julian Schwinger and Sin-Itiro Tomonaga.

## Public Engagement and Legacy

Beyond his academic achievements, Feynman was renowned for his ability to communicate complex scientific concepts to the general public. He authored several bestselling books, including *The Feynman Lectures on Physics* and *Surely You're Joking, Mr. Feynman!*, which highlighted his distinctive teaching style and personal anecdotes. His investigation into the Space Shuttle Challenger disaster in 1986, where he famously demonstrated the failure of O-ring seals using a glass of ice water, further cemented his public profile as a rigorous and accessible scientific thinker.
