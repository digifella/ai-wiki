# Candidate Screening Methods

Candidate screening methods are systematic approaches used by organizations to evaluate and filter job applicants during the recruitment process. These techniques serve to reduce large applicant pools to a manageable number of qualified candidates for further assessment. Screening typically occurs early in the recruitment funnel and relies on standardized criteria aligned with job requirements, organizational needs, and role specifications. Effective screening improves hiring efficiency by eliminating unsuitable candidates before more time-intensive assessment stages.

## Primary Screening Techniques

Resume and application review remains the most common screening method, where recruiters assess candidate qualifications, experience, and fit against stated job requirements. Many organizations use applicant tracking systems (ATS) to automate initial filtering based on [[concepts/keywords|keywords]], education level, years of experience, and required certifications. Phone and video screening calls represent a secondary step, allowing recruiters to verify information, assess communication [[concepts/skills|skills]], and gauge basic cultural fit before advancing candidates to formal interviews. Some organizations also employ assessment tools such as skills tests, cognitive ability tests, or personality inventories to objectively evaluate candidate [[concepts/capabilities|capabilities]].

## Considerations and Challenges

Screening methods must balance [[concepts/speed|speed]] and [[concepts/accuracy|accuracy]] to avoid both false negatives (rejecting qualified candidates) and false positives (advancing unsuitable candidates). Bias can emerge in resume review and assessment processes, potentially disadvantaging candidates from non-traditional backgrounds or those with employment gaps. Organizations increasingly recognize that standardized, transparent screening criteria and multiple evaluation touchpoints improve both hiring outcomes and candidate experience. The choice of screening methods often depends on role level, industry norms, organizational resources, and candidate volume.
