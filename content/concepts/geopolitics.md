# Geopolitics

Geopolitics is the study of how geography, natural resources, and territorial control shape international relations and state behavior. It examines the strategic competition between nations for [[concepts/power|influence]], resources, and [[concepts/security|security]], with consideration for physical location, climate, demography, and access to critical materials. Historical geopolitical competition has driven conflicts, alliances, and the formation of political blocs, while contemporary geopolitics increasingly involves non-state actors, economic interdependence, and transnational challenges.

## Geographic and Resource Dimensions

Physical geography constrains and enables state power. Control over waterways, straits, and land borders affects trade and military positioning. Access to natural resources—particularly energy, minerals, and agricultural land—influences national strategy and foreign policy. [[concepts/limited-resources|Scarcity]] of critical resources like rare earth elements, lithium, and oil creates competitive pressures and shapes alliance-building. [[concepts/climate-change|Climate change]] introduces new geopolitical variables by altering resource availability, [[concepts/habitability|habitability]], and migration patterns.

## Supply Chain Implications

Modern economies depend on complex global [[concepts/supply-chains|supply chains]] that span multiple nations and regions. Geopolitical tensions, trade restrictions, and territorial disputes can disrupt the [[concepts/flow|flow]] of materials and manufactured goods. Strategic chokepoints—such as the Suez Canal or Taiwan Strait—create [[concepts/vulnerability|vulnerability]] to disruption. Recent years have seen increased [[concepts/attention-mechanisms|attention]] to supply chain [[concepts/resilience|resilience]], with some states and corporations seeking to reduce dependence on geopolitically unstable or adversarial regions through diversification, nearshoring, or [[concepts/domestic-manufacturing|domestic production]].
