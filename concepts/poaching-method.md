# Poaching Method

Poaching is a moist-heat cooking technique in which food is gently simmered in liquid at temperatures below boiling, typically between 160–180°F (70–82°C). The surrounding liquid, known as the poaching medium, conducts heat gradually to cook the food evenly while maintaining its delicate structure. This method is particularly valued for producing tender results while preserving the natural flavour and texture of the ingredient, making it suitable for proteins and fruits that can become tough or dry with more aggressive cooking methods.

## Poaching Media

The choice of poaching liquid significantly influences the final taste of the dish. Common media include water, stock, wine, milk, or fruit juices. For savory applications, aromatics such as herbs, spices, and vegetables are often added to the liquid to infuse the food with additional flavour. In the case of Normandy-style poached salmon, the fish is simmered in cider, which imparts a distinct acidity and fruitiness that complements the richness of the subsequent cream sauce.

## Applications and Techniques

Poaching is widely used for delicate proteins such as fish, poultry, and eggs, as well as for fruits like pears and peaches. The key to successful poaching is maintaining a consistent temperature; the liquid should show only slight movement, with small bubbles occasionally rising to the surface, rather than a rolling boil. This gentle heat prevents the proteins from contracting too rapidly, which would otherwise squeeze out moisture and result in a dry texture. After cooking, the food is often removed from the liquid and served with a sauce made from the reduced poaching medium to maximize flavour retention.
