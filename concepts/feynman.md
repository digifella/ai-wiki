# Feynman

[[entities/richard-feynman|Richard Feynman]] (1918–1988) was an American theoretical physicist whose work fundamentally advanced twentieth-century [[concepts/physics|physics]], particularly in the fields of [[concepts/quantum-mechanics|quantum mechanics]] and quantum electrodynamics (QED). Born in Far Rockaway, New York, he earned his [[entities/phd|doctorate]] from Princeton University in 1942 under the supervision of [[entities/chef-john|John]] Archibald Wheeler. During [[entities/earth|World]] War II, Feynman was recruited to the Manhattan Project at Los Alamos, where he contributed theoretical calculations regarding [[concepts/nuclear-weapons|nuclear weapons]] development and became known for his role in investigating safety issues and explaining complex concepts to non-specialists.

Feynman’s most enduring scientific legacy is the formulation of quantum electrodynamics, a [[concepts/theory|theory]] describing how [[concepts/light|light]] and matter interact. He developed the [[concepts/feynmans-all-paths-interpretation|path integral formulation]] of quantum mechanics and introduced Feynman [[concepts/diagrams|diagrams]], a pictorial representation that simplified the calculation of [[concepts/particle-dynamics|particle interactions]]. These innovations provided a rigorous mathematical framework for QED and earned him the Nobel Prize in Physics in 1965, which he shared with Julian Schwinger and Sin-Itiro Tomonaga.

Beyond his research, Feynman was a prominent [[concepts/phil-moriarty|educator]] and [[concepts/science|science]] communicator. He served as a professor at the California Institute of Technology for many years and authored several influential textbooks, such as *The [[concepts/feynman-lectures|Feynman Lectures]] on Physics*. His popular books, including *Surely You're Joking, Mr. Feynman!* and *What Do You Care What Other People Think?*, detailed his personal life, his curiosity, and his unconventional approach to [[concepts/problem-solving-skills|problem-solving]], cementing his status as a cultural icon of [[concepts/scientific-method|scientific inquiry]].
