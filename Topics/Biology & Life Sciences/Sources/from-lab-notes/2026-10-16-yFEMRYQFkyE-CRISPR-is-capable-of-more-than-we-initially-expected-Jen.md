## CRISPR is capable of more than we initially expected | Jennifer Doudna
**Clip title:** CRISPR is capable of more than we initially expected | Jennifer Doudna
**Author / channel:** The Well
**URL:** https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=yFEMRYQFkyE

### Summary
Here's a summary of the video:

The video features Jennifer Doudna, a Nobel Laureate in Chemistry, discussing the discovery and impact of CRISPR, a groundbreaking gene editing technology. Doudna explains the anatomy of CRISPR, comparing it to a word processor that allows scientists to make precise alterations to the code of life. She shares the story of its discovery, highlighting the collaboration with Emmanuelle Charpentier and the foundational research that led to understanding how CRISPR-Cas9 works.

Doudna addresses the transformative potential of CRISPR, particularly in treating severe genetic diseases like sickle-cell anemia, noting the excitement surrounding recent FDA approvals. She also discusses the ethical considerations and challenges associated with gene editing, such as the high cost of therapies and the need for scalable solutions. Doudna emphasizes the importance of responsible use and regulation, especially in sensitive applications like germline editing and gene drives.

Furthermore, Doudna explores the intersection of CRISPR and artificial intelligence, suggesting that AI accelerates biological research but still relies on human creativity and judgment. She concludes with an inspiring message about the ongoing pace of scientific discovery and the importance of supporting curiosity-driven research to address global health and environmental challenges.

#### Description
Nobel laureate Jennifer Doudna co-invented CRISPR in 2012. She's spent the years since watching it cure disease, spark debate, and even solve health and climate issues. Here’s what it’s doing today.

What does 14 years of gene editing actually add up to? Jennifer Doudna, the Nobel Prize-winning biochemist who co-invented CRISPR, walks through the full arc of the technology she helped create.

She covers the FDA-approved, functionally curative — but $2 million — CRISPR therapy for sickle cell disease, efforts to reduce methane emissions from cattle and rice farming, and the ongoing ethical reckoning. Doudna also reflects on how CRISPR's early framework for responsible use compares to today's AI race, and where she thinks gene editing goes from here.
