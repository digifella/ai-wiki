# Cosmic Inflation

Cosmic inflation is a theoretical framework in cosmology describing the universe's rapid exponential expansion during an extremely brief period immediately following the Big Bang. This expansion occurred within the first fraction of a second after the initial singularity, significantly altering the standard understanding of the early universe's evolution. The theory was originally proposed in the 1980s by Alan Guth and subsequently developed by physicists such as Andrei Linde to address specific inconsistencies in the standard Big Bang model, such as the horizon and flatness problems.

Recent research initiatives, including those conducted at the Perimeter Institute, have focused on the implications of inflation for the universe's creation and the search for Weakly Interacting Massive Particles (WIMPs). These efforts aim to bridge the gap between early universe dynamics and particle physics, exploring how inflationary models might inform the detection of dark matter candidates.

Current studies also address tensions regarding the curvature of the universe. Researchers are investigating whether observational data supports the flat geometry predicted by standard inflationary models or if deviations indicate new physics. This ongoing work seeks to refine the theoretical parameters of inflation and reconcile them with precise cosmological measurements.
