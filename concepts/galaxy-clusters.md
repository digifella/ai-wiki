# Galaxy Clusters

Galaxy clusters are among the largest gravitationally bound structures in the universe, containing hundreds to thousands of galaxies distributed across millions of light-years. These massive assemblies form the [[concepts/nodes|nodes]] of the cosmic web and represent systems in approximate equilibrium, where gravitational attraction balances outward [[concepts/pressure|pressure]] from hot gas. Clusters are held together primarily by [[concepts/gravitational-pull|gravity]] and serve as key laboratories for studying matter distribution on the largest observable [[concepts/musical-scales|scales]].

## Mass Composition and Observational Challenges

The internal structure of galaxy clusters reveals a significant discrepancy between visible matter and total mass. While the galaxies themselves contribute to the cluster's mass, the majority of the [[concepts/baryonic-matter|baryonic matter]] exists as hot, diffuse intracluster [[entities/medium|medium]] (ICM) gas that emits X-[[concepts/rays|rays]]. Observations of this gas, combined with gravitational lensing effects and galaxy [[concepts/velocity|velocity]] dispersions, indicate that [[concepts/dark-matter|dark matter]] constitutes approximately 85% of the total mass. This mass distribution is critical for testing cosmological models, as the abundance and evolution of clusters provide constraints on parameters such as the [[concepts/density|density]] of dark matter and the amplitude of matter fluctuations.

Discrepancies in mass estimates derived from different observational methods have led to ongoing debates regarding the nature of dark matter and baryonic [[concepts/physics|physics]]. For instance, the "missing baryon problem" and the tension in measurements of the Hubble constant ($H_0$) often utilize galaxy clusters as pivotal data points. Theoretical models must account for complex [[concepts/feedback|feedback]] [[concepts/causes|mechanisms]] from [[concepts/active-galactic-nuclei|active galactic nuclei]] and [[concepts/supernovae|supernovae]], which influence the thermal state of the intracluster gas and, consequently, the inferred mass profiles. These challenges highlight the necessity of precise multi-wavelength observations to distinguish between [[concepts/biases|systematic errors]] in data analysis and potential modifications to standard gravitational [[concepts/theory|theory]].
