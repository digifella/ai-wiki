# Ashokan Edicts

The Ashokan Edicts are a series of inscriptions commissioned by Emperor Ashoka of the Mauryan Empire (circa 268–232 BCE) and carved into stone pillars, rocks, and cave walls throughout his realm across the Indian subcontinent. Dating to the 3rd century BCE, these edicts represent some of the earliest surviving written records of Indian history and provide direct evidence of Ashoka's reign, philosophical outlook, and administrative policies. The inscriptions were composed in multiple languages and scripts, reflecting the linguistic diversity of the Mauryan territories.

## Content and Themes

The edicts communicate Ashoka's conversion to Buddhism following the bloody conquest of Kalinga and his subsequent adoption of a philosophy of non-violence and moral governance. They address diverse topics including animal welfare, environmental protection, public health, the promotion of dharma (righteousness), and the treatment of conquered peoples. The messages were intended to reach common subjects across the empire and represent an early example of mass communication through standardized public inscription.

## Historical Significance

The Ashokan Edicts serve as crucial historical documents for understanding Mauryan administration, ancient Indian languages, and the early spread of Buddhism. Their decipherment in the 19th century, particularly by scholar James Prinsep, provided scholars with datable inscriptional evidence and aided in the reconstruction of ancient Indian chronology. The edicts remain among the most important archaeological sources for the period and continue to inform understanding of ancient Indian political thought and social organization.
