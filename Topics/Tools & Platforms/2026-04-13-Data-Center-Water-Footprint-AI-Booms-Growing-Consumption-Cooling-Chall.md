## Data Center Water Footprint: AI Boom's Growing Consumption & Cooling Challenges
**Clip title:** The [[concepts/big-data|Big Data]] Center Water Problem
**Author / channel:** Asianometry
**URL:** https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=tJYSzc7YkY0

### Summary
The video "The Data Center Water Problem" highlights the significant and growing water footprint of data centers, exacerbated by the current [[concepts/ai-boom|AI boom]]. A data center with 15 megawatts of [[concepts/it-capacity|IT capacity]] can consume 80-130 million gallons of water annually, comparable to three hospitals or two 18-hole golf courses. Data centers [[concepts/range|range]] from small closet-sized setups to massive hyperscale facilities built by tech giants like Google and [[entities/meta-ai|Meta]], which span entire football fields. These large facilities are designed for [[concepts/energy-efficiency|energy efficiency]], [[concepts/scaling|scaling]], and economic benefits, and their energy efficiency is measured by Power Usage Effectiveness (PUE), with lower numbers indicating better efficiency. Hyperscale data centers achieve lower PUEs (around 1.1-1.2) compared to smaller ones, primarily due to more efficient [[concepts/cooling-systems|cooling systems]].

The majority of a data center's energy consumption converts to heat, necessitating robust cooling systems to prevent long-term damage to electronic equipment. Most data centers use [air cooling](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Air_cooling) through raised floor systems, where cold air is channeled to servers and hot air is recollected. This process typically involves a two-fluid [[concepts/loop|loop]] system—a refrigerant loop and a water-based condenser loop—to transfer heat away from the IT racks and ultimately dissipate it to the outside via [evaporative cooling](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Evaporative_cooling) towers. These cooling towers are a significant source of direct water consumption, as about 1% of the water evaporates for every 10 degrees Fahrenheit of cooling, requiring constant replenishment. Beyond direct cooling, data centers also have a substantial [indirect water footprint](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Indirect_water_footprint) due to the electricity generation required to power them, especially from water-intensive thermoelectric sources like coal, natural gas, and nuclear power plants. In 2022, data centers accounted for 1-1.3% of global electricity consumption, and the indirect water use for this energy can be 2-3 times higher than the direct cooling water consumption.

To address these concerns, companies are exploring and implementing various strategies. Some, like Apple, invest in clean energy to offset their power use, while others, such as Digital Realty and [[entities/amazon-web-services|AWS]], are utilizing [reclaimed wastewater](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Reclaimed_wastewater) or locating data centers in areas with access to recycled water. Many companies are also adopting "free cooling" techniques, which leverage natural environmental conditions. This can involve direct fresh air cooling, though it often requires filtering and dehumidification, or "[waterside free cooling](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Waterside_free_cooling)" by situating facilities near cold water bodies like Google's Hamina data center in Finland, which uses seawater. [Heat recapture](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Heat_recapture) is another promising [[concepts/solution|solution]], where waste heat from data centers is collected and repurposed for uses like district heating or [[concepts/desalination|desalination]], though this requires significant infrastructure. Furthermore, operating data centers at higher internal temperatures (within acceptable ASHRAE limits of 15-32°C) can also lead to energy savings, despite some conflicting data on its impact on hardware longevity. [Liquid cooling](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Liquid_cooling), directly applying cold liquid to chips, is gaining traction as a highly efficient method for capturing and transferring heat, reducing overall energy consumption and potentially improving processor performance.

The accelerating demand from AI and [[concepts/generative-ai|generative AI]] applications is rapidly escalating these challenges. Microsoft's 2022 sustainability report indicated a 34% jump in water consumption year-over-year, largely attributed to AI. Utility providers are seeing requests for individual data centers grow from historical demands of 30 megawatts to 60-90 megawatts, with entire campuses needing several gigawatts of power. New, powerful GPU products from companies like Nvidia are also significantly more power-hungry, further intensifying the demand for electricity and, consequently, water. In conclusion, the future of computing and AI hinges on the industry's ability to swiftly transition towards more sustainable practices. A comprehensive approach combining efficient cooling solutions, [waste heat recovery](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Waste_heat_recovery), and greater reliance on renewable energy sources will be crucial for managing the escalating energy and water demands, ensuring both environmental sustainability and the long-term viability of these critical digital infrastructures.
