# Dry Vermouth

Dry vermouth is a fortified wine produced by adding distilled spirits to a neutral white wine base, raising the alcohol content to approximately 18–20% by volume. The fortified wine is then infused with a blend of botanicals, herbs, and spices—including wormwood, anise, citrus peel, and various aromatic plants—that create its characteristic flavor profile. The defining feature of dry vermouth is its minimal residual sugar content, typically less than 4 grams per liter, which produces a crisp, bitter-herbal taste that distinguishes it from sweeter vermouth varieties.

## Production and Origins

Dry vermouth developed in Turin, Italy in the 18th century, where it became established as a distinctive category of fortified wine. The production process involves careful selection and blending of botanicals, which are macerated or infused into the fortified wine base. Different producers maintain proprietary recipes for their botanical blends, contributing to variations in flavor between brands and regions.

## Culinary Uses

Dry vermouth is most commonly used as an ingredient in cocktails, particularly in martinis and Manhattan-style drinks, where its herbal complexity complements spirits like gin and whiskey. It is also consumed as an aperitif, either neat or on the rocks, and can be used in cooking to enhance savory dishes and sauces. Once opened, dry vermouth should be refrigerated and consumed within several weeks to preserve its flavor characteristics.
