# Geologic Epochs

The Carboniferous and Permian periods represent two critical intervals in Earth's history spanning roughly 360 to 250 million years ago. These epochs are distinguished by dramatic shifts in atmospheric composition and the formation of extensive coal deposits that would later become important fossil fuel reserves. The geological and climatic conditions during these periods fundamentally altered the composition of Earth's atmosphere and set the stage for modern energy systems.

## Atmospheric Changes During Fossil Fuel Formation

As vast swamp forests thrived during the Carboniferous period, [[concepts/oxygen-saturation|atmospheric oxygen levels]] rose to some of the highest concentrations in Earth's history—potentially reaching 35% compared to today's 21%. This elevated oxygen supported the growth of enormous plants and arthropods. Simultaneously, [[concepts/carbon-dioxide-levels|carbon dioxide levels]] declined as massive quantities of plant material accumulated and eventually became buried, storing carbon in the form of coal and other organic deposits. The burial of this organic material meant less carbon dioxide was recycled back into the atmosphere through decomposition.

## The Permian Transition

The Permian period that followed saw different atmospheric dynamics. Oxygen levels began to decline from their Carboniferous peaks, while the continued burial of organic material kept atmospheric carbon dioxide relatively low. The end of the Permian witnessed the most severe mass extinction event in Earth's history, partly attributed to volcanic activity and changing atmospheric conditions. These periods collectively created the geologic conditions that concentrated vast stores of carbon in fossil form—energy sources that would only be released back into the atmosphere millions of years later through human combustion.
