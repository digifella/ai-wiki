# Qanat Management

[[concepts/qanats|Qanats]] are underground water conveyance systems developed in ancient [[concepts/iran|Persia]], consisting of gently sloping tunnels that transport groundwater from aquifers to the surface for irrigation and domestic use. These [[concepts/gravitational-pull|gravity]]-fed systems were engineered to minimize water loss through evaporation in arid climates and represent a significant technological achievement in hydraulic engineering. [[concepts/qanat|Qanat]] networks became widespread across [[entities/iran|Iran]] and the broader Middle East, forming the backbone of water infrastructure for millennia.

## Historical Importance and Decline

For centuries, qanat systems sustained agricultural production and settlement patterns across Iran's arid and semi-arid regions. However, since the early 20th century, traditional qanat management has undergone substantial decline. The introduction of mechanized drilling technology and electric pumps provided alternative water extraction methods that required less maintenance and labor, causing many communities to abandon qanat systems. Simultaneously, groundwater depletion and changing [[concepts/land-use|land use]] patterns reduced the viability of existing networks.

## Contemporary Water Crisis

The decline of qanat systems has coincided with Iran's modern water crisis, characterized by severe groundwater depletion, reduced river flows, and competition for limited water resources among agricultural, industrial, and urban sectors. While mechanized alternatives initially appeared to offer progress, they have often accelerated unsustainable groundwater extraction. Some researchers and [[concepts/water-management|water management]] specialists have advocated for reviving and rehabilitating traditional qanat systems as part of broader strategies to improve water sustainability and [[concepts/security|security]], recognizing the ecological and hydrological knowledge embedded in these historical networks.
