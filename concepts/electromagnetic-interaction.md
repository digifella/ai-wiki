Electromagnetic interaction is one of the four fundamental forces of nature, governing the behavior of charged particles through electric and magnetic fields. Mediated by [[concepts/light|photons]], this force is responsible for atomic structure, chemical [[concepts/link-aggregation|bonding]], and the propagation of light. It operates over infinite distances, though its strength diminishes with the square of the distance, and it is significantly stronger than [[concepts/gravitational-pull|gravity]] at atomic and molecular [[concepts/musical-scales|scales]].

In the context of astrophysical structure formation, electromagnetic interactions play a critical role in preventing the immediate collapse of baryonic matter into [[concepts/black-holes|black holes]]. Unlike [[concepts/dark-matter|dark matter]], which interacts primarily through gravity, [[concepts/baryonic-matter|ordinary matter]] consists of charged particles that repel each other when compressed. This electromagnetic repulsion, along with thermal [[concepts/pressure|pressure]] generated by particle collisions, provides the necessary outward force to counteract gravitational attraction during the formation of [[concepts/stellar-objects|stars]] and galaxies.

Dark matter, by definition, does not participate in electromagnetic interactions. It is electrically neutral and does not emit, absorb, or reflect light. Consequently, dark matter lacks the mechanism for dissipating energy through radiation or experiencing pressure support against gravity. While this allows dark matter to form large-scale [[concepts/halos|halos]] that guide [[concepts/galaxy-formation|galaxy formation]], it also means dark matter cannot fragment and collapse into dense, compact objects like black holes through the same processes that govern ordinary matter. The absence of electromagnetic forces in dark matter dynamics is a key distinction in cosmological models, explaining why visible matter forms discrete structures while dark matter remains diffuse.
