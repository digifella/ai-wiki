# Mayan Mathematics

The [[concepts/mayan-culture|Maya civilization]] developed a sophisticated mathematical system between approximately 2000 BCE and the Spanish conquest in the 16th century. Unlike the base-10 system that would later dominate Western [[concepts/mathematics|mathematics]], the Maya employed a base-20 (vigesimal) notation—likely influenced by counting on both fingers and toes. This system proved remarkably effective for their practical purposes: managing intricate calendars, facilitating trade, and conducting astronomical observations.

## The Vigesimal System and Zero

A defining feature of Mayan mathematics was their use of [[concepts/number-system|positional notation]] combined with the concept of [[concepts/concept-of-nothingness|zero]]—a mathematical [[concepts/innovation|innovation]] that appeared independently in very few ancient cultures. The Maya represented numbers using combinations of dots (representing units) and bars (representing fives), arranged vertically in columns where each position represented a power of twenty. This allowed them to express large numbers and perform complex calculations essential for tracking their elaborate Long Count calendar, which could span millions of days.

## Practical Applications

Mayan mathematics was fundamentally tied to practical concerns rather than abstract [[concepts/theory|theory]]. Astronomers used the system to predict celestial events and maintain calendrical [[concepts/accuracy|precision]], while administrators applied it to taxation, resource management, and construction projects. The mathematical sophistication necessary to reconcile their multiple overlapping calendar systems—including the 365-day solar year, the 260-day ritual calendar, and the Long Count—demonstrates the system's computational power.

## Historical Significance

[[concepts/feynman|Richard Feynman]] referenced Mayan mathematics to illustrate an important principle: that mathematics functions as a practical tool for solving real-[[entities/earth|world]] problems rather than existing solely as abstract understanding. The Maya had no apparent interest in mathematics for its own sake, yet their system enabled them to accomplish remarkable feats of astronomical [[concepts/user-attention-prediction|prediction]] and administrative [[concepts/coordination|coordination]]—demonstrating that effective mathematics can emerge from necessity rather than philosophical inquiry.
