# Neuroplasticity

Neuroplasticity, also known as brain plasticity, is the nervous system's ability to change its structure and function in response to experience. Unlike the historical view of the adult brain as a static organ, modern neuroscience recognizes that neural networks remain dynamic throughout life. This adaptability allows the brain to reorganize synaptic connections, strengthen or weaken existing pathways, and even generate new neurons in specific regions, primarily in response to learning, environmental stimuli, or injury.

The mechanisms underlying neuroplasticity involve both structural and functional changes. At the cellular level, this includes synaptic plasticity, where the strength of connections between neurons is modified based on activity levels. Long-term potentiation and long-term depression are key processes that facilitate this adjustment. Additionally, neurogenesis, the formation of new neurons, occurs in certain areas such as the hippocampus, contributing to memory formation and cognitive flexibility. These changes are driven by molecular and genetic factors that respond to neural activity patterns.

Neuroplasticity plays a critical role in recovery from brain injuries, such as strokes or traumatic brain injuries. By rerouting functions from damaged areas to healthy regions, the brain can restore lost capabilities through rehabilitation and repeated practice. It also underpins the learning process, allowing individuals to acquire new skills, languages, and knowledge. The extent of plasticity varies across the lifespan, being most pronounced during early development but remaining significant in adulthood, demonstrating that the brain retains a capacity for change regardless of age.
