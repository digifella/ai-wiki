# Dreyfus And Dreyfus

Hubert [[entities/dreyfus|Dreyfus]] and Stuart Dreyfus are philosophers and cognitive scientists known for developing an influential model of [[concepts/skill|skill]] acquisition. In the 1980s, they proposed a five-stage framework describing how individuals progress from novice to expert in various domains: novice, advanced beginner, competent, proficient, and expert. Their model emphasized that [[concepts/expertise|expertise]] involves developing intuition and tacit knowledge that cannot be fully reduced to explicit rules or computational procedures.

## Critique of Expert Systems

The Dreyfus brothers became known for critiquing [[concepts/ai-technologies|artificial intelligence]] research, particularly [[concepts/expert-systems|expert systems]] that were prominent in the 1980s. They argued that these systems operated at the "competent" level at best, following explicit rules and lacking the intuitive judgment characteristic of true experts. Their work suggested fundamental limitations in how machines could replicate human expertise, as [[concepts/unconscious-competence|expert performance]] relies on [[concepts/thematic-analysis|pattern recognition]] and contextual judgment developed through extensive [[concepts/experience|experience]] rather than rule-based [[concepts/reasoning|reasoning]].

## Legacy and Influence

The [[concepts/dreyfus-model|Dreyfus model]] has been widely applied in educational and professional development contexts, influencing how organizations think about training and expertise. However, their skepticism about [[entities/ai|artificial intelligence]]'s capacity to achieve genuine expertise has been debated among researchers. Subsequent advances in machine [[concepts/learning|learning]] and [[concepts/ai-models|AI systems]] have prompted ongoing discussion about whether their critiques remain valid or require reassessment in [[concepts/light|light]] of modern computational capabilities.
