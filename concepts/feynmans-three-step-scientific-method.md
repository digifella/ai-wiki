# Feynmans Three Step Scientific Method

Feynman's three-step scientific method is a simplified framework for empirical investigation articulated by physicist [[concepts/feynman|Richard Feynman]]. The approach distills the scientific process into three essential stages: making an initial guess about how nature works, computing the predictions that follow from that guess, and comparing those predictions against observations from experiments and the natural [[entities/earth|world]]. This framework emphasizes the cyclical and self-correcting nature of [[concepts/scientific-method|scientific inquiry]].

The first step involves formulating a hypothesis or "guess" regarding the underlying [[concepts/causes|mechanisms]] of a phenomenon. This initial conjecture serves as the foundational model from which all subsequent logical deductions are derived. Feynman stressed that the quality of the guess is critical, as it determines the scope and validity of the entire investigative process.

The second step requires rigorous [[concepts/computation|computation]] to derive specific, [[concepts/observable-consequences|testable predictions]] from the initial hypothesis. This stage bridges theoretical [[concepts/abstraction-layer|abstraction]] and empirical reality by translating abstract concepts into quantifiable outcomes. The predictions must be precise enough to allow for unambiguous [[concepts/verification|verification]] or falsification through subsequent experimentation.

The final step entails comparing the computed predictions against actual observations from nature. If the predictions align with experimental data, the hypothesis gains credibility; if they diverge, the hypothesis must be revised or discarded. This comparison ensures that scientific knowledge remains grounded in [[concepts/empirical-evidence|empirical evidence]] rather than mere speculation, reinforcing the iterative nature of discovery.
