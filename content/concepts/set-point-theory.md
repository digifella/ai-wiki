# Set Point Theory

Set Point Theory is a biological [[concepts/regulation|regulation]] principle proposing that organisms maintain stable internal states around predetermined reference points, known as set points. These reference values act as targets for homeostatic mechanisms, allowing systems to detect deviations and initiate corrective [[concepts/responses|responses]]. The [[concepts/theory|theory]] applies across multiple biological [[concepts/musical-scales|scales]], from cellular processes to whole-organism physiology, explaining how living systems achieve and maintain equilibrium despite environmental fluctuations.

## Physiological Applications

The concept originated in studies of temperature regulation, where organisms maintain core body temperature around a species-specific set point through behavioral and physiological [[concepts/adjustments|adjustments]]. Similar mechanisms operate in other systems: blood glucose levels are regulated around approximately 100 mg/dL, blood pressure around 120/80 mmHg, and osmolarity through fluid balance mechanisms. When actual values deviate from their set points, [[concepts/feedback|feedback]] [[concepts/loops|loops]] trigger compensatory responses—such as sweating when body temperature exceeds its set point, or insulin release when glucose rises above threshold.

## Broader Implications

Set Point Theory extends beyond simple physiological variables to complex systems including weight regulation and potentially psychological states. The theory suggests that body weight may be defended around a genetically influenced set point through adjustments in appetite and metabolism, though this remains debated in obesity research. Critics note that set points may be more flexible or plastic than traditional formulations suggest, particularly in response to prolonged environmental changes or aging.
