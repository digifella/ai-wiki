### Summary
The recent findings from the [[concepts/the-composition-of-the-universe-extends-beyond-what-is-directly-observable|Dark Energy]] [[concepts/survey|Survey]] (DES) and the [[concepts/the-composition-of-the-universe-extends-beyond-what-is-directly-observable|Dark Energy]] Spectroscopic Instrument (DESI) have sparked renewed interest in the nature of dark energy, the mysterious force driving the [[concepts/accelerating-universe|accelerating expansion]] of the universe. While DESI's initial [[concepts/deployment|release]] hinted that dark energy might not be a constant cosmic force, the latest comprehensive results from DES offer a complementary perspective. The overarching question explored is not merely whether one [[concepts/scientific-experiment|experiment]] confirms the other, but rather why combining these distinct scientific studies provides a more powerful and nuanced understanding of cosmological parameters than any single observation alone. This approach highlights a fundamental aspect of modern [[concepts/science|science]]: narrowing down possibilities rather than seeking direct, singular measurements.

Both DESI and DES employ unique methodologies to probe the universe. DESI focuses on precisely measuring the distances to [[concepts/electrical-stimulation|tens]] of millions of galaxies, constructing an unprecedented three-dimensional map of the cosmos. By studying Baryon Acoustic Oscillations (BAOs)—faint fossil patterns left by sound waves in the [[concepts/early-universe|early universe]] that act as a cosmic ruler—DESI traces the expansion history with extraordinary [[concepts/accuracy|precision]]. In [[concepts/contrast|contrast]], DES adopted a broader approach, utilizing a dedicated 570-megapixel camera on the Blanco 4-meter telescope to scan a vast region of the southern sky for six years. DES's efforts concentrated on measuring tiny distortions in distant galaxy shapes caused by weak gravitational lensing, mapping the invisible distribution of [[concepts/dark-matter|dark matter]], tracking the growth of cosmic structures, and observing thousands of Type Ia [[concepts/supernovae|supernovae]] ([[concepts/standard-candles|standard candles]] that first led to the discovery of dark energy), while also independently measuring BAOs.

The true strength of these diverse approaches lies in their ability to eliminate countless hypothetical universes that do not align with observed reality. No single [[concepts/scientific-experiment|experiment]] directly measures dark energy; instead, cosmologists observe various phenomena like galaxy distances, distortions, and ancient temperature fluctuations (the [[concepts/cosmic-microwave-background|Cosmic Microwave Background]]) that are all influenced by a set of unknown cosmological parameters. These parameters, collectively, dictate how the universe expands and how structures form. However, a significant challenge arises from "degeneracies," where multiple plausible parameter combinations could yield the same observations, and "covariance," where different probes may share underlying [[concepts/biases|biases]] or rely on similar assumptions. By combining data from complementary probes, scientists can rigorously rule out these possibilities, minimizing uncertainties and tightening constraints on the universe's fundamental properties.

The final results from DES are remarkably consistent with the standard cosmological model, known as Lambda-CDM, which posits dark energy as a cosmological constant. However, crucially, DES did not definitively rule out the intriguing hints from DESI that dark energy might be decreasing over time, suggesting that this possibility remains an exciting area of investigation. This continuous process of asking different questions and eliminating inconsistent realities [[entities/will|will]] intensify with forthcoming missions like the Rubin Observatory, Euclid, and the Roman Space Telescope. These next-generation instruments promise to discover billions of new galaxies and monitor the universe with unprecedented depth and [[concepts/solution|resolution]] over the coming decade, steadily pushing [[concepts/cosmology|cosmology]] closer to pinpointing the precise values of the cosmological parameters that define our unique spacetime.
