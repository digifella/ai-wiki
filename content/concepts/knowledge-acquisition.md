# Knowledge Acquisition

Knowledge acquisition in business strategy refers to the systematic process of gathering, processing, and integrating information into [[concepts/organization|organizational systems]], particularly through the [[concepts/training|training]] of [[concepts/artificial-intelligence-models|artificial intelligence models]] and [[concepts/expert-systems|expert systems]]. This encompasses both the technical mechanisms by which models learn from data and the strategic frameworks organizations use to capture and operationalize domain expertise. The field bridges traditional [[concepts/knowledge-management|knowledge management]] practices with modern machine [[concepts/learning|learning]] approaches, recognizing that effective knowledge transfer requires intentional design of both data inputs and learning architectures.

## Skill Development Framework

The [[entities/dreyfus-and-dreyfus|Dreyfus and Dreyfus]] model of skill acquisition provides a foundational framework for understanding knowledge acquisition, distinguishing between novice, advanced beginner, competent, proficient, and expert levels. This progression reflects how individuals and systems move from rule-based decision-making toward intuitive, context-aware judgment. Applied to AI and expert systems, this framework highlights the difference between systems trained on explicit rules versus those that develop nuanced understanding through [[concepts/exposure|exposure]] to diverse real-world [[concepts/scenarios|scenarios]]. Organizations implementing knowledge acquisition strategies must account for these developmental stages, recognizing that early-stage systems require different training approaches and oversight than those approaching expert-level performance.

## Implementation Challenges

Knowledge acquisition faces practical obstacles in contemporary business environments, particularly when deploying [[concepts/large-language-model-llm|large language models]] and AI systems across organizations. The gap between [[concepts/training-data|training data]] and actual operational requirements can lead to systems that perform well in controlled settings but fail in applied contexts. Additionally, the acquisition of tacit knowledge—expertise held by experienced professionals—remains difficult to systematize, requiring careful [[concepts/interview-techniques|interview techniques]] and scenario analysis to surface genuine competencies rather than surface-level [[concepts/responses|responses]]. Successful knowledge acquisition demands ongoing calibration between theoretical model performance and real-world business outcomes.
