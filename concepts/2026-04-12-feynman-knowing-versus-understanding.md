# 2026 04 12 Feynman Knowing Versus Understanding

Richard Feynman, an American theoretical physicist best known for his contributions to quantum electrodynamics, developed a sustained critique of superficial learning throughout his career as both a researcher and educator. His observations centered on a fundamental distinction: knowing the name or definition of something differs fundamentally from understanding how it actually works. This critique emerged from his experience teaching and observing students who could recite textbook definitions or use technical terms correctly in sentences without grasping the underlying mechanisms or principles those terms described.

## The Core Distinction

For Feynman, genuine understanding required the ability to explain a concept in simple terms, predict how systems would behave under different conditions, and recognize connections between ideas. By contrast, mere knowledge—what he sometimes called "knowing the name"—involved memorizing definitions, vocabulary, and facts without this deeper comprehension. He argued that educational systems, particularly in science and mathematics, frequently rewarded the acquisition of terminology and the ability to reproduce received knowledge, inadvertently encouraging students to mistake familiarity with words for actual understanding.

## Educational Impact

Feynman's distinction became influential in twentieth-century debates about pedagogy and scientific literacy. His observation that students often internalize names and labels while remaining unable to apply or manipulate the underlying concepts raised questions about assessment methods and curriculum design. The critique suggested that education could effectively obscure gaps in understanding by creating the appearance of knowledge through linguistic competence alone, a concern that has continued to shape discussions of how scientific and technical subjects should be taught.
