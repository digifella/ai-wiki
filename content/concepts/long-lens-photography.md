# Long Lens Photography

Long lens photography is a technique that combines [[concepts/telephoto-lens|telephoto]] lenses with image stitching to create wide-angle compositions while retaining the visual characteristics of compressed perspective. Rather than using a [[concepts/ultra-wide-lens|wide-angle lens]] directly, photographers capture multiple overlapping frames with a longer [[concepts/focal-length|focal length]] and stitch them together into a panorama. This approach preserves the [[concepts/lens-compression|lens compression]] effect—where distant elements appear closer together and more densely packed—while achieving an overall wide field of view.

## Lens Compression and Perspective

Telephoto lenses naturally compress spatial [[concepts/relationships|relationships]], making foreground and background elements appear closer together than they do to the naked eye. By stitching multiple long-lens frames, photographers maintain this compression effect across a wider [[concepts/wide-angle-composition|panoramic composition]]. This differs from using a wide-angle lens, which exaggerates depth and spreads elements apart. The resulting panoramas convey a flattened, layered quality that can emphasize patterns, [[concepts/texture|texture]], and spatial density in a scene.

## Practical Applications

Long lens panoramas are particularly useful in landscape and architectural photography where foreground-background relationships matter. The technique allows photographers to capture expansive scenes without distortion artifacts common to ultra-wide lenses. It also provides greater flexibility in framing, as the photographer can compose by selecting which elements to include across multiple shots rather than being constrained by a single fixed focal length.
