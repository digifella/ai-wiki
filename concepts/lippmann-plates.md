# Lippmann Plates

Lippmann plates are specialized photographic media used in Lippmann photography, a color imaging technique developed by Gabriel Lippmann in 1891. Unlike conventional color photography that relies on chemical dyes or pigments to reproduce hues, Lippmann plates capture color through optical interference patterns formed directly within the photographic emulsion. This method records the wavelength of light physically, creating a permanent record of the light's spectral composition without the need for color filters or dyes.

The process involves pressing a layer of mercury against the back of a glass plate coated with a fine-grain, high-resolution photographic emulsion. When exposed to light, the incoming waves interfere with the reflected waves from the mercury, creating standing waves within the emulsion. These standing waves expose the silver halide crystals at specific depths corresponding to the wavelength of the incident light, effectively recording the color information in the physical structure of the plate rather than in chemical pigments.

Upon development, the plate appears monochromatic but exhibits iridescent colors when viewed under white light. This phenomenon, known as structural color, arises because the periodic layers of silver act as a Bragg reflector, selectively reflecting specific wavelengths based on the viewing angle. This demonstrates the distinction between true color, defined by the physical properties of light, and the illusion of color created by subtractive mixing in traditional photography.

Lippmann photography is historically significant for its scientific precision and artistic uniqueness. Although the process is complex and requires precise control over exposure and development, it produces images with exceptional resolution and color fidelity. The technique remains a notable example of how physical optics can be harnessed to capture reality, serving as a foundational concept in the study of structural color and interferometry.
