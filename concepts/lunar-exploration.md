# Lunar Exploration

Lunar exploration encompasses scientific and crewed missions designed to study the Moon and establish a sustained human presence in lunar orbit and on the lunar surface. Modern programs build upon decades of robotic reconnaissance, including orbital mapping missions and lander deployments, as well as the Apollo program's achievements in the 1960s and 1970s. Contemporary initiatives integrate advanced spacecraft systems, updated operational procedures, and refined technologies developed since the last crewed lunar landing in 1972.

## Current Programs

NASA's Artemis program represents the primary contemporary effort to return humans to the Moon. The program aims to land the first woman and the next man on the lunar surface, specifically targeting the south pole region. Artemis missions serve as critical tests for crewed spacecraft systems and operations, including the Space Launch System rocket and the Orion spacecraft. These missions are designed to establish the Lunar Gateway, a space station in orbit around the Moon, which will support long-term exploration and serve as a staging point for surface missions.

## Historical Context

Previous lunar exploration was dominated by the Apollo program, which successfully landed twelve astronauts on the Moon between 1969 and 1972. Prior to and following these crewed missions, numerous robotic probes conducted orbital mapping and surface analysis. These earlier efforts provided the foundational data and engineering experience that inform current international and commercial partnerships. The transition from the Apollo era to modern exploration involves a shift toward sustainable operations and international collaboration rather than singular national achievements.
