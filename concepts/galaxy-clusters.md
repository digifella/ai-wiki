# Galaxy Clusters

Galaxy clusters are among the largest gravitationally bound structures in the universe, containing hundreds to thousands of galaxies distributed across millions of light-years. These massive assemblies form the nodes of the cosmic web and represent systems in approximate equilibrium, where gravitational attraction balances outward pressure from hot intracluster gas. Clusters are held together primarily by gravity and serve as key laboratories for studying matter distribution on the largest observable scales.

The mass composition of galaxy clusters presents significant challenges to current cosmological models. Observations indicate that visible baryonic matter accounts for only a small fraction of the total mass required to hold these structures together. The majority of the mass is attributed to dark matter, which does not emit light but exerts gravitational influence detectable through X-ray emissions from hot gas and gravitational lensing effects.

Discrepancies between the observed distribution of baryonic matter and the predicted distribution of dark matter have led to ongoing debates regarding the nature of dark matter itself. Some theories suggest modifications to gravitational laws on large scales, while others refine the standard cold dark matter model to account for complex baryonic feedback processes. These investigations remain central to understanding the formation and evolution of large-scale structures in the universe.
