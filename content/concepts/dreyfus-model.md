# Dreyfus Model

The Dreyfus Model is a framework developed by Stuart and Hubert [[entities/dreyfus|Dreyfus]] that describes the stages through which people progress when acquiring [[concepts/skills|skills]]. The model proposes five levels of proficiency: novice, advanced beginner, competent, proficient, and expert. Each stage represents increasing sophistication in how practitioners understand and apply knowledge, moving from rigid rule-following to intuitive, context-dependent [[concepts/decision-making|decision-making]].

## Application to Expert Systems

The model became relevant to discussions of [[concepts/ai-technologies|artificial intelligence]] and [[concepts/expert-systems|expert systems]] in the 1980s. The [[entities/dreyfus-and-dreyfus|Dreyfus brothers]] argued that expert systems—computerized systems designed to replicate human expertise through encoded rules and logic—were fundamentally limited because they could only operate [[concepts/assistive-technology|at]] the competent level of human [[concepts/skill|skill]] development. Expert systems relied on explicit rules extracted from experts, but could not achieve the intuitive, pattern-recognition [[concepts/capabilities|capabilities]] that characterize truly expert human performance.

## Limitations and Critique

The Dreyfus Model's applicability to machine [[concepts/learning|learning]] and artificial systems has been subject to criticism. Scholars including [[entities/gobel|Gobel]] and [[entities/chassy|Chassy]] have challenged the model's assumptions about the distinct boundaries between skill levels and questioned whether its framework accurately describes how both humans and machines learn [[concepts/complex-tasks|complex tasks]]. Despite these criticisms, the model remains influential in discussions about the gap between rule-based artificial intelligence and more sophisticated forms of [[concepts/machine-learning|machine learning]].
