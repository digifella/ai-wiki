# Egg Texture

Achieving tender, [[concepts/perfect-scrambled-eggs|creamy scrambled eggs]] requires careful control of three primary variables: [[concepts/salt-timing|salt timing]], liquid ratio, and cooking method. According to [[entities/americas-test-kitchen|America's Test Kitchen]], proper technique in each of these areas produces consistent results across different cooking conditions.

## Salt Timing

Salt should be added to raw eggs before cooking rather than after. When salt dissolves in the eggs, it breaks down proteins and allows them to retain more moisture during the cooking process. This pre-salting approach prevents the dense, rubbery [[concepts/texture-slider|texture]] that results from adding salt to already-cooked eggs, which can draw out moisture. Optimal results occur when salt is mixed in at least five minutes before cooking begins, giving it time to fully dissolve and affect the [[concepts/protein-structure|protein structure]].

## Liquid and Heat Control

The ratio of added liquid—typically water, milk, or cream—to eggs affects both [[concepts/texture|texture]] and richness. More liquid produces softer, more custard-like eggs, while less liquid yields a firmer result. Cooking temperature is equally critical: low to medium-low heat allows proteins to denature gradually, preventing overcooking and the separation of curds and whey that creates an unpleasant, watery texture.

## Equipment Considerations

The choice of pan and cooking vessel influences heat distribution and the rate at which eggs cook. Heavier-bottomed pans distribute heat more evenly, reducing hot spots that can cause some portions to overcook before others are set. Constant, gentle [[concepts/stirring-technique|stirring]] throughout cooking ensures uniform texture development and prevents large curds from forming.
