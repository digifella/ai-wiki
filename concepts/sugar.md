# Sugar

Sugar has been consumed by humans for thousands of years, with evidence of sugarcane cultivation dating back to ancient India and Papua New Guinea. Early civilizations extracted juice from sugarcane and boiled it to create crystalline sugar through a labor-intensive process that remained localized to specific regions for centuries. The substance was initially rare and expensive in Europe and the Middle East, where it was treated as a luxury good and medicinal compound rather than an everyday foodstuff.

## Medieval Trade and Early Colonization

Arab traders and later Venetian merchants facilitated the spread of sugar cultivation across the Mediterranean and the Middle East during the medieval period. As European demand grew, Portuguese and Spanish explorers introduced sugarcane to Atlantic islands such as Madeira and the Canary Islands in the 15th century. This expansion marked the beginning of large-scale plantation agriculture, which relied heavily on enslaved labor to meet the increasing European appetite for sweeteners.

## The Atlantic Sugar Complex

The establishment of sugar plantations in the Caribbean and Brazil during the 16th and 17th centuries created a triangular trade network that linked Europe, Africa, and the Americas. This system drove the transatlantic slave trade, as the brutal conditions of sugar production necessitated a continuous supply of forced labor. Sugar transformed from a rare spice into a staple commodity, fundamentally altering global diets and economies while entrenching systems of racialized enslavement that persisted for centuries.
