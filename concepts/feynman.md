# Feynman

Richard Feynman (1918–1988) was an American theoretical physicist whose contributions to quantum mechanics and quantum electrodynamics fundamentally advanced twentieth-century physics. Born in Far Rockaway, New York, he completed his doctorate at Princeton University in 1942 under John Archibald Wheeler. During World War II, Feynman worked at Los Alamos as part of the Manhattan Project, where he performed theoretical calculations related to nuclear weapons development.

## Scientific Work

Feynman's most significant contribution was the development of quantum electrodynamics (QED), for which he shared the 1965 Nobel Prize in Physics with Sin-Itiro Tomonaga and Julian Schwinger. He introduced Feynman diagrams—a visual representation method for particle interactions—which became a standard tool in theoretical physics. His work provided solutions to problems in quantum field theory that had resisted earlier approaches and established methods still used in particle physics today.

## Later Career and Legacy

Following his academic career at Cornell University and the California Institute of Technology, Feynman became known for his distinctive communication style and pedagogical approach. He also gained public recognition through his role investigating the Space Shuttle Challenger disaster in 1986 and through popular physics books and lectures. His emphasis on understanding fundamental principles rather than memorizing formulas influenced how physics has been taught. Feynman died in 1988 from cancer at age 69.
