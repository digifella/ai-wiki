## Overview

The P vs NP problem stands as one of the most significant unsolved questions in theoretical computer science and mathematics. At its core, the problem asks whether every problem whose solution can be quickly *verified* by a computer can also be quickly *solved* by a computer. P represents the class of problems solvable in polynomial time, while NP represents problems whose solutions can be verified in polynomial time. The question of whether P equals NP—or whether these classes are distinct—remains open despite decades of intensive research.

## Computational Significance

The practical implications of solving the P vs NP problem are profound. If P were to equal NP, it would mean that thousands of problems currently believed to be computationally intractable could be solved efficiently. This would have immediate consequences for cryptography, optimization, artificial intelligence, and numerous other fields. Conversely, if P does not equal NP (the widely held conjecture), it confirms fundamental limits on computational capability and validates the security assumptions underlying modern encryption.

## Historical Context

The problem was formally posed in the 1970s and has since become one of the Clay Mathematics Institute's Millennium Prize Problems, with a one-million-dollar reward for its resolution. Countless attempted proofs have emerged, though none have withstood peer review. The problem's centrality to complexity theory has made it a touchstone for understanding the nature of computational difficulty and what problems machines can realistically solve.
