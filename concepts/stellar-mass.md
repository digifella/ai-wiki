# Stellar Mass

Stellar mass refers to the total mass of a star, typically expressed in solar masses (M☉), where one solar mass equals approximately 2 × 10³⁰ kilograms. Stars exhibit a wide range of masses, from approximately 0.08 solar masses for the smallest red dwarfs to over 100 solar masses for the most massive stars observed. Stellar mass is a fundamental parameter that directly determines a star's luminosity, core temperature, nuclear fusion rate, lifespan, and evolutionary trajectory.

## Mass-Luminosity Relationship

For main-sequence stars, there is a strong correlation between mass and luminosity, often described by the mass-luminosity relation. This relationship indicates that more massive stars are significantly brighter than less massive ones, with luminosity scaling roughly as the mass raised to the power of 3.5 for stars similar to the Sun. This proportionality arises because higher mass increases gravitational pressure in the core, accelerating nuclear fusion and energy output.

## Evolutionary Implications

The initial mass of a star dictates its entire life cycle. Low-mass stars burn their fuel slowly and can live for trillions of years, while high-mass stars consume their nuclear fuel rapidly and end their lives in supernova explosions. The final remnant of a star—whether a white dwarf, neutron star, or black hole—is determined by the mass of its core after it has exhausted its nuclear fuel.

## Sub-Solar Mass Objects

While traditional stellar formation processes generally produce objects above the hydrogen-burning limit, recent gravitational wave detections have provided evidence for sub-solar mass compact objects. These findings suggest the existence of primordial black holes or other exotic remnants that do not fit standard stellar evolution models, expanding the understanding of mass distribution in the universe beyond conventional stellar boundaries.
