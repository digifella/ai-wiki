# Tsunami

A tsunami is a series of large ocean waves generated by sudden, large-scale disturbances that displace a significant volume of water. The primary triggers include submarine earthquakes, volcanic eruptions, underwater landslides, and meteorite impacts. Among these causes, undersea earthquakes account for the majority of destructive tsunamis globally. When the seafloor shifts abruptly during seismic activity, it vertically displaces the overlying water column, initiating waves that radiate outward from the epicenter at high speeds.

Tsunamis differ fundamentally from typical wind-driven waves in both their origin and physical behavior. In the deep ocean, tsunami waves have long wavelengths and low amplitudes, allowing them to travel at speeds comparable to a jet aircraft with minimal energy loss. As these waves approach shallow coastal waters, their speed decreases while their height increases dramatically due to the shoaling effect. This transformation results in the characteristic massive walls of water that cause widespread devastation upon landfall.

The impact of a tsunami is not limited to a single wave but occurs as a sequence of waves, known as a wave train, arriving over a period of hours. The timing and intensity of each wave vary depending on the local bathymetry and coastline geometry. Because the first wave to arrive is not necessarily the largest, coastal communities remain at risk for an extended duration after the initial impact. Effective warning systems and understanding of these propagation dynamics are critical for mitigating loss of life and property in vulnerable regions.
