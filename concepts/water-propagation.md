# Water Propagation

Water propagation is a horticultural technique in which plants grow in water rather than soil, deriving their nutrients from dissolved minerals in the solution. This method is used both for propagating new plants from cuttings and for maintaining mature plants as permanent hydroponic specimens. The practice eliminates the need for soil medium while allowing roots to absorb water and nutrients directly from the surrounding liquid environment.

## Common Applications

Many indoor houseplants adapt well to water propagation, including pothos, philodendron, coleus, and lucky bamboo. These species typically develop water roots—which differ structurally from terrestrial roots—within one to three weeks of being placed in water. Water propagation is particularly useful for creating new plants from stem cuttings, as the transparent growing medium allows growers to monitor root development easily.

## Practical Considerations

Successful water propagation requires periodic water changes to maintain dissolved oxygen levels and prevent bacterial growth. Some growers add liquid fertilizers to provide essential nutrients that would otherwise come from soil. Container choice matters; clear vessels allow light penetration and root observation, though any watertight container functions mechanically. Plants grown this way may require adjustment periods if later transferred to soil, as their water-adapted root systems must acclimate to terrestrial conditions.
