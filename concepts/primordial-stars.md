# Primordial Stars

Primordial stars, theoretically designated as Population III stars, represent the first generation of stellar objects to form in the universe. They are predicted to have emerged within the first few hundred million years after the Big Bang, arising from primordial gas clouds composed almost entirely of hydrogen and helium. Unlike subsequent stellar generations, these early stars formed from pristine material with negligible heavy element content, a composition that fundamentally distinguished their physical properties and evolutionary paths from modern stars.

Due to the absence of metals, which act as efficient coolants in stellar formation, primordial gas clouds required higher densities to collapse under gravity. This environment likely led to the formation of extremely massive stars, potentially hundreds of times more massive than the Sun. These massive progenitors burned through their fuel rapidly, ending their short lives in powerful supernovae or direct collapse into black holes, thereby seeding the early universe with the first heavy elements necessary for subsequent generations of stars and planets.

Recent astronomical observations have provided indirect evidence supporting the existence of these early stellar populations. Data from the James Webb Space Telescope (JWST) has identified signatures consistent with Population III star formation in the high-redshift galaxy GN-z11. These findings help constrain models of cosmic reionization and the chemical enrichment history of the early universe, bridging the gap between theoretical predictions and observable cosmological phenomena.
