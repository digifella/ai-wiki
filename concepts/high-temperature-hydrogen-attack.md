# High Temperature Hydrogen Attack

High Temperature Hydrogen Attack (HTHA) is a degradation mechanism affecting steel and other ferrous alloys exposed to hydrogen-rich environments at elevated temperatures, typically above 200°C. The process occurs when hydrogen atoms diffuse into the steel matrix and [[concepts/react-framework|react]] with carbon present in the material to form methane gas. Unlike atomic hydrogen, which can diffuse back out of steel, methane molecules are too large to escape through the material's lattice structure and become trapped internally.

## Mechanism and Effects

The accumulation of pressurized methane in internal voids, grain boundaries, and inclusions creates significant localized [[concepts/stress|stress]]. As the [[concepts/pressure|pressure]] exceeds the material's strength, microcracks initiate and propagate, leading to decarburization and a loss of mechanical [[concepts/honesty|integrity]]. This results in a reduction in ductility and [[concepts/tensile-strength|tensile strength]], often manifesting as intergranular cracking that is difficult to detect through standard non-destructive testing methods.

## Prevention and Mitigation

[[concepts/countermeasures|Mitigation strategies]] primarily involve material selection and operational controls. Engineers typically specify low-carbon steels or alloys stabilized with carbide-forming elements such as chromium, molybdenum, or vanadium, which form stable carbides that do not react with hydrogen. Additionally, maintaining operating temperatures below the threshold for significant hydrogen diffusion or reducing hydrogen partial pressure in the service environment are critical [[concepts/primary-prevention|preventive measures]]. Regular inspection using advanced ultrasonic techniques is essential for monitoring existing equipment for signs of internal damage.
