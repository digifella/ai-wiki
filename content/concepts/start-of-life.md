# Start of Life

The origins of life on Earth remain one of the central questions in [[concepts/science|science]], spanning biology, chemistry, [[concepts/geology|geology]], and anthropology. Life is believed to have emerged sometime between 3.5 and 4 billion years ago, during Earth's early history when the planet had cooled sufficiently to allow liquid water to persist on its surface. The earliest evidence for life comes from chemical signatures in ancient rocks and, more controversially, from possible microbial [[concepts/fossils|fossils]] in stromatolites dating to around 3.5 billion years ago.

## Chemical Origins

The transition from non-living chemistry to living systems likely involved the formation of self-replicating organic molecules in Earth's primordial environment. The famous Miller-Urey experiment of 1952 demonstrated that amino acids—the building blocks of proteins—could form spontaneously from simple inorganic compounds under conditions thought to resemble early Earth. Other hypotheses propose that life may have originated in deep-sea hydrothermal vents, where chemical energy gradients could [[concepts/power|power]] the synthesis of organic molecules. [[concepts/rna|RNA]] is thought to have played a crucial early role, potentially serving as both genetic material and catalyst before the evolution of DNA and proteins.

## Early Life Forms

The earliest known organisms were simple, single-celled prokaryotes, likely similar to modern bacteria and archaea. These microorganisms dominated Earth for roughly the first two billion years of life's history. Photosynthetic bacteria eventually evolved, beginning to release oxygen into the atmosphere around 2.5 billion years ago—a development that fundamentally transformed Earth's chemistry and enabled the later evolution of more complex, oxygen-dependent life forms.
