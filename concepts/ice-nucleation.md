# Ice Nucleation

Ice nucleation is the physical process by which water vapor in the [[concepts/earths-atmosphere|atmosphere]] condenses and freezes into ice crystals, serving as a fundamental mechanism for cloud formation and precipitation. [[concepts/liquid-water|Liquid water]] droplets do not freeze spontaneously at subfreezing temperatures; instead, they require a surface or particle to initiate [[concepts/crystal-formation|crystallization]]. These initiating particles are known as ice nuclei or nucleants. In the absence of such particles, water can remain in a liquid state even at temperatures significantly below 0°C, a phenomenon referred to as supercooling.

The efficiency of ice nucleation depends heavily on the chemical and structural properties of the available nucleants. Natural ice nuclei include mineral dust, soot, and biological particles. Among these, certain fungal proteins act as highly effective ice nucleation sites. These proteins facilitate the freezing of water droplets at temperatures closer to 0°C than many other natural particles, thereby playing a critical role in the initiation of rain and snow formation within atmospheric processes.
