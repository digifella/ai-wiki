# Egg Laying Mammal

Egg-laying [[entities/mammals|mammals]], scientifically classified as monotremes, constitute the most ancient surviving lineage of mammals. Currently, only five extant species exist: the platypus and four species of echidnas. These animals are native exclusively to Australia and nearby regions, including New Guinea. Their [[concepts/evolutionary-lineage|evolutionary history]] diverged from other mammalian groups approximately 160 million years ago during the Jurassic period, predating the evolution of live birth that characterizes all other modern mammalian orders.

The reproductive biology of monotremes distinguishes them from marsupials and placentals. Females lay leathery eggs rather than giving birth to live young, although they do produce milk to nourish their offspring. This combination of reptilian-like reproduction with mammalian [[concepts/thermal-regulation|thermoregulation]] and lactation reflects a unique evolutionary trajectory. Geographic [[concepts/disconnection|isolation]] in Australasia has allowed these species to persist with physiological characteristics that offer critical insights into early [[concepts/mammalian-evolution|mammalian evolution]].

Recent biological discoveries have provided new information regarding the specific traits and genetic makeup of the platypus and echidnas. Studies continue to clarify how these animals maintain their distinct reproductive strategies while adapting to their environments. The monotreme lineage serves as a vital link in understanding the transition from early synapsids to modern mammals, highlighting the diversity of evolutionary paths taken by vertebrates.
