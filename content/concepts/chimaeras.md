# Chimaeras

Chimaeras are a group of [[concepts/cartilaginous-fish|cartilaginous fish]] closely related to sharks and [[concepts/rays|rays]], belonging to the subclass Chimaeromorphi. They have existed since [[concepts/assistive-technology|at]] least the Jurassic period and represent an important lineage in the evolution of jawed vertebrates. While less well-known than sharks, chimaeras provide valuable insights into the diversity of [[concepts/adaptations|adaptations]] that emerged among early marine predators.

## Physical Characteristics and Adaptations

Chimaeras display a [[concepts/range|range]] of unusual morphological features that reflect their specialized ecological roles. Many species possess distinctive tooth plates rather than individual teeth, which they use for crushing hard-shelled prey such as mollusks and crustaceans. Their bodies are typically elongated with large pectoral fins, and some species developed elaborate specialized structures—including tooth-like rostral appendages and venomous fin [[concepts/spines|spines]]—that demonstrate the variety of predatory strategies that evolved within this group during the Paleozoic and Mesozoic eras.

## Evolutionary Significance

The study of chimaeras contributes to broader understanding of [[concepts/shark-evolution|shark evolution]] and the success of cartilaginous fishes as a whole. By examining the fossil record and comparative anatomy of chimaeras alongside sharks and rays, paleontologists can trace the development of innovations in feeding mechanisms, sensory systems, and body plans that allowed these organisms to diversify and dominate marine environments over hundreds of millions of years.
