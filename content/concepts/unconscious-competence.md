# Unconscious Competence

Unconscious competence refers to the stage in skill acquisition where a person or system performs tasks proficiently without conscious deliberation. In the context of AI and [[concepts/expert-systems|expert systems]], this concept becomes particularly relevant when examining how trained models develop and deploy knowledge. The term draws from the [[concepts/dreyfus-model|Dreyfus model]] of skill acquisition, which describes progression through stages from novice to expert, with unconscious competence representing the final, intuitive stage where performance becomes automatic.

## Application to Expert Systems

The [[concepts/training|training]] of models and expert systems in the 1980s and beyond attempted to encode human expertise into computational systems. However, early expert systems struggled to achieve true unconscious competence—the ability to handle novel situations with the fluid, intuitive [[concepts/reasoning|reasoning]] that characterizes human experts. Critics including Gobel and [[entities/chassy|Chassy]] have questioned whether the Dreyfus model's framework adequately explains the limitations encountered in these systems, particularly in their inability to generalize beyond explicitly programmed knowledge domains.

The gap between explicit rule-based knowledge and the tacit, intuitive expertise of human practitioners remains a significant challenge in training AI systems. While models can be optimized to perform specific tasks reliably, replicating the seamless, context-aware competence of experienced humans requires capabilities that extend beyond traditional expert system architectures. This limitation continues to inform contemporary approaches to machine [[concepts/learning|learning]] and AI training methodology.
