# Structural Color

Structural color refers to color produced by the physical structure of a material rather than by pigments or dyes. Unlike pigmentary color, which results from the absorption and reflection of [[concepts/light|light]] by chemical compounds, structural color emerges from the way light interacts with microscopic or nanoscopic patterns, layers, and geometries within a material. These structures manipulate light through interference, diffraction, and scattering, creating colors that can appear vivid and sometimes iridescent. Examples include the shimmering wings of butterflies and birds, the iridescence of oil films, and the metallic sheen of certain beetles.

## Physical Mechanisms

Structural color operates through several optical phenomena. Thin-film interference occurs when light reflects from multiple layered surfaces, causing some wavelengths to reinforce and others to cancel out. Diffraction gratings—regularly spaced microscopic structures—bend light of different wavelengths at different angles. Scattering by nanoparticles can also produce color effects. These [[concepts/causes|mechanisms]] mean that structural colors often shift or change [[concepts/presence|appearance]] depending on viewing angle and lighting conditions, distinguishing them from the stable hues of pigmented materials.

## Lippmann Photography and Structural Color

Gabriel Lippmann's color photography process, developed in 1891, exploited structural color principles to capture true color without pigments or filters. His method used standing waves of light to create interference patterns in a photographic emulsion, effectively recording color information as microscopic layered structures. When viewed, these structures reproduced the original colors through the same interference effects that created them. Though largely superseded by more practical color photography methods, [[concepts/lippmann-photography|Lippmann photography]] demonstrated that color could be encoded and reproduced through physical structure alone, revealing the fundamental relationship between light, structure, and color perception.
