# Shark Teeth

Shark teeth are specialized dental structures that differ fundamentally from mammalian dentition in both attachment and replacement mechanisms. Unlike teeth anchored in bone sockets, shark teeth attach to the jaw via connective tissue fibers, facilitating a continuous cycle of shedding and replacement throughout the animal's lifetime. This modular design has supported successful adaptation across hundreds of millions of years, allowing sharks to rapidly evolve diverse feeding strategies.

The morphological diversity of shark teeth reflects their evolutionary history, particularly during the Golden Age of Shark Evolution. This period saw the emergence of bizarre and highly specialized adaptations, including forms that functioned like buzzsaws, anvils, and spikes. These varied structures enabled sharks to exploit different ecological niches and prey types, contributing to their long-term survival and dominance in marine environments.

Current research continues to examine how these ancient adaptations inform our understanding of shark biology and evolution. The study of shark teeth provides critical insights into the functional morphology of extinct species and the evolutionary pressures that shaped modern shark diversity.
