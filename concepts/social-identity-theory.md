# Social Identity Theory

Social Identity Theory (SIT) is a psychological framework developed by Henri Tajfel and John Turner in the 1970s to explain how individuals derive aspects of their self-concept from membership in social groups. The theory posits that people categorize themselves and others into groups based on characteristics such as ethnicity, nationality, profession, or shared interests. This cognitive process of social categorization influences behavior, attitudes, and intergroup relationships by establishing a distinction between the in-group and out-group.

The theory proposes that individuals are motivated to maintain or enhance a positive social identity. This drive leads to in-group favoritism, where members prefer their own group over others, and out-group discrimination, which may manifest as prejudice or conflict. These dynamics are not necessarily rooted in personal animosity but are often the result of the psychological need for positive self-esteem derived from group membership.

SIT has been extensively applied to understand phenomena such as prejudice, stereotyping, and intergroup conflict. It provides a foundational explanation for how group membership shapes individual behavior and how social structures influence personal identity. The framework remains a central concept in social psychology, sociology, and political science for analyzing collective behavior and group dynamics.
