# 2026 04 12 Feynman Knowing Versus Understanding

Richard Feynman, a theoretical physicist and educator, articulated a distinction between knowing the name or definition of something and genuinely understanding how it works. This idea became central to his philosophy of science and teaching. Feynman argued that much of what passes for knowledge in education and popular discourse amounts to superficial familiarity with terminology—knowing *that* something exists or *what* it is called—rather than comprehending *why* it behaves as it does or *how* its principles connect to observable reality.

## The Problem of Nominal Knowledge

Feynman criticized the tendency to confuse definition with understanding. Knowing that a force is measured in Newtons, for instance, does not constitute understanding force. True understanding requires the ability to predict behavior, explain mechanisms, and connect concepts across domains. He emphasized that if you cannot explain something in simple terms, you probably do not understand it. This principle became known colloquially as the "Feynman Technique" in later popularizations, though Feynman himself presented it as a basic standard for rigorous thinking rather than a novel method.

## Implications for Experimental Science

This distinction relates closely to experimental validation. Feynman held that understanding must ultimately rest on evidence from observation and experiment, not merely on internal logical consistency. A theory might be mathematically elegant or internally coherent yet fail to understand nature if it makes no testable predictions or if experiments contradict it. This stance reflected his broader commitment to empiricism as the arbiter between genuine comprehension and mere intellectual scaffolding.
