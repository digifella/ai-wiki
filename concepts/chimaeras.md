# Chimaeras

Chimaeras are a diverse group of cartilaginous fish belonging to the subclass Chimaeromorphi, making them close relatives of sharks and rays. Like their relatives, chimaeras possess skeletons made entirely of cartilage rather than bone. They have inhabited marine environments since at least the Jurassic period and represent one of the major surviving lineages of jawed vertebrates. Despite their evolutionary significance and an extensive fossil record spanning over 300 million years, chimaeras remain conserved in their basic body plan, often referred to as "living fossils."

## Physiology and Anatomy

Chimaeras exhibit distinct morphological features that separate them from other elasmobranchs. They possess a single gill opening on each side, covered by a fleshy operculum, and lack the exposed gill slits typical of sharks. Their bodies are generally elongated, with a long, whip-like tail that lacks a caudal fin in many species. The skull is heavily calcified, providing structural support, while the teeth are fused into grinding plates adapted for crushing hard-shelled prey such as crustaceans and mollusks.

## Evolution and Classification

As members of the subclass Holocephali, chimaeras diverged from the lineage leading to modern sharks and rays during the Devonian period. Their survival through multiple mass extinction events highlights their ecological resilience. While their external appearance has changed little over millions of years, genetic studies reveal significant diversity within the group, which includes three extant families: Chimaeridae, Rhinochimaeridae, and Callorhinchidae. This diversity is reflected in variations in habitat depth, feeding strategies, and reproductive behaviors.

## Habitat and Distribution

Chimaeras are primarily demersal, inhabiting continental shelves and slopes in temperate and polar waters worldwide. They are absent from most tropical regions, preferring cooler temperatures. Most species are benthic, spending much of their time on or near the sea floor, where they use their specialized teeth to excavate invertebrates from sediment. Some deep-sea species exhibit bioluminescence, likely used for communication or attracting prey in the dark depths of the ocean.
