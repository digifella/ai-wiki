# Interlocking Mechanisms

Interlocking mechanisms are the functional core of zipper systems, enabling two separate rows of teeth or elements to interlock when drawn together by a slider. The mechanism works through precise mechanical geometry: as the slider moves along the tape, it forces teeth or coils from opposing sides into [[entities/contact|contact]], creating a [[concepts/secure|secure]], reversible closure. This fundamental design has remained central to zipper function since the early 20th century, though the specific materials and manufacturing methods have evolved considerably.

## Historical Development

The modern zipper emerged from the "[[entities/clasp-locker|Clasp Locker]]," patented by Whitcomb L. Judson in 1891, which used hook-and-eye [[concepts/buttons|fasteners]]. Gideon Sundback refined this concept into the "Hookless Fastener" in 1913, introducing the interlocking tooth design that became the basis for contemporary zippers. This [[concepts/innovation|innovation]] shifted the mechanism from loose hooks to precisely shaped, interlocking elements that could engage and disengage smoothly under the slider's action.

## YKK and Industrial Standardization

YKK (Yoshida Kogyo Kabushikikaisha), founded in 1934, became the world's dominant zipper manufacturer by perfecting and standardizing interlocking mechanisms across diverse [[concepts/software|applications]]. The company's three-letter acronym appears on the majority of zippers globally because their engineering established reliable specifications for tooth shape, spacing, and material composition. YKK's mechanisms are manufactured using coil, metal, or molded plastic elements, each suited to different durability and aesthetic requirements, from apparel to industrial applications.
