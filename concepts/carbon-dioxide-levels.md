# Carbon Dioxide Levels

Carbon dioxide levels in [[concepts/earths-atmosphere|Earth's atmosphere]] have fluctuated significantly throughout geological history. These variations result from numerous natural processes, including volcanic outgassing, chemical weathering of rocks, photosynthesis by plants and marine organisms, and decomposition of organic matter. Over millions of years, these processes have created cycles of higher and lower atmospheric CO₂ concentrations that correlate with major [[concepts/climate-change|climate shifts]] and changes in ocean chemistry.

## Carboniferous and Permian Periods

During the [[concepts/geologic-epochs|Carboniferous and Permian periods]], atmospheric carbon dioxide levels experienced a dramatic decline. This reduction was driven by the extensive proliferation of land plants, which absorbed vast amounts of CO₂ through photosynthesis. As these plants died, a significant portion of their biomass was buried in swampy environments before it could fully decompose. This burial process prevented the carbon from returning to the atmosphere, leading to a long-term drawdown of atmospheric CO₂.

The low carbon dioxide concentrations during this era contributed to [[concepts/global-cooling|global cooling]] and the expansion of ice sheets. Simultaneously, the accumulation of organic matter in anaerobic conditions laid the foundation for the formation of major fossil fuel deposits, particularly coal. The resulting environmental conditions were characterized by high [[concepts/oxygen-saturation|atmospheric oxygen levels]] and low CO₂, creating a distinct geological epoch that contrasts sharply with modern atmospheric [[concepts/writing|composition]].
