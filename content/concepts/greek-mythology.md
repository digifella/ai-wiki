# Greek Mythology

Greek mythology comprises the collection of myths, deities, and narrative traditions that emerged from ancient Greece, spanning from the Bronze Age through the Classical period and beyond. These stories served multiple functions in Greek society, including religious observance, cultural transmission, and entertainment. The myths were preserved through oral tradition before being recorded in written form by poets and historians such as Homer and Hesiod during the archaic and classical periods.

## Major Deities and Cosmology

The Greek pantheon consisted of twelve Olympian deities—including Zeus, Hera, Poseidon, and Athena—who were believed to reside on Mount Olympus. These gods possessed human characteristics and emotions while wielding supernatural powers over natural phenomena and human affairs. Greek mythology also included numerous lesser deities, spirits, and monsters, as well as cosmological narratives explaining the origin of the gods and the universe.

## Cultural Significance

Greek myths were central to ancient Greek religion, [[concepts/art|art]], literature, and drama. They appeared prominently in theatrical productions, particularly tragedies and comedies performed during religious festivals. The stories provided frameworks for understanding moral concepts, human [[entities/nature|nature]], and the relationship between mortals and divinity. Greek mythology has exerted substantial [[concepts/power|influence]] on Western culture, literature, and art throughout subsequent historical periods, remaining relevant through reinterpretation and adaptation for contemporary audiences.
