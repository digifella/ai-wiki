# Game Theory

Game theory is a mathematical framework for analyzing strategic interactions between rational decision-makers, known as players, who each seek to optimize their own outcomes. Developed formally in the mid-20th century, particularly through the work of John von Neumann and John Nash, game theory models situations where the result for any participant depends not only on their own choices but also on the choices of others. It applies to competitive [[concepts/scenarios|scenarios]] where players have opposing interests, as well as cooperative situations where mutual benefit is possible.

## Core Elements

A game in the theoretical sense consists of a set of players, a collection of possible actions or strategies available to each player, and payoff functions that determine outcomes based on the combination of choices made. Games are typically classified by [[concepts/structure|structure]]: zero-sum games where one player's gain is another's loss, non-zero-sum games where mutual benefit or mutual loss is possible, and games of perfect or imperfect information depending on what players know about others' moves. The Nash equilibrium, a central concept, describes a state where no player can improve their outcome by unilaterally changing strategy given the others' choices.

## Applications

Game theory has applications across economics, political science, biology, and computer science. In economics, it models market competition and auction design. In [[concepts/evolutionary-biology|evolutionary biology]], it explains animal behavior and competition for resources. In political science, it analyzes [[concepts/vote|voting]] systems and international relations. Computational game theory intersects with [[concepts/wikilinkcomputational-complexity-theory|complexity theory]], as determining optimal strategies in certain games relates to fundamental problems in computer science, including questions about computational feasibility.
