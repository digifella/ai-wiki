# Goldbach Conjecture

The Goldbach Conjecture is a prominent unsolved problem in [[concepts/number-theory|number theory]], originally proposed by Christian Goldbach in a 1742 letter to [[entities/leonhard-euler|Leonhard Euler]]. It asserts that every even integer greater than 2 can be expressed as the sum of two [[concepts/prime-numbers|prime numbers]]. Despite its straightforward formulation, the conjecture has resisted proof for nearly three centuries, remaining one of the oldest and most famous open questions in [[concepts/mathematics|mathematics]].

Extensive [[concepts/verification-complexity|computational verification]] supports the validity of the conjecture for [[concepts/enormous-numbers|very large numbers]]. As of recent calculations, the statement has been verified for all even integers up to at least $4 \times 10^{18}$. However, [[concepts/empirical-evidence|empirical evidence]] does not constitute a [[concepts/proof|mathematical proof]], and the lack of a counterexample does not guarantee the conjecture's truth for all integers.

Significant progress has been made toward related results, particularly in the realm of additive number theory. In 1937, Ivan Vinogradov proved the "ternary" Goldbach conjecture, which states that every odd integer greater than 5 can be written as the sum of three primes. The binary version, concerning the sum of two primes, remains unproven. Various approaches involving sieve methods and the circle method have provided partial results, but a complete [[concepts/solution|solution]] continues to elude mathematicians.
