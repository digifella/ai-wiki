## iPhone 18 Pro Max Astrophotography: Evaluating Variable Aperture Performance
**Clip title:** [[entities/iphone|iPhone]] 18PM vs The [[concepts/night-sky|Night Sky]]
**[[entities/tasia-custode|Author]] / channel:** Shayne Mostyn - Mobile Photography
**URL:** https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=Xt19nAGSLio

### Summary
The video, hosted by [[entities/shayne-mostyn|Shayne Mostyn]], delves into the evolution of [[concepts/astrophotography|astrophotography]] using iPhones, culminating in a hands-on test of the (hypothetical) [[concepts/iphone-18-pro|iPhone 18 Pro]] Max. Mostyn begins by humorously referencing his journey from testing the [[entities/iphone-11-pro|iPhone 11 Pro]]'s initial [[concepts/night-mode|Night Mode]]—a "pretty exciting" introduction—through the subsequent 12, 13, 14 (which significantly improved after an iOS update), and 15 Pro Max models. Each [[concepts/iteration|iteration]] brought noticeable enhancements, with the [[entities/iphone-15-pro-max|iPhone 15 Pro Max]] previously lauded for its "mint" astrophotography capabilities. The [[entities/iphone-18-pro-max|iPhone 18 Pro Max]] is presented as a pivotal moment, as [[entities/apple|Apple]] has now officially embraced and promoted astrophotography as a key feature.

Central to the iPhone 18 Pro Max's advancements are its rumored larger sensor, promising better low-[[concepts/light|light]] performance, and a new variable [[concepts/aperture|aperture]]. Historically, iPhones operated with a wide-open [[concepts/aperture|aperture]] (around f/1.8), but the 18 Pro Max allows users to select between f/1.48, f/1.8, f/2.8, and f/4. Mostyn explains that the iPhone's computational photography excels by taking multiple 10-second exposures within a 30-second capture, intelligently focusing and exposing across these shots to ensure [[concepts/sharpness|sharpness]] in both foreground and distant [[concepts/stellar-objects|stars]]. Due to unexpected cloudy weather affecting his planned rock outcrop [[concepts/writing|composition]], Mostyn adapted by testing the different aperture settings on a tripod with a subtly lit foreground tree, aiming to evaluate the new model's low-[[concepts/light|light]] and focusing capabilities.

The comparative results from the different aperture settings revealed distinct visual characteristics. The f/1.48 setting captured the most [[concepts/stellar-objects|stars]] and detail but exhibited some "artifacting" or [[concepts/digital-image-noise|digital noise]] in the sky. Mostyn identified f/1.8 as the "sweet spot," providing a more natural [[concepts/presence|appearance]] with good detail and less noticeable digital artifacts. As the aperture closed further to f/2.8 and f/4, the images progressively darkened and lost significant detail, reminiscent of older iPhone models like the 11 Pro. Post-processing in [[concepts/adobe|Adobe]] [[concepts/lightroom|Lightroom]] (a process also achievable on mobile) involved [[concepts/layer-masks|masking]] the sky and applying minor dehaze and [[concepts/clarity-slider|clarity]] [[concepts/adjustments|adjustments]] to enhance the raw images.

In conclusion, Mostyn declares the iPhone 18 Pro Max a substantial leap forward for mobile astrophotography, surpassing the iPhone 15's performance. He highlights that while professional cameras offer ultimate flexibility, the iPhone's computational photography makes [[concepts/night-sky|night sky]] photography remarkably accessible and effective, even negating the need for external shutter releases due to its internal processing. The video reinforces that the iPhone 18 Pro Max is not just a casual tool but a capable device for capturing impressive astrophotos, allowing users to delve into this rewarding genre of photography without needing specialized, expensive gear.
