# Baryonic Matter

Baryonic matter comprises all ordinary matter in the universe composed of atoms and molecules, including [[concepts/stellar-objects|stars]], planets, gas clouds, and dust. It is the only form of matter that interacts electromagnetically and is directly observable through [[concepts/light|light]] emission and absorption. According to current cosmological models, baryonic matter represents approximately 5% of the universe's total mass-[[concepts/calorie-count|energy content]], while [[concepts/dark-matter|dark matter]] accounts for roughly 27% and [[concepts/the-composition-of-the-universe-extends-beyond-what-is-directly-observable|dark energy]] comprises the remainder.

## Distribution in Galaxy Clusters

Recent astronomical observations have revealed unexpected discrepancies in the distribution of baryonic matter within [[concepts/galaxy-clusters|galaxy clusters]]. Measurements of the hot intracluster gas, which constitutes the majority of the visible baryonic mass, often fall short of predictions derived from gravitational lensing and X-ray data. This deficit suggests that a significant portion of ordinary matter may be in a diffuse, hard-to-detect [[concepts/phase|phase]] or has been expelled from cluster centers by [[concepts/feedback|feedback]] [[concepts/causes|mechanisms]] from [[concepts/active-galactic-nuclei|active galactic nuclei]].

These findings present challenges to current dark matter [[concepts/theory|theory]], as they complicate the precise mapping of mass distributions required to validate dark matter models. Resolving the "missing baryon" problem in clusters is critical for refining cosmological parameters and understanding the baryon cycle, which governs the [[concepts/flow|flow]] of ordinary matter between galaxies and the intergalactic [[entities/medium|medium]].
