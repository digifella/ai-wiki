## Myosin Development: Mastering Burger, Sausage, Meatball Textures

### Summary
This video from [[entities/cooks-illustrated|Cook's Illustrated]] "Techniquely" series explains the crucial role of [[concepts/myosin-development|myosin development]] in mastering various [[concepts/ground-meat|ground meat]] preparations, from tender burgers to snappy sausages and moist meatballs. The main topic is how controlling the [[concepts/deployment|release]] and activation of myosin, a protein found in [[concepts/muscle-fibers|muscle fibers]], directly impacts the final [[concepts/texture-slider|texture]] and quality of ground meat dishes.

The video begins by highlighting the common issue of tough, unappetizing burgers made from store-bought ground beef and introduces myosin as the key to understanding and solving this problem. Myosin is described as having "spikes" that act like Velcro, allowing it to bind to water, fat, and other proteins once freed from muscle fibers during cutting or grinding. The level of "myosin development" – how much myosin is extracted and activated – dictates the [[concepts/texture-slider|texture]] of the cooked product. The video then demonstrates three distinct levels of myosin development, tailored for different culinary applications.

For *minimal myosin development*, ideal for tender burgers, the process involves starting with [[concepts/lean|lean]] beef sirloin, cutting it into small pieces, partially freezing it, and then pulsing it gently in a food [[concepts/cpu|processor]] in small batches. This careful handling minimizes the breaking of muscle fibers and the [[concepts/deployment|release]] of myosin, resulting in a burger that remains tender and juicy without buckling during cooking. This method contrasts sharply with over-processed store-bought ground beef, which has excessive myosin development, leading to a tough texture and the common need to dimple burger patties.

*Moderate myosin development* is shown to be essential for dishes like Italian bulk sausage, which requires a firm, snappy texture. Here, pork butt is seasoned with salt (which aids [[concepts/whirlpool-technique|myosin extraction]]), rested, partially frozen, and then put through a traditional meat grinder. The grinding process is more aggressive than food processing for burgers, and the mixture is kept very cold to prevent fat from melting due to [[concepts/friction|friction]]. Further vigorous mixing (folding and smearing) in a bowl helps to activate more myosin, creating the desired springy [[concepts/logical-consistency|consistency]] characteristic of good sausage and various Asian dumplings.

Finally, *maximal myosin development* is demonstrated with Lion's Head Meatballs, where a melt-in-your-mouth tender and moist texture is desired. Store-bought ground pork (already twice-ground for high myosin content) is combined with salt, [[concepts/baking-soda|baking soda]], water, and various flavorings, then aggressively mixed in a stand mixer. This intense mixing lightens the mixture's color, making it tacky and stiff, signifying significant myosin development. The high amount of activated myosin acts as an emulsifier, binding all the added water and fat within the meatball, ensuring it remains incredibly tender, moist, and juicy even after extended simmering, and holds its shape perfectly.

In conclusion, the video effectively illustrates that mastering ground meat dishes is not just about ingredients but fundamentally about understanding and controlling myosin development. Whether aiming for tenderness, a snappy chew, or a moist, cohesive structure, consciously manipulating the meat to achieve the proper level of myosin development is critical for culinary [[concepts/success|success]] across a wide range of recipes.
