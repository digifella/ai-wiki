# Positron

A positron is the [[concepts/antimatter|antimatter]] counterpart of the electron. It possesses an identical mass to the electron (approximately 9.11 × 10⁻³¹ kilograms) but carries a positive electrical charge of +e, opposite to the electron's negative charge. When a positron encounters an electron, the two particles annihilate each other, converting their combined mass into energy in accordance with Einstein's mass-energy equivalence formula.

## Discovery and Properties

The positron was discovered in 1932 by physicist [[entities/carl-anderson|Carl Anderson]] through his analysis of cosmic ray interactions in a cloud chamber. This discovery provided experimental confirmation of Paul Dirac's theoretical prediction that antimatter particles should exist. The positron was the first known antiparticle and remains one of the most well-studied antimatter particles.

## Applications

Positrons have practical applications in medical imaging, most notably in positron emission tomography (PET) scans. In this technique, a radioactive tracer that emits positrons is introduced into a patient's body. The positrons quickly annihilate with electrons in nearby tissue, producing [[concepts/gamma-rays|gamma rays]] that are detected to create detailed diagnostic [[concepts/images|images]]. Positrons are also used in materials science research and in fundamental [[concepts/physics|physics]] experiments investigating the [[entities/nature|nature]] of antimatter.
