# Color Theory

Color theory is the practical and scientific framework that explains how colors interact, combine, and affect perception in creative work. In [[concepts/visual-arts|visual arts]], photography, and design, understanding color theory enables practitioners to make deliberate choices about composition, mood, and [[concepts/visual-communication|visual communication]]. The field draws from both [[concepts/physics|physics]]—how light wavelengths produce color—and psychology—how humans perceive and respond to different hues.

## Core Principles

The fundamental elements of color theory include hue (the color itself), saturation ([[concepts/color-saturation|color intensity]]), and value (lightness or darkness). Color [[concepts/relationships|relationships]] are organized through models like the color wheel, which shows how primary, secondary, and tertiary colors relate to one another. Complementary colors sit opposite each other on the wheel and create [[concepts/contrast|contrast]], while analogous colors sit adjacent and create [[concepts/musical-harmony|harmony]]. These relationships form the basis for developing coherent color palettes in creative work.

## Application in Practice

Photographers and visual artists apply color theory to guide viewer [[concepts/attention-mechanisms|attention]], establish emotional tone, and create visual balance. Warm colors (reds, oranges, yellows) tend to feel energetic or intimate, while cool colors (blues, greens, purples) often suggest calm or distance. The saturation and value of colors work together to create depth and [[concepts/hierarchy|hierarchy]] within a composition. Effective [[concepts/color-palette-control|color palette control]]—the deliberate selection and orchestration of colors within a work—depends on understanding these underlying principles.
