# Desalination Plants

Desalination plants are industrial facilities that remove salt and dissolved minerals from seawater or brackish water to produce freshwater. This process is essential in regions where conventional freshwater sources—rivers, lakes, and groundwater aquifers—are limited, depleted, or contaminated. Desalination has become critical infrastructure in arid and semi-arid climates, as well as in densely populated coastal areas facing water scarcity.

## Primary Technologies

The two dominant desalination methods are reverse osmosis (RO) and thermal distillation. Reverse osmosis forces seawater through a semi-permeable membrane under high pressure, separating salt from freshwater. Thermal distillation heats seawater to produce vapor, which is then condensed into freshwater. Multi-effect distillation (MED) and reverse osmosis dominate global capacity, though newer hybrid systems combining both technologies are increasingly deployed.

## Challenges and Environmental Considerations

Desalination plants consume significant energy, making their operation costly and carbon-intensive unless powered by renewable sources. They also produce brine—a concentrated salt byproduct—which poses disposal challenges and can harm marine ecosystems if released without treatment. The upfront capital costs are substantial, limiting adoption in economically disadvantaged regions despite their water security benefits. Maintenance of membranes and equipment requires specialized expertise and ongoing investment.

## Global Distribution and Use

Major desalination capacity exists in the Middle East, North Africa, and increasingly in East Asia and the Americas. The technology serves municipal water supplies, agricultural irrigation, and industrial processes. As freshwater demand grows and climate change intensifies drought conditions in vulnerable regions, desalination capacity is expected to expand, though its sustainability depends on coupling it with renewable energy and improved brine management solutions.
