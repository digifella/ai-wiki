# Caldera

A caldera is a large volcanic depression formed when a volcano's summit collapses inward following a major eruption or the withdrawal of magma from the underlying chamber. Unlike the relatively small crater found at a volcano's peak, a caldera can span many kilometers across and extend hundreds of meters or more in depth. These features represent some of the most dramatic topographic changes created by volcanic activity, often resulting in significant alterations to the landscape.

## Formation

Calderas form through subsidence when the support for the roof of a magma chamber is removed. This typically occurs after a massive eruption empties the chamber, causing the overlying rock to collapse under its own weight. In some cases, gradual withdrawal of magma without a catastrophic eruption can also lead to this structural failure. The resulting depression is significantly larger than the vent that produced the eruptive material.

## Classification and Examples

Calderas are generally classified based on their formation mechanism, such as collapse calderas formed by roof subsidence or explosion calderas created by violent steam-driven eruptions. Notable examples include Yellowstone Caldera in the United States, which is one of the largest active calderas on Earth, and Santorini in Greece, formed by the Minoan eruption. These structures often become sites for subsequent volcanic activity, including the formation of lava domes or new vents within the depression.
