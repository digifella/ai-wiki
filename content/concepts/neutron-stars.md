# Neutron Stars

Neutron stars are extremely dense remnants left behind after massive stars explode as [[concepts/supernovae|supernovae]]. Composed primarily of neutrons, these objects pack the mass of the Sun into a sphere roughly the size of a city, giving them densities that exceed any terrestrial material. They are among the most extreme objects in the universe, with surface [[concepts/gravitational-pull|gravity]] so intense that a teaspoon of neutron star material would weigh as much as a mountain on Earth.

## Detection and Observation

Neutron stars are typically detected through their emission of [[concepts/light|electromagnetic radiation]], particularly as pulsars—rapidly rotating neutron stars that emit beams of radiation like cosmic lighthouses. In recent years, advanced gravitational wave detectors like LIGO have opened a new observational window, allowing astronomers to detect collisions and mergers between neutron stars and other compact objects by measuring ripples in spacetime itself.

## Distinguishing from Black Holes

While neutron stars and black holes are both endpoints of [[concepts/stellar-evolution|stellar evolution]], they represent different physical regimes. Gravitational wave observations have proven particularly valuable in distinguishing between these objects, as signals from neutron star mergers show characteristic features distinct from black hole collisions. The detection of sub-solar mass compact objects through [[concepts/gravitational-waves|gravitational waves]] has raised questions about whether some observed events involve [[concepts/primordial-black-hole|primordial black holes]]—hypothetical black holes formed in the [[concepts/early-universe|early universe]]—rather than neutron stars or stellar-mass black holes.
