# Satellite Communication

Satellite communication facilitates global connectivity by relaying radio signals between ground-based transmitters and orbiting satellites, operating independently of terrestrial infrastructure. This architecture enables telecommunications and broadcasting in regions where traditional network deployment is impractical or impossible. The system relies on a network of satellites in various orbital regimes to maintain continuous coverage, allowing data, voice, and video transmission across vast geographic distances with minimal latency compared to earlier generations of geostationary systems.

Modern implementations, such as SpaceX's Starlink, utilize Low Earth Orbit (LEO) constellations to reduce signal propagation delay. The technical evolution of these systems has progressed through distinct hardware iterations, moving from the initial V1.5 configuration to the more advanced V3 satellites. These upgrades involve significant changes in antenna design, laser inter-satellite links, and orbital mechanics to enhance bandwidth capacity and network resilience.

The underlying physics of satellite communication involves managing signal attenuation, Doppler shifts, and atmospheric interference. As satellite density increases, orbital architecture becomes critical to prevent collisions and manage spectrum allocation. The shift from single high-orbit satellites to massive LEO constellations represents a fundamental change in how global internet infrastructure is constructed, prioritizing low-latency performance over the wide-area coverage characteristics of traditional systems.
