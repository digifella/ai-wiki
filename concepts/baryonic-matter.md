# Baryonic Matter

Baryonic matter comprises all ordinary matter in the universe composed of atoms and molecules, including stars, planets, gas clouds, and dust. It is the only form of matter that interacts electromagnetically and is directly observable through light emission and absorption. According to current cosmological models, baryonic matter represents approximately 5% of the universe's total mass-energy content, while dark matter accounts for roughly 27% and dark energy makes up the remaining 68%.

In the context of galaxy clusters, the distribution and quantity of baryonic matter are critical for testing gravitational theories. Recent observations suggest that the amount of baryonic matter in these clusters may be underestimated, presenting challenges to current dark matter theory. This discrepancy highlights the complexity of mapping visible matter against the inferred gravitational effects attributed to dark matter.

The study of baryonic matter in large-scale structures remains a focal point for cosmologists seeking to refine models of the universe's composition. By improving the accuracy of baryonic mass measurements, researchers aim to resolve tensions between observational data and theoretical predictions regarding the role of dark matter in cosmic evolution.
