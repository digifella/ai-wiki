# Usb 20

USB 2.0 is a standardized interface for data and power transfer between computers and peripheral devices. Released in 2000, it succeeded USB 1.1 and offered substantially improved performance, supporting data transfer rates up to 480 Mbps. This represented a significant advancement that enabled practical use of higher-bandwidth devices like external hard drives and digital cameras. USB 2.0 became the dominant connection standard throughout the 2000s and 2010s, used for keyboards, mice, printers, and countless other peripherals.

## Technical Specifications

USB 2.0 operates over a four-wire cable configuration, utilizing differential signaling for high-speed data transmission. The standard defines three primary transfer modes: Low Speed (1.5 Mbps), Full Speed (12 Mbps), and High Speed (480 Mbps). It supports up to 127 devices on a single bus through the use of hubs, allowing for extensive peripheral expansion. The specification also outlines power delivery capabilities, typically providing 5 volts at up to 500 milliamps per port, which was sufficient for most consumer electronics of its era.

## Compatibility and Evolution

While USB 2.0 established the physical connector form factors (Type-A, Type-B, Mini-A/B, and Micro-A/B) that remained prevalent for decades, it has largely been superseded by USB 3.x standards for high-performance applications. Modern devices often utilize USB-C connectors, which are physically reversible and support multiple protocols, including USB 2.0 signaling for backward compatibility. However, mixing USB 2.0 peripherals with newer hosts or vice versa can sometimes lead to confusion regarding speed limitations and power delivery expectations, necessitating careful attention to cable and port specifications.
