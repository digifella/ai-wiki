# Distant Galaxies

Distant galaxies are galaxies located billions of light-years from Earth, observable only through powerful telescopes. Their remoteness makes them valuable subjects for cosmological research, as observing them allows astronomers to study the universe as it existed in its early periods. The light reaching Earth from these galaxies has traveled for billions of years, providing a direct view of conditions shortly after the Big Bang. Because of the finite speed of light, astronomers effectively observe the distant past when they look at remote galaxies.

## Observation and Detection

The James Webb Space Telescope (JWST) has significantly advanced the detection and study of distant galaxies since its deployment. By observing in infrared wavelengths, JWST can detect galaxies that formed within the first few hundred million years after the Big Bang, pushing back the boundaries of what was previously observable. Earlier observations from ground-based telescopes and the Hubble Space Telescope identified distant galaxies, but JWST's enhanced sensitivity has revealed even more remote objects and provided clearer data about their properties.

## Scientific Significance

Studying distant galaxies contributes to understanding galaxy formation, evolution, and the large-scale structure of the universe. Observations of these ancient galaxies help astronomers test cosmological models and constrain parameters about the universe's age, composition, and expansion history. The chemical composition, star formation rates, and structural characteristics of distant galaxies reveal how galaxies have developed over cosmic time.
