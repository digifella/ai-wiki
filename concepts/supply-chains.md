# Supply Chains

Supply chains are the networks of organizations, people, activities, and resources involved in delivering products or services from production to consumption. They encompass the procurement of raw materials, manufacturing, logistics, distribution, and retail, often spanning multiple countries and involving complex [[concepts/coordination|coordination]] between suppliers, manufacturers, and retailers. Modern supply chains have become increasingly globalized over the past several decades, with companies sourcing components and labor from wherever costs are lowest, a strategy enabled by advances in transportation and communication technologies.

The structure of these networks is typically characterized by a tiered system of suppliers. Tier 1 suppliers provide components directly to the original equipment manufacturer, while Tier 2 and Tier 3 suppliers provide materials to those upstream partners. This hierarchical arrangement allows for specialized efficiency but also creates dependencies that can be vulnerable to disruptions. The globalization of these chains has historically prioritized [[concepts/expenditure-reduction|cost reduction]] and just-in-time [[concepts/inventory-management|inventory management]] to minimize holding costs and maximize capital efficiency.

Recent developments in supply chain management have focused on [[concepts/resilience|resilience]] and [[concepts/risk-mitigation|risk mitigation]]. The [[concepts/four-year-experiment|four-year experiment]] investigating whether a product can be manufactured entirely in [[entities/america|America]] and remain competitive in the marketplace highlights the tension between [[concepts/cost-efficiency|cost efficiency]] and geographic concentration. Such initiatives examine the feasibility of reshoring production to reduce [[concepts/exposure|exposure]] to international geopolitical conflicts, trade barriers, and logistical delays. These efforts aim to determine if [[concepts/domestic-production|domestic manufacturing]] can achieve price parity with globalized alternatives while maintaining quality and [[concepts/speed|speed]] to market.

The political and [[concepts/economic-implications|economic implications]] of supply chain configuration are significant. Governments increasingly view supply chains as matters of [[concepts/export-control|national security]], leading to [[concepts/policies|policies]] that encourage domestic production or diversification of sources away from single-point dependencies. This shift influences trade agreements, industrial policy, and [[concepts/enterprise-strategy|corporate strategy]]. As companies reassess their global footprints, the balance between [[concepts/lean|lean]] efficiency and robust redundancy remains a central challenge in modern supply chain design.
