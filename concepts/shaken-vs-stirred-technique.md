# Shaken Vs Stirred Technique

The choice between shaking and [[concepts/stirring-technique|stirring]] a martini produces measurably different results in temperature, dilution, and [[concepts/texture-slider|texture]]. Shaking with ice rapidly cools the cocktail while introducing small air bubbles and faster dilution from melting ice, resulting in a slightly cloudy [[concepts/presence|appearance]] and lighter mouthfeel. Stirring in a mixing glass achieves a gentler chill with more controlled dilution, producing a clearer drink with a smoother, silkier [[concepts/logical-consistency|consistency]].

## Temperature and Dilution

Shaking cools a cocktail more quickly than stirring, typically reaching the desired temperature in 10-15 seconds. This rapid chilling comes at the cost of faster ice melt and greater [[concepts/moisture-content|water content]] in the final drink. Stirring requires more time—usually 20-30 seconds—but allows the bartender to control exactly how much dilution occurs, as ice melts more slowly in a stationary mixing glass.

## Visual and Sensory Differences

The physical agitation of shaking incorporates air into the cocktail, creating a foamy layer and a slightly opaque appearance. Stirred martinis remain clear and luminous. On the palate, shaken martinis feel lighter and more effervescent, while stirred versions present a more refined, velvety [[concepts/texture|texture]] that many consider more suitable to a traditional martini's formal presentation.

## The James Bond Reference

[[entities/james-bond|James Bond]]'s famous instruction to "shake, not stir" his martini has become cultural shorthand for the technique, though cocktail professionals traditionally reserve shaking for drinks containing citrus, cream, or eggs, and stirring for spirit-forward cocktails like the martini. Bond's preference reflects a stylistic choice rather than an adherence to bartending convention.
