# Muon G-2

The [[concepts/muon-g-2-experiment|Muon g-2 experiment]] is a precision [[concepts/physics|physics]] measurement conducted [[concepts/assistive-technology|at]] Fermilab that measures the [[concepts/anomalous-magnetic-moment|anomalous magnetic moment]] of the [[concepts/muon|muon]], a fundamental particle similar to an electron but approximately 200 times more massive. The experiment involves storing muons in a ring-shaped magnetic field and observing how their spin precesses relative to their orbital motion. Any deviation from theoretical predictions calculated using the [[concepts/standard-model-of-particle-physics|Standard Model of particle physics]] could indicate the presence of new, undiscovered particles or interactions.

## Experimental Method

Muons are generated by firing a proton beam at a target, creating pions that decay into muons. These muons are then injected into a [[entities/storage|storage]] ring where a precisely calibrated magnetic field causes them to circulate. Sensitive detectors track the decay of muons and measure their spin orientation as they orbit. The ratio of the spin precession frequency to the orbital frequency—expressed as the [[concepts/g-factor|g-factor]]—is determined with extraordinary precision, currently to parts per billion.

## Significance and Results

The Muon [[concepts/muons|g-2 measurement]] has long been one of the most stringent tests of the [[concepts/standard-model|Standard Model]]. Previous experiments at Brookhaven National Laboratory, conducted over two decades, found a persistent discrepancy between the measured value and theoretical predictions. Fermilab's ongoing measurements aim to confirm or clarify this discrepancy. If verified, such a difference would suggest physics beyond the Standard Model and could provide clues about [[concepts/dark-matter|dark matter]] or other unknown phenomena.
