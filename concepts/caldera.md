# Caldera

A caldera is a large volcanic [[concepts/depression|depression]] formed when a volcano's summit collapses inward following a major eruption or the withdrawal of magma from the underlying chamber. Unlike the relatively small crater found at a volcano's peak, a caldera can span many kilometers across and extend hundreds of meters or more in depth. These features represent some of the most dramatic topographic changes created by [[concepts/volcanic-eruption|volcanic activity]], often resulting in significant alterations to the landscape.

## Formation

Calderas form through subsidence when the structural support provided by magma in the chamber is removed. During large eruptions, magma is expelled rapidly, causing the overlying rock to lose its buoyant support. This loss of [[concepts/pressure|pressure]] leads to the collapse of the volcano's roof into the emptied or partially emptied [[concepts/magma-chamber|magma chamber]]. The resulting depression is typically circular or oval in shape and can be several kilometers in diameter.

## Classification and Examples

Calderas are generally classified based on their formation mechanism and size. Some form during explosive eruptions that eject vast amounts of material, while others develop through gradual magma withdrawal without significant explosive activity. Notable examples include Yellowstone Caldera in the [[entities/united-states|United States]], which is part of a massive supervolcanic system, and Santorini in Greece, formed by the Minoan eruption. These structures often become sites for subsequent volcanic activity, including the formation of [[concepts/lava-flows|lava]] domes or new craters within the depression.
