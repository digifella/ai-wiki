# Coal Formation

Coal is a combustible sedimentary rock composed primarily of carbon, along with variable quantities of other elements such as hydrogen, sulfur, oxygen, and nitrogen. It originates from the accumulation of plant debris in ancient swampy environments, where anaerobic conditions prevented complete decomposition. Over geological timescales, these organic deposits were buried under successive layers of sediment, initiating a transformation process known as coalification.

The coalification process involves the progressive alteration of plant material into distinct ranks of coal: peat, lignite, bituminous coal, and anthracite. As overlying sediments accumulate, increasing [[concepts/pressure|pressure]] and geothermal heat [[concepts/ambition|drive]] off volatile compounds and water. This compaction and heating concentrate the carbon content, resulting in denser and more energy-dense forms of coal. The specific rank achieved depends on the duration and intensity of the heat and pressure applied during burial.

The formation of significant coal and oil deposits is linked to periods of high [[concepts/oxygen-saturation|atmospheric oxygen levels]]. This oxygenation is associated with the [[concepts/rock-oxidation|oxidation of rocks]], a process notably observed in the geological history of the red center of Australia. The availability of oxygen during these periods facilitated the [[concepts/preservation|preservation]] of vast amounts of organic matter, which eventually became the fossil fuel reserves utilized in modern energy systems.
