# EU Chips Act 1.0

The EU Chips Act is a legislative framework adopted by the [[concepts/european-union|European Union]] to bolster its semiconductor ecosystem, aiming to double the EU's share of global production by 2030 and mitigate [[concepts/pandemic-supply-chain-disruption|supply chain vulnerabilities]] exposed during the global chip shortage. The act provides financial support for R&D, manufacturing, and design capabilities across member states.

## Strategic Objectives
- **[[concepts/resilience|Resilience]]:** Reduce dependence on non-EU manufacturers (primarily Asian) for critical semiconductor supplies.
- **[[concepts/innovation|Innovation]]:** Foster a competitive domestic industry in advanced [[concepts/nodes|nodes]] and mature technologies.
- **[[concepts/coordination|Coordination]]:** Align national subsidy schemes with EU state aid rules to prevent fragmentation.

## Implementation Status & Assessment
Initial implementations faced bureaucratic delays and lower-than-expected private sector uptake compared to US CHIPS and [[concepts/science|Science]] Act incentives. By mid-2026, significant gaps remained between targets and actual capacity additions.

### Critical Failures (2026 Review)
See detailed analysis in [[lab-notes/2026-06-08-EU-Chips-Act-1.0-Critical-Assessment-of-Strategy-and-Imp|EU Chips Act 1.0: Critical Assessment of Strategy and Implementation Failures]].

- **Strategic Misalignment:** The act failed to account for the [[concepts/speed|speed]] of Asian competitor expansion, particularly in mature nodes where [[entities/europe|Europe]] lacks natural advantages.
- **Bureaucratic [[concepts/inertia|Inertia]]:** Complex approval processes deterred agile private investment, allowing US and Chinese incentives to capture talent and capital more effectively.
- **Scale Deficit:** Subsidy amounts were insufficient to attract anchor tenants for mega-fabs without unprecedented national co-financing, which remained politically contentious in several member states.
- **Market Distortion Concerns:** Fear of distorting the single market hindered decisive action, leading to a "lowest common denominator" approach that satisfied no major industrial player.
