# Biological Immortality

Biological immortality refers to the theoretical absence of senescence—the progressive biological decline associated with aging—in living organisms. An organism possessing this trait would not [[concepts/experience|experience]] age-related functional deterioration and could [[entities/theoretically-media|theoretically]] live indefinitely, provided it avoids death from external causes such as injury, disease, predation, or [[concepts/environmental-risks|environmental hazards]]. This concept differs significantly from popular conceptions of immortality, which often imply invulnerability to all forms of death.

Several species demonstrate negligible senescence under controlled conditions. Notable examples include the hydra, certain jellyfish species like *Turritopsis dohrnii* which can revert to their polyp stage, and the bristlecone pine. These organisms exhibit [[concepts/causes|mechanisms]] that allow them to maintain cellular [[concepts/honesty|integrity]] and reproductive capacity without the typical signs of aging observed in most [[entities/mammals|mammals]].

Research into the limits of such traits in mammals has yielded cautionary insights. A 20-year Japanese study on mammalian [[concepts/serial-cloning|serial cloning]] concluded that the process leads to a genetic dead end. The findings suggest that while biological immortality may be achievable in specific contexts or species, the mechanisms sustaining it are not easily replicable or sustainable across generations in complex mammals without significant genetic degradation.
