# Plant Life

During the Carboniferous and Permian periods, approximately 360 to 250 million years ago, vast swamps and dense forests covered significant portions of Earth's landmass. This era witnessed the proliferation of extensive plant life, including early ferns, horsetails, and seed ferns, which thrived in the warm, humid climates. The sheer volume of this biomass created an unprecedented concentration of organic carbon within the geological record, setting the stage for major geochemical shifts.

The decomposition and subsequent burial of this extensive plant material, alongside marine organisms, led to the formation of vast coal deposits. This process significantly altered global atmospheric composition by sequestering large amounts of carbon dioxide while releasing oxygen. Consequently, atmospheric oxygen levels rose to peaks not seen before or since, while carbon dioxide concentrations dropped, contributing to global cooling and the eventual glaciation events of the late Paleozoic.

These geochemical changes had profound implications for terrestrial ecosystems and atmospheric chemistry. The high oxygen levels supported the evolution of giant arthropods and influenced the metabolic rates of early tetrapods. As the Permian period progressed, climatic shifts and tectonic activity reduced the extent of swampy habitats, leading to a decline in the specific plant communities that had dominated the Carboniferous and a gradual normalization of atmospheric gas levels.
