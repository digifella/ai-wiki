# Gandhi

Mohandas Karamchand Gandhi (1869–1948) was an Indian political and spiritual leader who pioneered nonviolent resistance as a method for achieving independence and social reform. Born in Gujarat, he studied law in [[entities/united-kingdom|England]] and practiced as a barrister in [[entities/south-africa|South Africa]] from 1893 to 1915. During his two decades in South Africa, Gandhi developed *satyagraha* (literally "truth-force"), a [[concepts/philosophy|philosophy]] of nonviolent civil disobedience formulated in response to racial discrimination against Indian immigrants.

Upon returning to India in 1915, Gandhi became a central figure in the Indian independence movement against British rule. He organized mass campaigns of non-cooperation, boycotts, and peaceful protests, most notably the 1930 Salt March, which challenged the British salt monopoly. His leadership emphasized self-reliance (*swaraj*) and the unity of India's diverse religious and social groups, mobilizing millions of Indians across class and caste lines.

Gandhi's influence extended beyond politics into social reform, where he campaigned against the caste system's "untouchability" and promoted Hindu-Muslim unity. He advocated for the revival of traditional Indian crafts and simple living as a means of economic and moral regeneration. Despite his efforts to maintain communal harmony, the partition of India in 1947 led to widespread violence, which Gandhi viewed as a personal failure.

Gandhi was assassinated in January 1948 by Nathuram Godse, a Hindu nationalist who opposed Gandhi's tolerance toward Muslims. His legacy as a proponent of nonviolent resistance has inspired civil rights movements and anti-colonial struggles worldwide, including those led by Martin Luther King Jr. and Nelson Mandela. He remains a global symbol of peace and ethical political action.
