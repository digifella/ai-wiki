# Dry Vermouth

Dry vermouth is a type of aromatized, fortified wine characterized by its crisp, bitter-herbal profile and minimal residual [[concepts/sugar|sugar]] content, typically less than 4 grams per liter. It is produced by adding distilled spirits to a neutral white wine base, which raises the alcohol content to approximately 18–20% by volume. This fortification process stabilizes the wine and serves as a solvent for the subsequent infusion of botanicals.

The defining flavor profile is achieved through the infusion of a complex blend of botanicals, herbs, and spices. Key ingredients commonly include wormwood, which gives vermouth its name, along with anise, citrus peel, and various other aromatic plants. The specific combination and proportion of these ingredients vary by producer, creating distinct regional and brand-specific characteristics while maintaining the overall dry [[concepts/style|style]].

Historically, dry vermouth emerged in the early 19th century in Turin, [[entities/italy|Italy]], and later gained prominence in France, particularly in Chambéry. It became a staple in cocktail culture, serving as a primary component in classic drinks such as the Martini and the Manhattan. Its low sugar content and high acidity make it a versatile mixer that balances the strength of spirits without adding significant sweetness.
