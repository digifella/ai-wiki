# Theory

In physics, a theory constitutes a comprehensive, systematic framework of ideas, principles, and mathematical relationships designed to explain and predict natural phenomena. It represents the highest level of scientific explanation, distinguished from casual speculation or preliminary hypotheses by its extensive empirical support and rigorous testing. A valid physical theory must demonstrate the ability to account for diverse observations and experimental results, providing a coherent structure that unifies previously disparate facts.

The development of physical theories follows an iterative process involving hypothesis formation, mathematical modeling, and experimental verification. Unlike laws, which often describe specific relationships under defined conditions, theories provide broader explanatory contexts that can encompass multiple laws. For instance, the theory of general relativity explains gravity not merely as a force but as the curvature of spacetime, thereby unifying Newtonian mechanics with astronomical observations that classical physics could not fully address.

Theories are subject to continuous refinement and, in some cases, replacement when new evidence contradicts existing predictions. However, established theories are rarely discarded entirely; instead, they are often incorporated as limiting cases within more general frameworks. The transition from classical mechanics to quantum mechanics illustrates this progression, where the former remains valid for macroscopic scales while the latter provides the necessary description for atomic and subatomic phenomena. This cumulative nature of theoretical physics ensures that scientific knowledge expands without rendering previous accurate observations invalid.
